# Two Factories: Mechanical Production, Ecological Production, and the Reframing of Cosmo-Localism

**Working concept note — September 2026**
**In response to Michel Bauwens, "How Should Bioregionalism Adapt to Chinese Planetary Industrialisation?"**

---

## The argument in one paragraph

Bauwens frames the central question as dependency versus reproductive capability: can bioregions understand, repair, and adapt the technologies flowing through planetary supply chains, or will they become "the ecological management layer of somebody else's machine civilization"? The frame is half right. It correctly identifies the risk of dependency. But it treats mechanical production as the only production that counts, and asks bioregions to acquire reproductive capability within that single paradigm. The reframe: there are two co-equal forms of production, not one. The mechanical factory (cities, automation, AI) and the ecological factory (watersheds, soils, biodiversity, climate regulation). A functioning civilization requires both in full measure. Each has its own logic, its own knowledge system, its own coordination form, and its own irreplaceable operators. The city cannot run the ecological factory. The bioregion cannot run the mechanical factory. Neither is dependent on the other in the way Bauwens frames it. Both are interdependent, and the interdependence is structural.

---

## What Bauwens gets right

The essay is clear-eyed about several things worth acknowledging before departing from it.

First, the concept of "China Outside China" (Chor Pharn's phrase) names a real structural condition. Chinese manufacturing relocates globally, but the deeper capabilities that make the factory reproducible remain connected to China. The question "who can build the next factory?" is the right question about mechanical production.

Second, the three reproductions (ecological, social, technical) are correctly identified as distinct domains. Bauwens does not collapse them.

Third, the observation that bioregions risk becoming a management layer for someone else's machine civilization is precisely the failure mode the BioConomy framework also identifies. If the ecological factory is understood as subordinate to the mechanical factory, the people who operate it become service providers to a system they do not govern.

Where the argument needs extending is in the assumption that the mechanical factory is the primary productive system and the ecological factory is a secondary concern to be "managed." That assumption is inherited from M-form coordination, where price is the signal and anything that cannot be priced is externalized. The ecological factory produces goods without which the mechanical factory cannot operate: water, breathable air, stable climate, fertile soil, pollination, disease regulation, genetic diversity. These are not amenities. They are inputs to industrial civilization. The relationship is not factory plus environment. It is factory plus factory.

## Rethinking "factory"

The word "factory" does useful work here because it forces a productive framing onto what is usually treated as conservation.

**The mechanical factory** is an engineered system that converts inputs into outputs through controlled processes. Its logic is throughput, efficiency, and optimization. Its exemplars are Bauwens' Chinese industrial system, AI-driven automation, additive manufacturing, and robotics. Its operators need technical knowledge that can, in principle, be globalized: a CNC machine in Shenzhen operates on the same principles as one in Stuttgart. The knowledge that makes the mechanical factory reproducible is codifiable, transmissible, and increasingly embedded in the machines themselves. This is the domain where Bauwens' "reproductive capability" argument applies directly.

**The ecological factory** is a living system that produces ecological goods through processes that cannot be fully engineered. A mountain is a water factory: precipitation, infiltration, aquifer recharge, and baseflow. A river is a nutrient transport system. A grassland is a carbon sequestration plant. A wetland is a water purification facility. A fynbos hillslope is a biodiversity production line whose outputs include genetic material, pollination services, fire regulation, and soil stabilization. None of these can be designed from first principles. None of them can be operated remotely. None of them can be automated. They can only be tended by persons who hold the local knowledge: which slopes retain water after which rains, which fire regimes maintain which plant communities, which grazing patterns build soil and which destroy it, which riparian interventions restore baseflow, and which divert it.

The mechanical factory's knowledge is Silicon Intelligence: codifiable, transmissible, and scalable. The ecological factory's knowledge is Carbon Intelligence: experiential, place-bound, and accumulated across generations. Both are forms of intelligence. Neither substitutes for the other, and both are required for high-functioning societies, particularly as we approach the slowdown phase of the S-curve.

This is where cosmo-localism takes on a meaning slightly different to what Bauwens intended. In his formulation, cosmo-localism means "design global, manufacture local." The knowledge circulates; the production stays put. That describes the mechanical factory's ideal configuration. For the ecological factory, the formula inverts. The ecological factory's knowledge cannot circulate in the same way, because it is constituted by the relationship between a specific community and a specific landscape. What circulates is not the knowledge itself but the methodology for generating it: the protocols, the monitoring frameworks, and the coordination architectures. What stays local is not just the production but the intelligence that makes production possible.

## Steiner's Threefolding and the two factories

Rudolf Steiner's social threefolding provides the constitutional architecture for holding the two factories in a productive relationship without allowing either to colonize the other.

Steiner's structural claim: the French Revolution named the destination (freedom, equality, fraternity) but could not build the vehicle, because it tried to apply all three principles simultaneously to a unified system. Each principle belongs to a different sphere of social life, and each sphere must operate according to its own logic while cooperating with the others.

**Cultural life (freedom):** This is where the ecological factory's knowledge lives. Indigenous ecological knowledge, traditional land management, the farmer's reading of soil, the elder's memory of rainfall patterns, and the community's accumulated understanding of its watershed. Cultural freedom means this knowledge is not subordinated to either state mandate or market price. It is cultivated, transmitted, and applied by the people who hold it, according to their own judgment. This is also the sphere where scientific research, ecological monitoring methodology, and the global knowledge commons operate. The Carbon-Silicon Partnership is a relationship within cultural life: Silicon Intelligence (AI, remote sensing, computational modeling) serves the knowledge-holders, not the other way around.

**Rights life (equality):** This is where the boundary between the two factories is governed. Who decides where mechanical production ends and ecological production begins? What rights does a watershed community hold against an upstream industrial operation? What obligations does a city hold toward the bioregion that supplies its water? These are questions of law, governance, and democratic participation. They cannot be settled by either market logic or cultural tradition alone. Steiner's point: equality before the law is the operating principle here, and it must be institutional, not left to the stronger party's goodwill.

**Economic life (fraternity/association):** This is where the two factories exchange. The mechanical factory's outputs (tools, infrastructure, technology, and medicine) flow to the bioregion. The ecological factory's outputs (water yield, carbon sequestration, biodiversity data, food systems, and heritage economies) flow to the city. The exchange must be governed by association (Steiner's term for cooperative economic governance among producers) rather than by unilateral price-setting. The BioConomy's tender compacts and commitment pools are instruments of associative economics: producers pooling their forward commitments and tendering collectively, rather than each selling separately into a market that cannot price what they produce.

The threefolding prevents three specific failure modes:

First, it prevents the mechanical factory from colonizing the ecological factory through market logic. If watershed function is priced like a commodity, the people who produce it become price-takers in someone else's market. Steiner's separation of economic life from rights life means the terms of exchange are governed by associative agreement, not by the mechanical factory's pricing power.

Second, it prevents the state from colonizing either factory. State-directed ecological management (conservation as command-and-control) and state-directed industrial policy (the Chinese model) both produce the same structural error: subordination of the sphere's own logic to political authority. The ecological factory needs to be operated by its knowledge-holders, not by a ministry or a municipal function.

Third, it prevents cultural life from being subordinated to economic imperatives. When indigenous ecological knowledge is valued only insofar as it generates bankable outcomes, the knowledge system is hollowed out. Cultural freedom means the knowledge tradition has its own standing, independent of whether the market can price what it knows.

## The quadriform: where the two factories sit in TIME

The TIME framework adds a coordination layer that Steiner's threefolding does not address directly: the question of which coordination form each factory runs on.

The mechanical factory is primarily an M-form achievement. Markets organized industrial production at the scale the modern world requires. Prices signal what to produce, capital allocates toward returns, wages compensate labor, and contracts settle transactions. The Chinese planetary industrial system is the M-form's most developed expression, with I-form (state institutional) coordination providing the governance structure. Bauwens recognizes this. His concern is that bioregions will be subordinated to this M+I apparatus.

The ecological factory cannot run on M-form coordination. This is not a political preference. It is a structural limitation. The Market form's coordination signal is price, and anything a price cannot reliably be attached to at any useful timescale, the Market form externalizes. Watershed function, soil carbon, biodiversity, aquifer recharge, and cultural continuity are all externalized, because the substrate cannot pay for its own restoration.

The ecological factory runs on E-form coordination: commitment pooling, outcome instruments, tender compacts, place-anchored reciprocity at the scale of the watershed. The BioHub is the E-form's coordination node, the way the stock exchange and global supply chains are the M-form's. The ecological factory's operators coordinate through pooled forward commitments verified against substrate outcomes, not through price signals.

But the ecological factory also depends on the earlier TIME forms in ways the mechanical factory does not:

**T-form (Tribal):** The ecological factory's knowledge is held in T-form relationships. Kinship, belonging, identity, the stories that encode a community's relationship to its landscape. A farmer's reading of soil moisture is T-form knowledge: transmitted through apprenticeship, embedded in relationship, and activated by loyalty to place. The mechanical factory can operate without T-form bonds (a technician transferred from Shenzhen to São Paulo can run the same CNC machine). The ecological factory cannot.

**I-form (Institutional):** Both factories require institutional governance, but of different kinds. The mechanical factory needs regulatory frameworks, trade law, intellectual property regimes, and environmental standards. The ecological factory needs water law, land tenure security, commons governance, and the institutional recognition of ecological knowledge as a legitimate basis for land management decisions.

**M-form (Market):** The ecological factory does participate in markets. Farmers sell produce. Tourism operators sell experiences. But the ecological goods the factory produces (water yield, carbon sequestration, biodiversity) are not M-form goods. They are E-form goods coordinated through different instruments. The M-form handles the factory's commodity outputs. The E-form handles the factory's ecological outputs. Both run simultaneously, on the same land, often produced by the same people.

**E-form (Emergent):** The coordination logic proper to the ecological factory. Commitment pooling, BioScore verification, and tender compacts with downstream beneficiaries. This is where the BioHub sits: as the coordination node that organizes the ecological factory's production and tenders it to the cities and institutions that depend on it.

The quadriform analysis reveals something Bauwens' essay does not address. Bauwens asks how bioregions can acquire reproductive capability over the mechanical factory's technologies. The TIME framework asks a different question: does the mechanical factory have any reproductive capability over the ecological factory's production? The answer is no. AI can model a watershed. It cannot restore one. Automation can monitor biodiversity. It cannot produce it. The city depends on the ecological factory's outputs and has no coordination logic for producing them. The dependency Bauwens worries about runs in both directions, and the bioregion's leverage is precisely that its factory is the one that cannot be offshored, automated, or reproduced at a distance.

## What changes about cosmo-localism

Bauwens' cosmo-localism operates within the mechanical factory's paradigm: global knowledge, local manufacturing. Design circulates. Production stays put. The framework assumes one form of production and asks how to distribute it.

The two-factory frame produces a different cosmo-localism. The "cosmo" is not just knowledge circulation. It is the entire mechanical production system: cities, industrial supply chains, AI, automation, and the global apparatus that produces manufactured goods. The "local" is not just local manufacturing. It is the ecological production system: the watershed, the soil, the biodiversity, the community that knows how to tend them, and the coordination infrastructure (the BioHub) that organizes their output.

Cosmo-localism, in this frame, is the structural relationship between two forms of production. The city needs what the bioregion produces. The bioregion needs what the city produces. Neither can do the other's work. The relationship is not dependency. It is a partnership between co-equal productive systems.

This reframing has consequences:

**For Bauwens' "reproductive capability" argument:** The bioregion does not need to acquire reproductive capability over the mechanical factory. It needs to develop, protect, and govern its own productive capability as an ecological factory, and to negotiate the terms of exchange with the mechanical factory from a position of structural equality. The BioHub's leverage is that it operates a factory the city cannot reproduce.

**For the "AI habitats" concept:** Bauwens proposes bioregions as "AI habitats" where AI connects to physical reality through sensor networks and ecological monitoring. In the two-factory frame, AI is a tool of the ecological factory, not its organizing logic. The ecological factory's organizing logic is E-form coordination. AI (Silicon Intelligence) serves the Carbon Intelligence that operates the factory. The Carbon-Silicon Partnership is the operational relationship, and the boundary between the two is a Carbon Intelligence judgment that cannot be automated.

**For the risk Bauwens identifies:** His central risk is that bioregionalism becomes "the ecological management layer of somebody else's machine civilization." The two-factory frame names what prevents this: the ecological factory is not a management layer. It is a productive system of equal standing. The people who operate it are not custodians of someone else's externalities. They are producers whose output the mechanical factory cannot function without. The power relation changes when both sides of the exchange recognize what the ecological factory produces as production, not stewardship.

## The design question

The question is not Bauwens' question (how do bioregions acquire reproductive capability over imported technologies?) but a different one: how do two co-equal productive systems, running on different coordination logics, governed by different knowledge systems, producing different classes of goods, negotiate an exchange that serves both?

Steiner's threefolding provides the constitutional architecture. TIME provides the coordination vocabulary. The BioConomy provides the instruments (tender compacts, commitment pools, and outcome bonds). The BioHub provides the coordination node.

What remains to be designed is the interface: the specific institutional, legal, and economic arrangements through which the mechanical factory's outputs reach the bioregion and the ecological factory's outputs reach the city, on terms negotiated by association rather than imposed by the stronger party's pricing power.

That interface is the next piece of work.
