On 28 February 2026, coordinated US and Israeli strikes on Iran killed its supreme leader, and Iran answered by closing the Strait of Hormuz, cutting off the route that had carried about 20 million barrels of oil a day and a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas; this snapshot records the first two weeks, to 15 March 2026. [IC]

## The strikes and the closure

The strikes of 28 February opened a war between Iran and the United States and Israel, with Iranian retaliation reaching Gulf states that host US forces. [IC] Iran did not need to mine or blockade the strait to close it. Radio warnings from the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, together with the withdrawal of war-risk insurance and of the major shipping lines, were enough to stop most traffic within days. [IC] The UK Navy said the Iranian messages had no binding legal force. [IC]

Before the war the strait carried about 11 percent of global trade, including about 34 percent of seaborne oil exports and 30 percent of LNG exports, by UNCTAD's 2025 count. [IC] The Gulf also ships more than 30 percent of the world's traded urea through it, so fertilizer is exposed alongside fuel. [TBV]

The killing of the supreme leader turned a military exchange into a succession. The choice of his son, described in international reporting as a hardliner, and his statement that the strait must stay closed, set expectations for a long closure. [IC]

## Oil

The International Energy Agency called the war "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market." [IC] Flows of crude and products through the strait fell from about 20 million barrels a day to a trickle, and Gulf producers cut output by at least 10 million barrels a day, as Iraq and Kuwait reduced production with nowhere to send it. [IC] Brent futures came close to $120 a barrel in the first days, traded near $109 on 9 March and eased to about $92 by the time the IEA reported. [IC] Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu offered a partial bypass for some crude. [TBV]

The coordinated release of 400 million barrels on 11 March bought time; it could not replace a route that had carried a fifth of the world's oil. [structural inference] The IEA cut its forecast for oil demand in March and April by more than a million barrels a day, an early sign that high prices were already forcing consumption down. [IC]

## Gas and fertilizer

Qatar supplies about a fifth of the world's LNG, around 77 million tonnes a year, mostly to Asian buyers on long-term contracts. [IC] After drones struck Qatari gas facilities on 2 March, QatarEnergy halted production and declared force majeure, and it also shut aluminium and petrochemical output. [IC] India was hit hardest, having taken about 41 percent of its February LNG imports from Qatar, and it asked industry to cut gas use by 10 to 20 percent. [TBV] By mid-March the Dutch TTF benchmark for European gas had nearly doubled, to over €60 per megawatt-hour. [TBV]

Gas is the main input for nitrogen fertilizer. A shock to Gulf gas and urea in March reaches farms at the next planting season, and food prices after that. [structural inference]

## Shipping and the Cape route

Within 48 hours the major container lines stopped Gulf transits. Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd suspended Hormuz crossings, MSC stopped all cargo bookings to the Middle East and told its ships in the Gulf to seek shelter, and CMA CGM suspended Suez Canal sailings. [IC] Because the war also put the Bab el-Mandeb and the Red Sea at risk, lines diverted Asia to Europe services around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10 to 14 days to the voyage, and added war-risk and emergency conflict surcharges to cargo bound for the region. [IC]

## South Africa

South Africa imports most of its refined fuel, and the shock reached it through the oil price and the currency. [IC] The rand weakened sharply against the dollar after the strikes, and by 9 March the Central Energy Fund's data pointed to record fuel price increases in April; Stanlib estimated increases of R2 to R5 a litre. [IC] Transport Minister Barbara Creecy said on 5 March that supplies were stable and that the government was "closely watching the situation, the oil price and supplies, including jet fuel." [IC] The country also sits on the diversion route: ships sent around the Cape pass its ports, with consequences for port congestion and bunkering that were still unclear by mid-March. [TBV]

## What it means for bioregional practice

These are readings for practitioners, offered as inference from the record above.

A single narrow passage turned out to hold up the fuel, fertilizer and freight costs of economies thousands of kilometers away. Every place that imports its energy and its farm inputs carries that exposure, whether or not it appears in local accounts. [structural inference] A [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] can make the exposure visible: how much of a valley's food, transport and water pumping depends on imported diesel and fertilizer, and how quickly a price shock reaches households there.

The case for [[concepts/retention-logic|retention logic]] gets stronger when distant supply fails. Soil fertility built with local organic matter, water held in the ground, and food and energy produced near where they are used all cut a place's dependence on routes it cannot control. [structural inference] [[concepts/water-retention-landscapes|Water retention landscapes]] belong in this argument: a place that keeps its water on the land needs less pumped water and the fuel that pumps it.

The shock is also a test of the [[glossary/m-s/s-curve|S-curve]] reading. An economy that has passed its acceleration phase has less spare capacity to absorb a supply shock, and recovery depends on whether the shock passes. [structural inference] [[concepts/deceleration-indicators|Deceleration Indicators]] tracks the longer evidence; this folder tracks the shocks.

## What to watch (as of 15 March 2026)

- Whether the new supreme leader holds to keeping the strait closed, or opens talks.
- Whether the IEA release brings prices down, and for how long.
- Qatar's estimate of when liquefaction can restart.
- South Africa's April fuel price adjustment.
- Fertilizer prices ahead of the southern hemisphere's winter planting and the northern spring.
- Congestion and delays at ports on the Cape route.
