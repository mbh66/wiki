Convergences gathers tellings of one story that turns up in many places, each credited to the people who told it.

## Overview

A group of people decide to leave the place they called home. In so doing, they become detached from the place-based knowledge that served them well. In moving away, the group gains power they never had before. They can now make and move things in new ways, but the group loses its footing. Much later, near the limit of their new power, the group starts looking for a way back.

Scripture tells this story, and so do cosmology, anthropology and a nineteenth-century equation. No one owns this story, and the fact that it appears so often is the takeaway.

This wiki interprets the convergence as a sign that the pattern lives in shared human thought, in the layer [[vernadsky-vladimir|Vladimir Vernadsky]] called the [[glossary/m-s/noosphere|noosphere]] [MS]. Whether that's the right explanation is still to be determined. Each version of the story is listed below in alphabetical order by the teller, with their sources.

## The tellings

### Hugh Brody, *The Other Side of Eden* (2000)

Brody spent years with the Inuit and other hunting peoples before he wrote this book. He interprets Genesis as a farmers' story, and writes that "agriculture evokes the curses of Genesis" (p. 90). The loss of Eden, and Cain's quarrel with Abel, describe farming peoples spreading over the land of hunters. In his words, "the agricultural system is a form of settlement that depends upon, and gives rise to, the most pervasive form of nomadism" (p. 87). A farm stays put, but a farming people doesn't. Families grow until the land fills, and the next generation goes off to clear new ground. Hunters, who know one territory in depth, are the ones who stay (p. 90).

### Jared Diamond, "The Worst Mistake in the History of the Human Race" (1987)

A short essay in *Discover*. Diamond, then a physiologist at UCLA's medical school, looked at skeletons from early farming sites and found people shorter and sicker than the hunter-gatherers before them. Agriculture fed more people. For most of them it also meant worse health and a harder social order.

### Genesis 2 to 4

This is a story of the Garden of Eden, the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, the serpent "more subtil than any beast of the field" (Genesis 3:1, King James Version), and the gate shut behind as Adam and Eve were banished from the gaden. Then Cain, a tiller of the ground, builds a city, and his descendant Tubal-cain works bronze and iron.

### The serpent as the S-curve (2026)

I interpret these chapters of Genesis along the [[glossary/m-s/s-curve|logistic growth curve]]. The Garden of Eden is the long flat stretch at the start, when people lived on what a place provided. Eating the fruit is the take-off. The serpent is the curve itself, because from inside the steep part you can't see where it levels off, and it tells you you won't die. I've set out the stages in [[concepts/major-evolutionary-transitions|Major Evolutionary Transitions]], and a longer essay is coming.

### The Kogui (Kággaba), Elder Brother and Younger Brother

The Kogui live in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta in Colombia, which they call the Heart of the World. They describe themselves as the Elder Brother. The Younger Brother is the rest of us.

In Alan Ereira's BBC film *From the Heart of the World* (1990), a Kogi speaker tells how the creator sent the Younger Brother across the sea so that he "will not harm the Heart of the World". He went with knowledge of machines. The transcripts don't agree on whether that last part comes from the Kogi speaker or from Ereira's narration.

Ereira was the one who compared it to Eden. There's no choice in the Kogui story. The Younger Brother is made as one who doesn't listen, and he's sent away.

In 2026, Mamo Vangelio Moscote Alberto and Saka Rosa Mamatacán Sauna of the Ezuama Mayor Kuamaka wrote: "Not everything that can be accelerated should be accelerated." Jose Manuel Mamatacán translated their words into Spanish, and Christian Horstmann into English.

### Iain McGilchrist, *The Master and His Emissary* (2009)

McGilchrist is a psychiatrist. His case is that the brain's left hemisphere, which abstracts and grasps, has come to rule over the right, which takes in the whole of what's in front of it. The title comes from a story in Nietzsche about an emissary who takes his master's place.

### Daniel Quinn, *Ishmael* (1992)

A novel, in which a gorilla teaches a man. Quinn calls the culture born in the agricultural revolution about 10,000 years ago the Takers, who act as though the world belongs to them. Everyone else he calls Leavers, who live as though they belong to the world. The gorilla's claim is that Leavers told the story of the Fall first, to explain what the Takers were doing, and that Cain killing Abel is farmers overrunning herders.

### Pierre-François Verhulst, the logistic equation (1838)

Verhulst wrote it to model a population growing within limits. Plotted, it makes an S.
