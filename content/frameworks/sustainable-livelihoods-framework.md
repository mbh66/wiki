The Sustainable Livelihoods Framework sets out the main factors that affect how people make a living and how those factors relate to each other. The UK Department for International Development (DFID) published it in its *Sustainable Livelihoods Guidance Sheets* in April 1999, building on earlier work at the Institute of Development Studies and elsewhere.

## Overview

The framework is centered on people. Its authors stress that it isn't a model of reality and does not work in a straight line from cause to effect. Its purpose is to give people with different perspectives a shared structure for debating the factors that affect livelihoods and how those factors interact. From that debate come entry points for support.

The guidance sheets call the framework a living tool, to be adapted to each place. They also warn that elites may try to disguise how benefits are distributed and skew them toward themselves, so the analysis needs rigour about who gains what.

For the [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] corpus, the framework is useful because it starts from what people already have. That matches the wiki's move from [[glossary/m-s/needed-vs-needy|needy to needed]]: a place described by its assets can make commitments, where a place described only by its deficits can only ask.

## The five parts

### 1. Vulnerability context

The external conditions people live in and have little or no control over:

- **Trends**: population, resources, national and international economics, governance, and technology.
- **Shocks**: to human health, natural shocks, economic shocks, conflict, and shocks to crop and livestock health.
- **Seasonality**: of prices, production, health, and employment.

The sheets observe that the vulnerability context is the part of the framework furthest from people's control. Most outside change arrives through policy. The other way to manage it is to help people become more resilient and better able to use its positive side.

### 2. Livelihood assets: the five capitals

The asset pentagon sits at the core of the framework.

| Capital       | What it covers                                                                      |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Human**     | Skills, knowledge, ability to work, health                                          |
| **Social**    | Networks, membership of groups, relationships of trust and reciprocity              |
| **Natural**   | Land, water, biodiversity, environmental services                                   |
| **Physical**  | Infrastructure (shelter, transport, water supply, energy, communications) and tools |
| **Financial** | Savings, credit, regular inflows such as wages, pensions and remittances            |

Access to assets, whether by ownership or by right of use, is the framework's first concern. The sheets add that a single asset can produce several benefits, ask how far one kind of capital can stand in for another, and note that the pentagon's shape differs between communities and social groups and keeps shifting over time.

### 3. Transforming structures and processes

The organizations, institutions, policies, and laws that shape livelihoods, from the household to the international level. The sheets say they effectively determine three things:

- access to assets, to livelihood strategies, and to decision-making;
- the terms of exchange between different kinds of capital;
- the returns to any given livelihood strategy.

Structures are the organizations (public, private, and civil society). Processes are the policies, laws, institutions, and culture that govern how they operate. The sheets' worked example on access to land and shelter lists (under processes) local conventions on land allocation and inheritance, existing ownership rights and power relations, and power relations within the household.

The sheets also admit a gap: methods for linked policy and institutional analysis at several levels were not well developed. This is where [[frameworks/political-economy-analysis|political economy analysis]] does its work.

### 4. Livelihood strategies

The range and combination of activities and choices people make to reach their livelihood goals. The approach looks for diversity: households often combine several activities and may straddle places, with members working away seasonally. The sheets warn against assuming what people do (for example, that most poor people are farmers) and ask how positive people's choices really are.

### 5. Livelihood outcomes

What people are trying to achieve. The framework lists five: more income, increased well-being, reduced vulnerability, improved food security, and more sustainable use of the natural resource base. The sheets ask analysts to find out which outcomes people themselves prioritize.

## What it offers a BioHub

- **A settlement-level inventory.** The five capitals give a BioHub cohort a structure for describing each settlement's assets. The [[templates/place-mandate-template|Place Mandate Template]]'s value ledger and the [[templates/metabolic-study-template|Metabolic Study Template]]'s provisioning systems can both draw on it.
- **A check on assumptions.** The livelihood strategies sheet's warning against assuming what people do applies to any BioHub that plans around agriculture or tourism.
- **A measurement base.** The five outcomes, and changes in the five capitals, give a starting set of measures for [[frameworks/impact-measurement-guidelines|impact measurement]].

## Its limits

The framework describes the arena where power operates and says little about how to analyze power itself. Its authors acknowledged this in the transforming structures sheet. Used alone, it can produce a detailed asset map that doesn't explain why assets stay locked up. Paired with political economy analysis, the asset map shows what is at stake and the PEA shows who decides.

The wiki also reads the framework's capitals against the [[glossary/a-e/carrying-budget|carrying budget]]: natural capital in the livelihoods sense is a stock people draw on, while the carrying budget sets the ecological ceiling that drawing has to stay under. A livelihood can look sustainable at household level while the catchment it depends on is overdrawn.
