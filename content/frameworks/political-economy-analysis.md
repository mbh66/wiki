Political economy analysis (PEA) is the practice of finding out what's really going on behind a problem in a place: who holds power over the resources involved, and what each party stands to gain or lose from change. Development agencies built it after watching well-funded technical programs stall, and it is now expected in the design of most development work.

## Overview

The method grew out of a recurring failure. Donors would back a reform with advisers and money, the reform would stall, and the stall would be put down to a lack of "political will." PEA starts with the assumption that there's something else going on. It asks what those with power want and don't want, and what makes change possible. The analysis separates the forces a program has to adapt to from the forces it can try to influence.

The account on this page follows Alan Whaites' *Beginner's Guide to Political Economy Analysis*, written in 2017 for the UK's National School of Government International. The guide insists there is no single orthodox method. Its emphasis falls on questions and prompts, and on the claim that PEA can be done by practitioners themselves without hiring a specialist.

For the [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] corpus, PEA fills a specific gap. The founding suite's templates describe what a [[glossary/a-e/bioregion|BioRegion]] holds and what it could offer. They say less about who decides whether anything moves. PEA is the tool for that question.

## The four elements

The guide groups the issues a PEA examines into four elements. Most PEA tools cover all four and differ only in the weighting they allocate to each Element.

| Element                       | What it covers                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Typical questions                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Structural and contextual** | Background conditions that change over years: the economy, demography, regional factors, natural resources. Hard to influence, so usually adapted to.                                                                                       | What are the big economic issues? How does government spend its money, and why? What are the main divisions in society, by identity, age and gender?                          |
| **Bargaining processes**      | How deals get done or blocked, through formal rules and informal practice together. Includes the "political settlement" (the often unwritten understanding among powerful actors about how power is shared) and collective action problems. | How inclusive is bargaining, and who is left out? What is the currency of influence (loyalty, patronage, money, moral authority)? How big must a bargain be before it sticks? |
| **Stakeholders**              | Those who take part in bargaining, those excluded from it, and the networks that connect them to elites. Organizations have their own internal politics.                                                                                    | Who are the stakeholders, how do they organize, and why are they involved? How do they relate to each other beyond the obvious? How do they see the issue?                    |
| **Incentives and ideas**      | What pushes people toward or away from change: money, status, legacy, security, belief. Incentives and disincentives are usually in tension.                                                                                                | What would encourage change? What would discourage it? How have similar trade-offs been weighed before?                                                                       |

Two points from the guide carry particular weight. 
- First, stakeholders do not act only from financial interest, and their influence may bear no relation to their formal position. 
- Second, gender is often left out of PEA altogether, and with it a large share of how bargains work and whom they affect.

### Collective action problems

The guide draws on the Africa Power and Politics Programme to argue that many development failures are collective action problems: change needs agreement and effort from several parties, and that agreement is hard to reach. A reform signed off by a minister can still stall if the officials, landholders, or communities whose cooperation it needs have no reason to give it. What looks like missing will is often a coordination failure among people who do want the change.

This reading matches the wiki's view that [[concepts/consensus-is-not-the-bottleneck|consensus is not the bottleneck]] in a transition. The binding constraint is usually the structure of the bargain.

## Levels and varieties

PEA can be applied at three levels:

- **Issue-specific**: one policy or program question.
- **Sector**: the barriers and openings in one field, such as water or education.
- **Country and context**: the overall political settlement.

A BioHub works at a fourth level the guide doesn't name: the place. A place-level PEA covers one valley, catchment, or district, with its links up to municipal, provincial, and national government. Most of the guide's questions transfer to that level directly.

The many named tools (Drivers of Change, problem-driven analysis, Everyday Political Analysis and others) lean toward different elements. The guide advises choosing by the questions the analysis has to answer, and keeping the method simple.

## How it's done

**The one-hour conversation.** The guide borrows a light approach from the Effective States and Inclusive Development research center: a set of plain questions, framed around the issue and adapted to each person, explored in about an hour per person. It is a conversation with introductions and courtesy. The interviewer leaves room for answers that don't fit the question, because those often carry the most information about how things work day to day.

**History.** Whatever the tool, the analysis asks what has been tried before and why it failed. Past failures are useful as evidence.

**Reading and triangulation.** Background studies, earlier analyses, and official records are checked against each other and against what people say. Insights are played back to interlocutors to test them.

**Second-hand bias.** The guide warns that analysts tend to rely on the people easiest to talk to, such as other funders and NGOs. A place-level PEA has to reach the parties who are hardest to reach.

**Openness about purpose.** Being coy about why the analysis is being done makes it harder to discuss the findings with the people they concern.

## Thinking and working politically

The guide's last argument is that a PEA done once, at the design stage, tends to have no effect. Large formal studies were often set aside as programs moved toward approval. The Thinking and Working Politically community of practice responded by treating political analysis as a continuous part of the work, recorded as it happens (for example in project diaries) and fed into a cycle of implementation, monitoring, learning and adaptation. The guide sets this beside Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation and Doing Development Differently, which share an emphasis on context, best fit, flexible programming and real-time learning.

For a BioHub this means the PEA is a living record, revisited whenever the cohort meets a new party or a bargain shifts.

## Reading it through TIME

This section is the wiki's reading, offered as a [[frameworks/time-framework|TIME]] interpretation. It is not part of the official guide and it's included here because it has been a helpful tool to understand coordination preferences in rural groups.

PEA's "bargaining processes" map well onto the four TIME forms, because each form has its own currency of influence:

| TIME form         | Where bargaining happens                                     | Currency of influence                                   |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Tribal**        | Kin, elders, congregations, long-standing local protocol     | Standing, trust, obligation, memory                     |
| **Institutional** | Municipalities, departments, churches as landholders, courts | Legal authority, mandates, budgets, procedure           |
| **Market**        | Property, investors, employers, tourism                      | Money, land value, jobs, access to capital              |
| **Emergent**      | Forums, commitment pools, co-operatives, networks            | Shared commitments, data, reputation across the network |

A place-level PEA can use this table to check that it has looked for bargains in all four forms, and to notice where a deal blocked in one form might move through another.

## How it joins the other frameworks

PEA is one of three frameworks the wiki pairs for place-level analysis:

- The [[frameworks/sustainable-livelihoods-framework|Sustainable Livelihoods Framework]] describes what households and settlements have and what they face. Its "transforming structures and processes" box is where PEA does its work; the framework's own authors noted that methods for that box were poorly developed.
- [[frameworks/impact-measurement-guidelines|Impact Measurement Guidelines]] describe how a program shows that it worked.
- The [[templates/metabolic-study-template|Metabolic Study Template]] gives the [[glossary/a-e/carrying-budget|carrying budget]] that sets the ecological and social limits within which any bargain has to hold.

Together they answer four questions in turn: what the place has, why change has stalled, what would move it, and how anyone would know.
