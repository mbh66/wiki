The Cycle of Civilizations names the family of cyclical theories which hold that complex societies pass through recurring phases of formation, growth, maturity and decline, together with Michael Haupt's seven-phase synthesis of those theories, which the wiki uses as a diagnostic lens.

## Overview

The idea that societies rise and fall in a recurring pattern is among the oldest in historical thought. Its scholarly name is social cycle theory, and its quantitative successor is cliodynamics. Theorists disagree on the number of phases, the drivers and the time scale. Proposals run from four phases (Toynbee; Turchin and Nefedov) to seven (Quigley), and no consensus exists [IC].

Michael's working notes call the pattern the Cycle of Empire and combine several of these theories into seven phases, adding observable traits for each so that a reader can judge which phase a society is in. The synthesis is his [MS]. The [[frameworks/cycle-of-globalization|Cycle of Globalization]] applies it to the first civilization that covers the planet.

The evidence supports part of the cyclical view. Studies of hundreds of premodern states show that the risk of a state's ending rises over its first two centuries and then levels off. The same studies find no fixed lifespan and no regular period of repetition. The wiki uses the cycle as a lens for reading a society's condition, and makes no forecast of dates from it.

## Empire, civilization and culture

The three words name different things, and cycle theories mix them.

- An **empire** is a large state, usually ruling many peoples, under a central authority. Its span is political.
- A **civilization** is a complex society with cities, social stratification and systems of writing or record. It can outlast many empires.
- A **culture** is the customs, arts and institutions of a people, and can persist for millennia while political forms change.

The Roman Empire in the West lasted from 27 BCE to 476 CE, 502 years, while the Eastern Roman Empire continued to 1453 and Roman law and language shaped Europe for much longer [IC]. The Inca Empire lasted about 95 years on John Rowe's chronology (1438 to 1533), within Andean cultures thousands of years old [IC].

## The lineage

| Thinker | Work | Phases | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polybius (c. 200 to 118 BCE) | *Histories*, Book VI | Primitive monarchy, then kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy and mob rule, returning to monarchy | Each good form decays as later generations forget why it was founded |
| Ibn Khaldun (1377) | *Muqaddimah* | Rise, peak and decline of a dynasty over about three to four generations | Group solidarity (*asabiyyah*) eroded by luxury |
| Giambattista Vico (1725) | *Scienza Nuova* | Ages of gods, heroes and men, then a return (*ricorso*) | Changes in the forms of thought |
| Oswald Spengler (1918 to 1922) | *The Decline of the West* | Cultures as organisms passing through seasons, about a thousand years each, with "civilization" as the late stage | Exhaustion of a culture's founding vision |
| Arnold Toynbee (1934 to 1961) | *A Study of History* | Genesis, growth, breakdown, disintegration | Challenge and response; a creative minority becomes a dominant minority |
| Pitirim Sorokin (1937 to 1941) | *Social and Cultural Dynamics* | Fluctuation between ideational and sensate cultures | Shifts in a society's idea of reality |
| Carroll Quigley (1961) | *The Evolution of Civilizations* | Mixture, gestation, expansion, age of conflict, universal empire, decay, invasion | An instrument of expansion hardens into an institution |
| John Glubb (1976 to 1978) | *The Fate of Empires* | Ages of Pioneers, Conquests, Commerce, Affluence, Intellect, Decadence | Moral change with wealth; about ten generations |
| Peter Turchin and Sergey Nefedov (2009) | *Secular Cycles* | Expansion, stagflation, general crisis, depression | Population pressure, elite overproduction, state fiscal strain |
| Ray Dalio (2021) | *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order* | A Big Cycle of about 250 years, plus or minus 150, with a six-stage internal order cycle | Debt, internal conflict and shifts in reserve-currency power |

Two influential theories of decline are not cyclical. Joseph Tainter explains collapse as a response to diminishing returns on complexity, and Paul Kennedy explains the decline of great powers as imperial overstretch, commitments outrunning the economic base [IC]. Both supply mechanisms for the late phases of cyclical models.

Glubb's essay needs a note. Glubb was a retired British general, and *The Fate of Empires* is an essay, never peer reviewed. His average of about 250 years rests on eleven empires, mostly Near Eastern and European, and he wrote that his dates were "largely arbitrary" [IC]. William Ophuls's *[[sources/ophuls-immoderate-greatness|Immoderate Greatness]]* (2012) restates Glubb's account of moral decay as one of six failure factors, alongside ecological exhaustion and excessive complexity; it too is self-published. Glubb's phase names are memorable and widely repeated, and the wiki uses them as descriptions, without treating his durations as findings.

## The seven-phase synthesis

Michael's synthesis combines these sources into seven phases [MS]. The traits listed are his diagnostic markers.

| Phase | Name | Traits in Michael's synthesis | Nearest source stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stability | Cohesion after an upheaval; public reasoning and debate settle disputes | Quigley's mixture and gestation |
| 2 | Early growth | Expansion is financed; the division of labor deepens and class structure forms; optimism and a shared purpose | Quigley's expansion; Glubb's Pioneers and Conquests |
| 3 | Prosperity | A long period of growth and relative peace; challenges remain analyzable | Quigley's expansion; Glubb's Commerce |
| 4 | Overshoot | Resource use exceeds what the base can renew; conspicuous consumption; rising debt and inequality | Quigley's age of conflict; Glubb's Affluence |
| 5 | Hubris | Power and confidence concentrate in an elite; diminishing returns on earlier investments; complexity outruns understanding | Quigley's universal empire |
| 6 | Totalitarianism | The state demands compliance; disputes are settled by power; separate problems merge into one predicament | Quigley's decay |
| 7/0 | Collapse of the old, emergence of the new | The new appears from the movements that bore the costs of Phases 5 and 6; collapse and emergence run together | Quigley's invasion |

### Where the synthesis departs from its sources

- **Phase 1** is Michael's addition. Glubb's cycle begins with a sudden outburst of aggressive conquerors, and Quigley ties expansion to a group that has accumulated a surplus to invest. Cohesion is the trait the sources share. The emphasis on equality and ecological balance in this phase is Michael's [MS].
- **Phase 4** takes its name from ecology. "Overshoot" comes from William Catton and from *The Limits to Growth*, and appears in neither Glubb nor Quigley.
- **Phases 5 and 6** depart from Glubb. His Age of Intellect describes universities and endless debate, and his Age of Decadence describes defensiveness, pessimism and frivolity. Neither names elite concentration of power or totalitarian government, which are Michael's emphases and draw closer to Turchin's elite overproduction.
- **Reform.** Quigley held that a civilization can return to expansion if its institutions are reformed. The synthesis runs in one direction, and so leaves out the possibility of renewal within a cycle.
- **Phase 7/0** joins the end of one cycle to the start of the next. Michael's notes stress that collapse and emergence are simultaneous, which is why observers disagree about which they are seeing.

## What the evidence supports

Four studies bear on the cyclical claim [IC]:

- Samuel Arbesman (2011) found that the lifespans of 41 empires follow an exponential distribution with a mean of about 220 years. The distribution is memoryless: an empire's yearly risk of ending does not depend on its age.
- Luke Kemp (2019) gave an average lifespan of 336 years across 83 civilizations spanning 4,000 years.
- Marten Scheffer and colleagues (2023), using data on 324 premodern states, found that the risk of a state's ending rose steeply over about the first two centuries and stabilized thereafter.

- Graeme Cumming and Garry Peterson (2017) reviewed 17 cases of social-ecological collapse and identified 14 mechanisms in five classes. They argue that collapse can only be identified against a stated system identity and a measurable threshold, and that a system's structure shapes the kind of collapse it can undergo.

The duration studies agree that lifespans vary widely and that no fixed period recurs. Scheffer's result gives the first quantitative support for loss of resilience with age, which is the part of the cyclical view the evidence sustains.

## Critiques

Karl Popper argued in *The Poverty of Historicism* (1957) that history follows no laws of development from which the future can be predicted, because the growth of knowledge changes the course of events [IC]. Patricia McAnany and Norman Yoffee's *Questioning Collapse* (2010) and Guy Middleton's *Understanding Collapse* (2017) show that many societies described as collapsing reorganized, with their people and cultures persisting [IC]. Luke Kemp's *[[sources/kemp-goliaths-curse|Goliath's Curse]]* (2025) adds that collapse often left ordinary people better off than the hierarchies they had lived under, which questions the loss implied by the word. Critics of cycle theories also point to selective samples and to explanations by moral decay that carry the prejudices of their authors.

The wiki takes these critiques into its use of the cycle. The phases are read as patterns of condition. Moral language in the sources is reported as the sources' own. Dates are treated as projections.

## How the wiki uses it

The cycle gives practitioners a vocabulary for the condition of the society around them, and a reason to expect old and new institutions to run side by side in a late phase. The [[frameworks/cycle-of-globalization|Cycle of Globalization]] applies the synthesis to the global civilization, where the pattern meets a limit earlier civilizations did not face: there is no outside from which a successor can come. The [[frameworks/s-curve-thesis|S-Curve Thesis]] supplies the growth shape within which the phases fall.

## Provenance

Built 7 November 2026 from Michael Haupt's working note "Cycle of Empire", after a fact-check research run. Editorial decisions:

- Title changed to Cycle of Civilizations; "Cycle of Empire" kept as an alias. "Social Cycle Science" replaced with the established term, social cycle theory.
- The note's statement that historians generally agree on seven phases is replaced with the range of phase counts in the literature. The seven-phase model is presented as Michael's synthesis.
- The note's figures (cycles of 225 to 325 years, median 275, over 8,000 years; 26 collapses; 80 or more civilizations) are omitted because no source could be traced; the Arbesman, Kemp and Scheffer findings take their place.
- Corrections: the Western Roman Empire's span is 502 years; the Inca span is about 95 years on Rowe's chronology; Polybius's sequence is given as he states it, with kingship distinct from primitive monarchy; the cycle theory the note attributes to "Spangler" is Oswald Spengler's morphology of cultures.
- Quotations from William Ophuls's *Immoderate Greatness* (2012) in the note are left out pending page checks; the book is cited for its argument, and the research found that its moral-decay passages summarize Glubb.
- Phase traits are restated in neutral terms. Moral judgments in the note are reported as Michael's.
