The Cycle of Globalization is Michael Haupt's model of the first globalized civilization as a single life cycle in seven phases, running from the European settlement of 1648 to a transition now under way, with the Dutch, British and American hegemonic cycles nested inside it and an ending that cannot take the form the endings of earlier civilizations took.

## Overview

The model applies the [[frameworks/cycle-of-civilizations|Cycle of Civilizations]] to a civilization that covers the planet. Its phase structure follows Carroll Quigley's seven stages of a civilization, and its reading of the late phases draws on Joseph Tainter's account of diminishing returns to complexity and Peter Turchin's work on elite overproduction. The seven-phase scheme, the 1648 start, the phase boundaries and the reading of globalization as one civilization with one life cycle are Michael's synthesis [MS]. Scholars propose between four and seven phases for such cycles, and no consensus exists on the number.

The model takes three patterns from the evidence. Aging states become more vulnerable over their first two centuries and stay vulnerable thereafter, without a fixed lifespan. Complex societies meet diminishing returns on the complexity they build to solve problems. Within the modern world-system, leadership has passed from one hegemon to the next, each succession renewing the center while the system kept expanding.

From these it draws one inference. Earlier civilizations ended when a successor arrived from outside them. A global civilization has no outside, and in the [[glossary/a-e/deceleration-phase|deceleration phase]] it has no fresh frontier for a new center to expand into. Its seventh phase therefore becomes a fork, and the wiki reads [[concepts/glocalization|glocalization]] as the branch in which the cycle ends in maturation.

## What the model rests on

### Patterns the evidence supports

**No fixed lifespan.** Samuel Arbesman fitted an exponential distribution to the durations of 41 empires across more than three millennia and found a mean imperial lifetime of about 220 years; the distribution is memoryless, meaning the yearly risk of an empire's end does not depend on its age [IC]. Luke Kemp's 2019 survey of 83 civilizations across 4,000 years gives an average lifespan of 336 years [IC]. Marten Scheffer and colleagues, studying 324 premodern states, found that "the risk of state termination increased steeply over approximately the first two centuries after formation and stabilized thereafter" [IC]. Read together, these support a weaker claim than the cycle literature usually makes: age raises vulnerability, and nothing in the record sets a date.

**Diminishing returns to complexity.** Tainter's *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988) explains collapse as the point at which the costs of maintaining complexity exceed its benefits, so that simplification becomes the economical response [IC]. Tainter also wrote that in today's world of interconnected states, "Collapse, if and when it comes again, will this time be global" [IC].

**Hegemonic succession inside one system.** Giovanni Arrighi describes a sequence of systemic cycles of accumulation led in turn by Genoese, Dutch, British and American centers, each passing from a phase of material expansion to a phase of financial expansion, and from a "signal crisis" to a "terminal crisis" [IC]. Arrighi dates the signal crisis of US hegemony to the Vietnam War years, and the turn is commonly placed in the early 1970s [IC]. George Modelski's long cycles of world leadership and Immanuel Wallerstein's hegemonic cycles describe similar successions. Ray Dalio's Big Cycle of reserve-currency empires is a practitioner's version of the same pattern.

**Structure shapes the kind of collapse.** Graeme Cumming and Garry Peterson reviewed 17 cases of social-ecological collapse and identified 14 mechanisms in five classes. They argue that collapse can only be identified against a stated system identity and a measurable threshold, and that a system's structure shapes the kind of collapse it can undergo [IC].

**Elite overproduction in the late phases.** Turchin's structural-demographic theory identifies a rising number of aspirants to elite positions, against a fixed number of positions, as a driver of instability in the late phases of a secular cycle [IC]. The wiki tests the mechanism on one country in [[research/elite-overproduction-south-africa|Elite Overproduction in South Africa]].

### What is Michael's synthesis

The seven phases and their labels, the choice of 1648 as the start, every phase boundary, the treatment of the global system as one civilization, the inference set out below and all dates after 2026 are Michael's [MS]. The model is held as a working hypothesis.

## The seven phases

| Phase | Dates | Quigley's stage | Anchoring events | Leading center |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Foundation | 1648 to 1776 | Mixture | Peace of Westphalia (1648); English Navigation Act (1651); Glorious Revolution (1688 to 1689); Acts of Union (1707); Battle of Plassey (1757) | Dutch Republic, then Britain |
| 2. Gestation and early growth | 1776 to 1815 | Gestation | US Declaration of Independence and *The Wealth of Nations* (1776); British industrialization (c. 1760 to 1830); Waterloo and the Congress of Vienna (1815) | Britain |
| 3. Expansion | 1815 to 1914 | Expansion | Pax Britannica; gold standard in law (1816) with convertibility resumed (1821); repeal of the Corn Laws (1846); world commodity markets converging from the 1820s; sterling as reserve currency (1870 to 1914) | Britain |
| 4. Conflict and warnings of overshoot | 1914 to 1971 | Age of conflict | Two world wars and the Depression; Bretton Woods (1944); the dollar overtaking sterling gradually from the 1920s to the 1950s; decolonization | Britain to the United States |
| 5. Universal empire and hubris | 1971 to 2008 | Universal empire | End of dollar convertibility to gold (1971); *The Limits to Growth* (1972); oil shock (1973); capital liberalization; WTO (1995); the US-led unipolar period after 1991 | United States |
| 6. Decay and crisis | 2008 onward | Decay | Global financial crisis (2008 to 2009); the plateau in trade integration; the [[concepts/deceleration-indicators|deceleration indicators]]; the closure of the Strait of Hormuz (2026) | United States, contested |
| 7/0. Transition | 2020s onward, overlapping Phase 6 | Invasion | Projection (see below) | None settled |

The event dates are matters of record [IC]. Their grouping into phases is the model's [MS].

### Phase 1: Foundation (1648 to 1776)

The settlement of 1648 is taken as the political anchor for the system of territorial states through which globalization later ran. The Dutch Republic led commerce and finance for much of this phase, and Britain built the naval and fiscal state that would succeed it. Michael's notes describe the phase as a period of stability and cohesion after upheaval [MS].

### Phase 2: Gestation and early growth (1776 to 1815)

Industrialization began in Britain, the United States was founded on ideas largely taken from British and French thought, and the wars with revolutionary and Napoleonic France ended in 1815 with Britain dominant at sea. In Michael's notes this is the phase in which expansion is financed and the division of labor deepens [MS].

### Phase 3: Expansion (1815 to 1914)

Under British naval and financial leadership, trade, migration and capital flows reached a scale with no precedent. Economic historians date the integration of world commodity markets from the 1820s and the first great wave of globalization to 1870 to 1914 [IC]. Growth was large in aggregate and very unevenly shared, within industrializing countries and between them and the colonized world.

### Phase 4: Conflict and warnings of overshoot (1914 to 1971)

Two world wars and a depression broke the first globalization apart. The United States rebuilt the system after 1944 through the Bretton Woods institutions, the United Nations and its alliances, and the dollar became the system's anchor. The phase closes with the first scientific warnings that growth was approaching planetary limits.

### Phase 5: Universal empire and hubris (1971 to 2008)

The end of dollar convertibility in 1971 and the oil shock of 1973 mark the turn Arrighi calls the signal crisis of US hegemony. The system then entered its phase of financial expansion, with capital controls lifted, trade rules extended worldwide and, after 1991, a single superpower. Michael's label "hubris" names the confidence at the center that the arrangement was permanent [MS].

### Phase 6: Decay and crisis (2008 onward)

The financial crisis of 2008 to 2009 ended the rise in trade integration (see [[concepts/globalization|Globalization]]), and the indicators of deceleration have strengthened since. Michael's notes expect concentration of power and authoritarian government in this phase [MS]. The wiki's [[disruption/index|disruption snapshots]] record the phase as it unfolds.

### Phase 7/0: Transition

The last phase of one cycle and the first of the next overlap. Michael's notes describe the collapse of the old and the emergence of the new as simultaneous, so that observers disagree about which is happening [MS]. This matches the finding of the collapse literature that many collapses were reorganizations in which populations and cultures persisted [IC].

## Hegemonies inside the cycle

Hegemonic-succession theorists describe a series of centers, each replacing the last. The model nests those successions inside one longer cycle. The hegemonic cycles are the inner rhythm; the outer cycle is the world-system they led in turn. Each succession renewed the center while the system kept growing, because each new center commanded a larger base: new territory, new markets and a new energy source (wind and peat for the Dutch Republic, coal for Britain, oil for the United States) [TBV].

This nesting is the model's answer to the main objection the succession literature raises against a single civilizational cycle. On the succession view, East Asia or another center could inherit the system from the United States and the pattern would continue. The model accepts that a new center can inherit the system. Its inference concerns what a new center could not inherit.

## The inference

1. Earlier civilizations ended in replacement from outside them, or by a successor growing at their edge. Quigley's seventh stage, invasion, describes this.
2. A global civilization has no outside. Tainter drew the same conclusion: collapse, if it comes, will be global. Luke Kemp's *[[sources/kemp-goliaths-curse|Goliath's Curse]]* (2025) describes the present world as a single interconnected "Goliath" [IC].
3. Within the global civilization, the center has been renewed by succession several times, and each renewal came with an expanded base of energy, territory and ecological sinks.
4. In the deceleration phase the base is planetary and close to its limits (see [[concepts/deceleration-indicators|Deceleration Indicators]]). A new center can inherit the institutions of the system. It cannot inherit a fresh frontier.
5. The seventh phase therefore cannot repeat the pattern of earlier cycles. It becomes a fork with three branches:
   - **Succession on the same substrate.** A new center takes over and runs the phases again on a shrinking base, faster and with less room each time. This corresponds to fixation in Clare Graves's terms and to Technofeudalism in [[frameworks/three-futures|The Three Futures]].
   - **Simplification of the whole.** The system sheds complexity everywhere at once, the global collapse Tainter described. This corresponds to Termination.
   - **Change of substrate.** The system reorganizes around places that steward their own base, keeping the planetary layer of knowledge and infrastructure that globalization built. This is [[concepts/glocalization|glocalization]], and it corresponds to Transcendence and to the [[glossary/m-s/momentous-leap|Momentous Leap]].

   Whether the third branch counts as collapse or as transformation depends on what is taken to be the system's identity, which is the test Cumming and Peterson set for any claim of collapse. If the identity is the global market order, the branch is a collapse. If it is the human species living as one connected system, the branch is a reorganization in which that system survives.
6. In a global civilization, the role Quigley gave to outside invaders falls to the inside. The equivalent of the outside is the places and people the system drew value from. Michael's notes expect the new to emerge from grassroots movements that bore the costs of the late phases [MS]. Kemp finds that past collapses often left ordinary people better off than the hierarchies they had lived under [IC]. The wiki reads bioregional economies and [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHubs]] as one form of that emergence.

The inference sets no date. At 378 years from 1648, the system is well past the two centuries over which Scheffer and colleagues found the risk of ending rises, and so sits on the plateau of elevated risk. That describes a standing condition of the system, with no date attached.

> [!warning] Projections
> Michael's working notes place the height of the crisis between 2026 and 2032, a decade of disruption in the 2030s comparable to the 1930s, and a transition running to about 2060 [MS]. Ray Dalio wrote on 2 September 2025 that "the part of the Big Cycle that we are in is most analogous to the 1928 to 1938 period" [IC]. Both are forecasts. Karl Popper's argument that history follows no laws from which the future can be predicted applies to them directly.

## Caveats

The 1648 start is contestable. Andreas Osiander and Benno Teschke (*[[sources/teschke-myth-of-1648|The Myth of 1648]]*) argue that the Peace of Westphalia did not create a system of sovereign states, and the founding story told in international relations is largely a later myth [IC]. Economic historians date globalization from 1571 (the founding of Manila), from the 1820s (commodity-price convergence) or from 1870 [IC]. The model uses 1648 as the political anchor for the state system and accepts that economic globalization gathers pace later, in Phase 3.

The number of phases and their boundaries are choices. A different reading of the same events could draw the lines elsewhere, and the phase labels for Phases 5 and 6 depart from both Quigley and Glubb (see [[frameworks/cycle-of-civilizations|Cycle of Civilizations]]).

The central step of the inference, that a new hegemon cannot restore growth on a planetary base, is contestable. Succession theorists and many economists expect technology and a new energy source to renew expansion, as each earlier succession did.

Earlier versions of Michael's notes stated that historians agree on seven phases and that each cycle lasts 225 to 325 years across 26 collapses. No source for these figures could be traced, and the quantitative studies above contradict a fixed cycle length. They have been left out.

## For a BioHub

The model offers a reading of the period a BioHub is founded in. Institutions built for the expansion phases can be expected to weaken during Phase 6, while the new arrangements of Phase 7/0 take shape alongside them. A BioHub works in that overlap, building the place-based institutions of the next phase while the old ones still operate.

## Provenance

Built 7 November 2026 from two of Michael Haupt's working notes, "Cycle of Globalization" (subtitled "The 7 Phases of the Neoliberalism Global Civilization") and "Cycle of Empire", after a fact-check research run on both. Editorial decisions:

- Phase 3 now opens in 1815 (Congress of Vienna) where the note had 1823 (Monroe Doctrine), following the research finding that 1815 matches the economic-history evidence. The note gave Phase 3 two end dates (1929 and 1949); this page uses 1914, which removes the overlap with Phase 4.
- Phase 5 opens in 1971, the end of dollar convertibility, where the note had 1972. Phase 6 opens in 2008, the start of the financial crisis.
- The overlap of Phase 6 with Phase 7/0 is kept, as the note intends.
- Phase 6 is labeled "Decay and crisis". The note's label adds "Totalitarianism"; that expectation is reported as Michael's.
- Characterizations in the note that the research flagged as contested political judgments (on NATO, the war in Ukraine and climate policy) are left out. Phase 3's description as "abundance and wellbeing for all" is replaced with the evidence on uneven distribution.
- The note's sentence on seven agreed phases and the 225 to 325 year, 26-collapse figures are omitted as unsourced.
- The note's detailed event timeline of British and American history is reduced to anchoring events. Corrected dates follow the research report: the gold standard in law in 1816 with convertibility resumed in 1821; the dollar's ascendancy as gradual.
- The nesting of hegemonic cycles and the inference section are new on this page, drawn from Michael's framing that the cycle of civilizations applies, this time, to the first globalized civilization. The energy-base claim for each hegemon is tagged [TBV].
- Page numbers for the Tainter quotations follow the research report and should be checked against the printed book.
