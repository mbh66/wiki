Each of the four [[glossary/t-z/time|TIME]] forms has a production node where its way of making things takes physical shape, each pushed identifiable costs outside itself, and those costs have been taken up by whichever form chose to organize them, most often the state putting a price on them; the [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] inherits the chance to organize them, and costs of its own.

## Overview

The wiki describes the four TIME forms from two sides. From the side of coordination, each form produces a [[glossary/a-e/coordination-node|coordination node]] where collective action becomes visible: the sacred site, the cathedral, the skyscraper and trading floor, the BioHub. That sequence is set out on [[frameworks/evolution-of-coordination-nodes|The Evolution of Coordination Nodes]] and [[frameworks/time-physical-manifestations|The Physical Manifestations of TIME]]. From the side of production, each form has a node where goods and services are made: the Farm, the Guildhall, the Factory, the BioHub. This page holds the second sequence and the record of costs that goes with it.

Until 8 November 2026 the production sequence was written as Farm, Stock Market, Data Center, BioHub, and it appeared in three places (the TIME page, the Coordination Nodes page and a research memo) with the claim that "each era's externalities became the next era's inheritance" and that the BioHub has "no substrate below it to receive the costs." A research run tested both claims. The brief [[research/evolution-of-production|The Evolution of Production]] found the four production forms real, datable and still operating, found that every form externalized costs, and found no evidence that each later form arose to organize its predecessor's costs. This page carries the amended sequence.

The nodes as first listed were placeholders, set down before it was clear how each would materialize. The amended sequence adds the missing Institutional node, moves the Stock Market to the Market form as its capital node, records the Data Center as infrastructure the Market form owns and every form now uses, and keeps space in the Emergent row for place-based uses of AI.

The table follows Michael Haupt's production sequence as amended by the research run. TIME is [[ronfeldt-david|David Ronfeldt]]'s framework, renamed; the production sequence and its nodes are Michael's construct.

## The Farm (Tribal)

A community settling in a particular place, holding kin, land, water and labor, and collectively producing surplus for the first time. Kin production is older than farming; the Farm, about 12,000 years ago, is its agrarian version. Optimization target: caloric yield per unit of land. Costs pushed outside: soil depletion, downstream water quality and the commons the farm was carved from, together with the household labor that kept it running. The ILO counts 16.4 billion hours of unpaid care work a day worldwide, 76.2 percent of it done by women, worth 9 percent of global GDP at minimum wage (*Care Work and Care Jobs*, 2018) [IC]. The wiki's term for such work is [[glossary/t-z/unrecognized-contribution|unrecognized contribution]].

## The Guildhall (Institutional)

Production organized by privilege and command: the temple and palace estates of the first states, whose earliest records (Uruk, about 3300 BCE) are accounts of grain [IC]; the craft guilds of Europe from about 1000 to 1880; the chartered companies that traded and governed territory from 1600. Optimization target: the privilege of the producing body, held through charter, monopoly and rule. Costs pushed outside: monopoly prices paid by consumers and the exclusion of women and outsiders from trades, in Sheilagh Ogilvie's account [IC for her account; her reading of guilds is contested], and, for the chartered companies, colonized labor and land [TBV]. The guild and the chartered company operated inside growing markets, and the brief treats them as hybrids of the Institutional and Market forms.

## The Factory and the Stock Market (Market)

The factory gathered power, machines and labor under one roof from 1771; the managerial corporation coordinated throughput across a continent from the 1850s; the global value chain spread production across borders from the late twentieth century. The Stock Market, about 250 years old, is the Market form's capital node: ownership decoupled from operation, with the shareholder holding a claim on an enterprise the shareholder will never enter. Optimization target: return on capital. Costs pushed outside: labor conditions, biosphere depletion and the coherence of the communities from which labor is drawn. Britain's Factory Act 1833 opens by naming the cost: children's "Hours of Labour are longer than is desirable, due Regard being had to their Health and Means of Education" [IC]. The Leblanc alkali works vented hydrogen chloride that killed vegetation around factory towns, and when the law forced condensation the acid went into rivers [IC].

### The Data Center

The Data Center, about 50 years old, coordinates attention, behavior, information and computation, with prediction accuracy as its optimization target. It is owned and run by Market-form firms and used by every form, the Emergent form included. Its costs are energy and water: the IEA estimates data center electricity use at about 415 terawatt hours in 2024, 1.5 percent of world consumption, projected to reach about 945 TWh by 2030 (*Energy and AI*, 2025) [IC], drawn at rates that strain local grids and hydrological budgets. The research run found it fits the sequence as Market infrastructure, and the wiki records it here.

## The BioHub (Emergent)

A place-anchored coordination point that organizes activity around a defined watershed or [[glossary/a-e/bioregion|bioregion]]. Three features distinguish a BioHub from a farm, an ecovillage or a conservation project: a physical site that anchors regeneration beyond its own boundaries, an outward orientation toward the bioregion, and a role as connector across multiple domains of activity. Its production forms are [[glossary/m-s/peer-production|peer production]] and [[glossary/a-e/cosmo-local-production|cosmo-local production]]. Optimization target: the continued viability of the substrate it depends on.

Costs pushed outside. The BioHub has costs of its own to name. Peer production runs on unpaid labor: in Tidelift's 2024 survey of more than 400 open source maintainers, 60 percent were unpaid and 60 percent had quit or considered quitting [MS, Tidelift sells maintainer funding]. The Emergent form computes, stores and communicates on infrastructure it does not govern, and so draws energy and water through the Data Center. Distributed fabrication uses materials and energy that have not been measured at scale [TBV].

### Place-based AI

The Emergent row keeps space for uses of AI that serve a specific place and are governed, at least in part, by the people of that place. [[research/dark-fiber-and-the-ai-overbuild|Dark Fiber and the AI Overbuild]] records the present cases and keeps that label apart from "prosocial," which covers public goods anywhere. Te Hiku Media's te reo Māori speech models are held under its Kaitiakitanga License, which states that "data is not owned but is cared for under the principle of kaitiakitanga and any benefit derived from data flows to the source of the data" [MS]. Lelapa AI's InkubaLM covers five African languages at 0.4 billion parameters and runs on modest hardware [IC]. These are bounded systems: a particular problem, a particular body of data, governed close to its source. They draw less on the Data Center than open-ended systems do, and a BioHub can hold its own data under the principles of [[glossary/a-e/data-sovereignty|data sovereignty]].

The same brief finds that if the AI build-out is repriced, what others could acquire cheaply lies mostly in grid connections, sites, buildings and open-weight models, and that the rules deciding who acquires them are, as of 7 November 2026, proposals [IC that they are proposed]. Local permitting is the fork nearest a bioregion: the data center measures of November 2026 were decisions about how much power and water a facility may draw from a place [structural inference]. Whether BioHubs inherit any of that capacity is an open question, and this row is where the wiki will record the answer.

## Who organizes the costs

Each form's costs have been organized by whoever chose to take them up, and most often that has been the Institutional form putting a price on them. Britain's Factory Act of 1833 put inspectors in the mills; the Alkali Act of 1863 made factories condense the acid they had vented into the air; the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism began charging for carbon embedded in imports on 1 January 2026 [IC]. As costs the Market form left unpriced come back into prices, the work of retaining water, soil and biological complexity gains value, and so do the communities able to organize it [structural inference]. The BioHub is a node through which a place can take up that work and be paid for it. It also pushes costs outward (unpaid contribution, and the energy and water of infrastructure it does not govern), and its claim to value holds only while it names and organizes those costs too.

Rural communities hold a natural monopoly on what the Market and the State now need: functioning watersheds, biological complexity, soil carbon capacity, and the coordination infrastructure to maintain them. The BioHub is the node through which that monopoly becomes coordinated.

## What the sequence does not claim

The sequence is a set of layers that accumulate, each dated by the period in which it governed most production. First appearances overlap: temple estates traded through markets, guilds worked beside merchant putting-out for centuries, and household production never stopped. The research run rejected the claim that each later form arose to organize the prior form's costs: the factory's costs were taken up by the state, and the founders of free software cited proprietary restrictions and cheap computation. A bioregion can add peer production without passing through factory dominance, as Alexander Gerschenkron showed late industrializers did with Britain's stages. The sequence describes where costs have gone and who has organized them. It predicts no destination.
