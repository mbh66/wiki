The processes inside a protective space that help a young initiative develop: shaping shared expectations, building networks and learning.

Schot and Geels (2008) drew the three processes from ten years of strategic niche management studies. Expectations give people a reason to commit before results are in. Networks bring in the users, makers, funders and officials the initiative will need. Learning comes in two orders: first-order learning improves how the thing works, and second-order learning changes what people think it's for. Smith and Raven (2012) named these processes nurturing, the second property of [[glossary/m-s/protective-space|protective space]].

For community initiatives the evidence points to networks and intermediaries. Seyfang and Haxeltine (2012) found the Transition Towns movement weak at passing learning between projects. Hargreaves and others (2013) found that intermediary bodies helped UK community energy groups hold together, while the knowledge groups most needed stayed local. Feola and Nunes (2014) add a caution: less successful Transition initiatives tended to underestimate material resources, so nurturing doesn't replace money.

Monitoring methods built for learning, such as reflexive monitoring in action, track nurturing directly without scoring the initiative.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **[[glossary/m-s/shielding|Shielding]]** holds pressure back. Nurturing builds capacity while the pressure is held back.
- **Capacity building**, in development practice, often means training delivered from outside. Nurturing includes the expectations and networks a group builds for itself.
