The move of production to a country near the market it serves, such as from China to Mexico for the United States.

## Extended definition

Nearshoring shortens supply chains without bringing production home. Its clearest recent case is Mexico, where foreign direct investment rose 16 percent in 2025 to US\$44 billion, much of it reinvested earnings of exporters already there (UNCTAD). Globally the record is thinner: McKinsey Global Institute found that the average distance traded goods travel kept rising through 2025, and that nearshoring is not yet happening on a global scale.

In Weber's terms nearshoring is what happens when the critical [[glossary/f-l/isodapane|isodapane]] around a market shrinks and a neighboring low-wage country still lies inside it. Ownership usually stays with the same firms.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **[[glossary/m-s/reshoring|Reshoring]]**: return to the firm's home country.
- **[[glossary/f-l/friendshoring|Friendshoring]]**: the criterion is political alignment, whatever the distance.
