The idea that an individual's development repeats, in short form, the history of its species or of human culture.

Ernst Haeckel stated the biological version in 1866: the embryo passes through the adult forms of its ancestors, or in his phrase, ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny [IC]. Biology has rejected the strong form; embryos resemble the embryos of related species, which is a different claim [IC]. In the late nineteenth century the idea was carried into psychology and anthropology, where children, and peoples cast as earlier on a single scale, were said to repeat stages of human history. That use helped rank peoples, and is one reason later stage theories are read with suspicion [IC].

Some modern frameworks propose a parallel between individual and cultural sequences without claiming strict repetition; Ken Wilber and Don Beck and Christopher Cowan are examples [IC]. Steven Mithen uses child development only as an analogy and warns against recapitulation [TBV]. The [[research/evolution-of-consciousness/grid|grid]] tests the parallel as step B2, where the measured evidence is weak. The network's rule is that its sequence places no living people or person at a stage [MS].

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **A developmental parallel** claims that two sequences show the same order. Recapitulation claims that one repeats the other.
- **Co-presence,** the claim that earlier structures stay active in everyone (step C1), makes no claim that a person passes through them in historical order.
- **Neoteny,** the keeping of juvenile traits into adulthood, is a separate idea in evolutionary biology that Stephen Jay Gould set against recapitulation.
