The rule that a producer substitutes one input for another at the margin until their relative costs balance; applied to location, it treats transport as one input traded against labor, land and capital.

## Extended definition

The principle comes from general economics. Andreas Predöhl first applied it to location in 1925, and in English in 1928. Walter Isard (1956) combined it with Weber's framework, so that choosing a site became a choice of how much transport to substitute for other inputs, and Leon Moses (1958) showed that location and the input mix must be chosen together once input proportions can vary. Weber's deviations then become special cases of substitution.

The wiki uses the principle to read automation. Robots substitute capital for labor, which shrinks the labor saving that drew plants to low-wage countries and changes which sites are least-cost. It is sometimes described as an offshoot of Weber; the lineage runs the other way.
