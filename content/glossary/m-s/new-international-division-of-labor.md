The relocation of simplified manufacturing from industrial countries to world-market factories in low-wage countries, named by Folker Fröbel, Jürgen Heinrichs and Otto Kreye in 1977.

## Extended definition

Under the classical division of labor, developing countries supplied raw materials and industrial countries made goods. The authors argued that this was giving way to a new arrangement in which developing countries became sites for manufacturing goods sold on the world market, often in export processing zones. They named three preconditions: a worldwide reserve of low-paid labor, the subdivision of production into simple tasks, and transport and communication technology that made dispersed production manageable from a center.

The main critique is that several East Asian economies upgraded into complex industry and built their own firms, which the thesis did not anticipate. Global value chain research has since taken over the field. The wiki's [[research/glocalization-and-the-evolution-of-production|production-location brief]] finds one precondition weakened (China's labor reserve is absorbed) and two intact, so the NIDL persists in regional form, governed from the same centers.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **Global value chains**: the current literature's frame, which adds the governance of chains by lead firms and the smile curve of value along them.
