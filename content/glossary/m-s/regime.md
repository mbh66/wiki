In the multi-level perspective, the settled set of rules, technologies, practices and expectations that governs how a sector meets a need.

A regime is the way things are normally done in electricity, food, transport or housing: the infrastructure already built, the rules that govern it, the skills people have, what users expect and what investors will fund. Geels (2002) describes it as the middle of three levels, above the [[glossary/m-s/niche|niche]] where new things are tried and below the landscape, the wider context of population, ideas and the economy at large. Because its parts reinforce one another, a regime selects against anything that doesn't fit it. That selection pressure is what [[glossary/m-s/shielding|shielding]] holds back.

Critics say the term does too much work. Lawhon and Murphy (2012) argue that transitions research treats the regime as too singular and too detached from place and power. Wieczorek (2018) finds regimes in developing countries often less uniform than in Europe, with large parts of economic life outside them. Geels (2014) replied by adding the power of incumbents, and their resistance to change, to the framework.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **A political regime** is a system of government. The transitions term refers to a sector's working arrangements, which governments shape and don't wholly control.
- **The landscape**, in this technical sense, is the context the regime sits in; neither a regime nor a niche can change it directly.
- **An institution** is one rule or organisation. A regime is the whole set, working together.
