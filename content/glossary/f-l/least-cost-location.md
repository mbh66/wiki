The site at which a plant's total cost of moving materials in and product out is lowest, adjusted for any savings in labor or from clustering that justify moving away from it; the core idea of Alfred Weber's 1909 theory of industrial location.

## Extended definition

Weber treated location as a cost problem in three steps. First find the transport optimum, using the material index (the weight of localized materials divided by the weight of the product) to see whether materials or the market pull harder. Then ask whether cheaper labor elsewhere repays the added transport cost (the [[glossary/f-l/labor-deviation|labor deviation]]). Then ask the same of the savings from clustering with other firms (the agglomeration deviation). Lines of equal added transport cost, [[glossary/f-l/isodapane|isodapanes]], mark how far a plant can move.

The wiki uses the model to read both phases of the [[glossary/m-s/s-curve|S-curve]]. On the steep part, cheap labor abroad repaid long transport. As the labor saving narrows and transport risk and carbon costs return, the same model predicts that heavy goods move toward their markets. It predicts nothing about who owns the plant, which is why least cost location supports regionalization and is silent on [[glossary/f-l/glocalization|glocalization]] in the wiki's sense.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **Profit-maximizing location** (Lösch): the site that maximizes profit over a market area. Weber fixes demand at points and so has no market area.
- **[[glossary/m-s/substitution-principle|Substitution principle]]**: the general rule that later absorbed Weber, treating transport as one input traded against others.
