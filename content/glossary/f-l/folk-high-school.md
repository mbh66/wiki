A residential, exam-free school for adults, founded in Denmark in 1844 on [[people/historical-practitioners/grundtvig-n-f-s|N. F. S. Grundtvig]]'s ideas, that made [[glossary/a-e/bildung|Bildung]] a public practice across the Nordic countries.

## Extended definition

The Danish pastor and writer N. F. S. Grundtvig called for popular education for farmers and the lower classes, built on conversation ("the living word") and free of examinations. The first folk high school opened at Rødding in 1844; its early students were mostly civil servants and wealthier farmers [IC]. Christen Kold widened the base by teaching through the farming off-season, and women were admitted from 1861 [IC]. Norway's first school opened in 1864, Sweden's in 1868 and Finland's in 1889 [IC]. In the United States the model inspired the John C. Campbell Folk School (1925) and Myles Horton's Highlander Folk School (1932), which later trained civil rights organizers [IC].

The schools share a pattern: residential courses of a few months, open entry, no final exams or diplomas, and teaching by dialogue that joins practical subjects to questions of identity, citizenship and meaning. Denmark has around 70 today [IC]. [[people/theorists/andersen-lene-rachel|Lene Rachel Andersen]] and [[people/theorists/bjorkman-tomas|Tomas Björkman]] argue in *The Nordic Secret* (2017) that the movement drove the Nordic countries' transformation into high-trust democracies; that causal claim is the authors' thesis [TBV].

The wiki reads the folk high school as a model for the [[glossary/a-e/bioregional-learning-centre|bioregional learning center]]: an institution where a rural population forms itself for self-government. See [[concepts/bildung|Bildung (concept)]].

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- High school. In English "high school" means secondary schooling for teenagers. A folk high school serves adults and grants no qualifications.
