A country's relative supply of the factors of production (labor, capital, land and, in later work, skills and natural resources), which in the Heckscher-Ohlin model decides what it exports.

## Extended definition

Eli Heckscher (1919) and Bertil Ohlin (1933) argued that countries export goods that use their abundant factor intensively and import goods that use their scarce one. The Stolper-Samuelson theorem (1941) added that trade raises the real return to the abundant factor and lowers the return to the scarce one, which explains why less-skilled workers in capital-rich countries lost ground as trade with labor-abundant countries grew.

A low-wage country specializing in labor-intensive assembly is a factor-endowment case. It is often described as Ricardian comparative advantage, which rests on relative productivity. The wiki keeps the two apart. Its [[research/glocalization-and-the-evolution-of-production|production-location brief]] also asks whether endowments of place (cheap clean energy, water, ecological capacity) will count for more as labor costs converge, and finds the argument made but not yet measured.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **[[glossary/a-e/comparative-advantage|Comparative advantage]]**: Ricardo's theory of specialization by relative productivity, which needs no difference in endowments.
