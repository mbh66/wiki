The match between an institution's features and the features of the resource and problem it governs, judged against a particular outcome.

Young (2008) states the principle that institutions "need to be well-matched to the defining features of the problems they address," and argues that different kinds of environmental problem, such as the depletion of living resources or the overuse of free environmental services, call for different rights, rules and incentives. Cox (2012) argues that there is no single overall fit: a system can fit with respect to one outcome and not another, and each theory of fit links particular variables to a desired outcome. He formalizes three. *Extent fit* holds when a governance system covers the same area as the resource it governs. *Spatiotemporal fit* holds when a resource that varies across space and time is matched by mobile users and flexible, negotiable boundaries. *Spatial nesting fit* holds when layers of governance mirror nested resource units, as in the Taos valley acequias.

**On this wiki.** The [[bioconomy/cases/currencies/index|Currency Case Register]] scores each case's fit against one outcome, keeping a unit's value tied to the resource behind it, and adds *quantity fit*, the wiki's own extension of extent fit: whether the number of units moves with the quantity of the resource. The Omani *athar*, a share of whatever the channel carries, is the clearest case of spatiotemporal fit, and the aflaj go further: those whose flow varies most rent their common right at weekly auctions, while the steadier Falaj al-Khatmeen rents it on annual contracts; the assignats, whose notes multiplied while their land stayed fixed, the clearest failure of quantity fit.
