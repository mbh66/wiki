Financial deployment that targets a measurable social or environmental outcome alongside a financial return, with the investor retaining the option of exit. In the BioConomy corpus, impact investing is the structural counterpart to [[transvestment|transvestment]]: both move capital toward regenerative purposes, but impact investing keeps the capital denominated in the extractive regime and preserves the investor's ability to withdraw it.

## Extended definition

The term entered mainstream finance between 2007 and 2010, coined during a series of convenings organized by the Rockefeller Foundation. It names a class of investment that accepts dual accountability: financial performance measured in conventional return metrics, and social or environmental performance measured through impact frameworks (IRIS+, GIIN benchmarks, UN Sustainable Development Goals alignment, or proprietary ESG scoring). The Global Impact Investing Network (GIIN) estimated the market at approximately USD 1.164 trillion in assets under management in 2022.

The defining structural feature, from the BioConomy's analytical standpoint, is that the investor's capital remains denominated in the prevailing monetary system throughout. The entry point is fiat; the exit point is fiat; the return expectation, whether market-rate or concessionary, is expressed in fiat. The "impact" is a second-order output measured alongside that return, not a replacement for it.

## Why the corpus treats it as a distinct term

The Emancipation Architecture treats the choice between impact investing and transvestment as a structural diagnostic, not a moral one. The question is not whether one is better than the other but what each leaves in place.

Impact investing operates inside the triform coordination substrate ([[time|+T+I+M]]). It uses market-regime instruments (equity, debt, blended finance) to pursue outcomes the market-regime substrate does not natively produce. This is the pattern the (+T+I+M)^+N test identifies: a +N intention (regeneration, social equity, ecological restoration) layered onto a +M instrument, producing an overlay that defaults to triform coordination when stressed. The investor can exit because the capital never left the regime. The impact can be abandoned because the instrument does not depend on the impact for its own integrity.

Transvestment is the structural alternative. Capital crosses from one value regime into another and does not retain exit. The instrument's integrity depends on the regenerative outcome because the instrument is denominated in it. A [[commitment-pool|commitment pool]] backed by verified watershed restoration fails if the restoration fails; the capital holder cannot withdraw to fiat and leave the commitment standing. The one-directionality is not a sacrifice; it is the mechanism that binds capital to outcome.

## The spectrum between them

The distinction is not binary in practice. The [[frameworks/five-transvestment-pathways|Five Transvestment Pathways]] describe a gradient. Pathway C (patient capital through interest-free or below-market loans repaid in bioregional currency) sits closest to impact investing in form: the capital enters as fiat and returns over time. The difference is that it returns in bioregional currency, not fiat, meaning the lender's position migrates into the bioregional regime as the loan is repaid. An impact investor who makes the same loan with fiat repayment retains a fundamentally different structural position.

Pathway A (land and productive assets into a Bioregional Commons Trust) sits furthest from impact investing. There is no return of capital. The transvestor's standing comes from participation in the commons, not from residual ownership.

## Where the BioConomy engages with impact investing

Impact investing enters the BioConomy corpus at several points. The [[concepts/bioregional-economics|bioregional economics]] frame treats it as the nearest-available mechanism for funding ecological restoration within the current regime. The [[alpha-window|Alpha Window]] argument treats it as insufficient: impact capital's exit option means it will leave if the regime shift makes exit attractive, which is precisely when the regenerative substrate most needs capital to stay. The [[frameworks/three-feature-test|Three-Feature Test]] positions it as the Fugger pattern: patronage that funds the reform while keeping its own assets denominated in the order the reform addresses.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

Transvestment: one-directional crossing between value regimes, exit foreclosed by design. Impact investing: deployment within the existing regime, exit preserved. Divestment: withdrawal of capital without redirection. Philanthropy: grant capital, no return expected, but typically no structural crossing either (the foundation's endowment remains in the market regime). [[mycelial-patronage|Mycelial patronage]]: a coordination pattern in which the patron's standing depends on the reform's success; impact investing can be a vehicle for mycelial patronage if the investor's position genuinely depends on the outcome, though structurally it rarely does.
