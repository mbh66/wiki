The Garden of Eden is the garden of Genesis 2 and 3, where the first humans live until they eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and are sent out. Michael Haupt reads it as one telling of the [[glossary/a-e/bifurcation|Bifurcation]], with the serpent as the [[glossary/m-s/s-curve|S-curve]] [MS].

## The thesis's reading

Step T7 of the [[research/evolution-of-consciousness/anabranch/index|Anabranch thesis]] places a split in consciousness at the move from [[glossary/m-s/mythic-consciousness|Mythic]] to [[glossary/m-s/material-consciousness|Material Consciousness]], "as one possible interpretation of the Garden of Eden story tells it" [MS]. In Michael's telling, the garden is the time before the curve, when people lived on what a place provided. Eating the fruit starts the climb, slowly at first, with farming about 12,000 years ago. The serpent is the curve itself: from inside the steep part you can't see where it levels off, and it tells you that you won't die [MS].

The network holds that the Mythic structure tells this story as a garden, a fruit and a serpent, and the Material structure tells it as a growth curve [MS]. It reads the two side by side. The reading is published under Michael's name as one telling among others [MS]. The [[concepts/convergences|Convergences]] page gathers the rest, unranked, among them Hugh Brody's reading of Genesis as a farmers' story.

## Genesis in its scholars' terms

Hebrew Bible scholarship reads the chapters on their own terms, and its readings differ from the traditional one.

- **James Barr**, *The Garden of Eden and the Hope of Immortality* (1992), argues that the traditional reading of the story as the "Fall of Man" can't survive close study of the text. He reads it as a story of how human immortality was almost gained and then lost [IC].
- **Phyllis Trible**, *God and the Rhetoric of Sexuality* (1978), reads Genesis 2 and 3 as a love story gone awry. She translates *ha-adam* as the earth creature, a human not yet marked as male or female [IC].
- **Carol Meyers**, *Discovering Eve* (1988), reads the chapters against the farming life of ancient Israel's highland villages. She renders the first line of Genesis 3:16 as more toil and many pregnancies, two marks of most Israelite women's lives [IC].

None of these scholars reads the garden as a split in consciousness or as a growth curve (inference). Terence Fretheim notes that the word "fall" doesn't appear in Genesis 3 [IC].
