Richard Baldwin's term for the successive separations that globalization made possible: of production from consumption, of the stages of production from each other, and of workers from their workplaces.

## Extended definition

In Baldwin's account the first unbundling came with steam power and cheap transport in the nineteenth century, when goods could be made far from where they were used. The second came with information technology from about 1990, when a firm could run a factory across borders from its headquarters, and production split into global value chains. The third, which Baldwin calls globotics, is under way: telepresence and remote work let service tasks be done across borders while automation reshapes the factory.

The wiki uses Baldwin to separate light flows from heavy ones. Services reached 27.5 percent of world trade in 2025, the highest share since 2005, which fits the third unbundling, while goods have regrouped into blocs without coming home in volume. Coordination cost, which the second unbundling collapsed, is the term Weber's [[glossary/f-l/least-cost-location|least cost location]] model lacks.
