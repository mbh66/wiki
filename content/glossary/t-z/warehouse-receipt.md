A document issued by a licensed or certified store that records the quantity and grade of a commodity deposited there, and that can be transferred or pledged as collateral where the law makes it negotiable.

Warehouse receipt systems let a depositor store grain or another commodity and borrow against it or sell it without moving it. Ethiopia, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia passed dedicated laws between 2003 and 2010, and South Africa's electronic silo certificates are the largest system on the continent. Each receipt names a stock in a named warehouse, which gives it the clearest fit to its resource of any unit in the wiki's currency register. The record on who benefits is less favorable: in Malawi large traders took out most receipts, and in Ghana loans against receipts went mostly to corporate bodies and large traders.

**Contrast.** A warehouse receipt is a claim on a specific stored stock and is retired when the stock is withdrawn. It circulates among traders and banks as collateral and settlement, and none of the African systems has served as household money. Colonial Virginia's tobacco notes were warehouse receipts that the law made legal tender for debts payable in tobacco.
