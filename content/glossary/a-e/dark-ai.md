Powered, permitted data centre sites and grid connections built for frontier AI that sit unused or underused after a repricing and become available to new users below build cost.

Michael Haupt coined the term by analogy with dark fibre: the optical fibre laid in the late 1990s that stayed unlit after the telecom crash of 2001, and was later leased or sold to new users at a fraction of its cost. The analogy holds only for the slow layer of an AI data centre. David Rosenthal estimates that about 40 per cent of the cost is buildings, power connections and cooling, and about 60 per cent is accelerators, which lose most of their value within a few years ("Dark GPUs", 15 October 2026). He notes too that an idle building carries costs that a buried cable didn't. The definition therefore leaves out accelerators, and it leaves out open-weight models, whose price is zero whatever the market does.

The term names a category that was empty when it was defined. On 13 November 2026 H100 rental prices were rising on three independent indexes and no AI data centre had been sold at a distressed price; AI debt was trading at a discount. The [[research/dark-ai-and-the-place-based-inheritance|Dark AI brief]] judges the term more misleading than useful for that reason, and offers "stranded AI sites" as a plainer alternative.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **Dark fibre** is long-lived and cheap to keep. Dark AI, if it appears, is a building with a grid connection whose owner pays to hold it.
- **A stranded asset** is any asset written down before the end of its expected life. Dark AI is the subset that someone else can take on and use.
- **Cheap inference** comes mostly from model efficiency and competition. It doesn't need idle hardware, so it isn't evidence of dark AI.
