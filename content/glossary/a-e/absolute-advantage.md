A country's ability to produce a good at lower absolute cost than another country; Adam Smith's 1776 basis for trade.

## Extended definition

Smith argued that a country should import whatever another can make more cheaply than it can make for itself, and pay with goods it makes more cheaply in turn. Ricardo's [[glossary/a-e/comparative-advantage|comparative advantage]] went further, showing that trade pays even for a country with no absolute advantage in anything.

The distinction matters for the wiki because of capital mobility. Ricardo assumed capital stayed at home. Herman Daly and John Cobb argued in 1989 that once capital moves freely it follows absolute advantage, settling wherever costs are lowest, and that the comparative-advantage case for free trade then loses its ground. Trade economists reply that comparative advantage still operates over labor and land, which move less. The wiki's [[research/glocalization-and-the-evolution-of-production|production-location brief]] reports both positions.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **[[glossary/a-e/comparative-advantage|Comparative advantage]]**: specialization by relative opportunity cost, which holds where one country is better at everything.
