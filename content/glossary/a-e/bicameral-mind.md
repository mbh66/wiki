Julian Jaynes's proposed earlier mentality, in which commands from one side of the brain were heard as the voices of gods and obeyed, without introspective self-awareness.

Jaynes set out the idea in *The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind* (1976). By consciousness he meant something narrow: an inner space in which a person narrates their own life and reflects on it. He argued that this is learned through language and culture. Before it, people acted on voices that seemed to come from outside them. He read the *Iliad*, where heroes act at the gods' command, as a record of that older mentality, and dated its breakdown to about 3,000 years ago, under the strain of large, literate and mixed societies.

Ned Block's 1981 review argued that Jaynes confuses consciousness itself with the concept of consciousness. The Julian Jaynes Society has published replies to Block and other critics [IC]. Most specialists in psychology and in the ancient world reject the dating [TBV].

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **Gebser's structures of consciousness** describe ways of experiencing space, time and self. They make no claim about a division of labour between the two sides of the brain, or about hearing voices.
- **Iain McGilchrist's account of the hemispheres** holds that each side of the brain attends to the world in a different way, with both at work in every person. It proposes no earlier mentality of command and obedience.
- **A bicameral legislature,** with two chambers, gave Jaynes his metaphor and nothing else.
