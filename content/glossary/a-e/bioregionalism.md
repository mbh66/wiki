Bioregionalism is a movement and body of thought holding that people should organise their lives, economies and governance around bioregions: places defined by watersheds, landforms, living communities and the cultures that have grown up in them [IC].

## Extended definition

Peter Berg and Raymond Dasmann gave the movement its founding statement in "Reinhabiting California" (1977) [IC]. Planet Drum, the foundation Berg co-founded, describes the essay as the first definition of the bioregion to include human beings [TBV]. In it, a bioregion is both a geographical terrain and a terrain of consciousness [IC]. Its second idea is reinhabitation: learning to live in a place again, in step with its natural systems.

Kirkpatrick Sale's *Dwellers in the Land: The Bioregional Vision* (1985) carried the idea into economics, government and settlement. Its main thesis is that people should produce and consume in harmony with the natural features of their bioregions [IC].

On this wiki, the [[glossary/a-e/bioregion|BioRegion]] is the corpus's own unit of economic coordination, with its own definition [MS]. Bioregionalism is the wider tradition that unit comes from. In the [[research/evolution-of-consciousness/anabranch/index|Anabranch thesis]], bioregionalism is how the [[glossary/a-e/confluence|Confluence]] looks on the ground [MS].

## Contrast and critique

- **Bioregion as a science term.** Ecologists and conservation planners also use "bioregion" for mapped ecological units. Those maps describe ecology and carry no programme for how people should live.
- **Critics.** Donald Alexander argues that bioregions are shaped by culture and choice as much as by nature [IC]. Mark Purcell and J. Christopher Brown warn against the "local trap": no scale, the local included, is more just or sustainable in itself [IC].
- **Joining knowledges.** Bioregional writing often draws on [[glossary/f-l/indigenous-knowledge|Indigenous knowledge]]. Paul Nadasdy argues that efforts to fold traditional knowledge into management shift power away from the people who hold it [IC]. Arun Agrawal argues that no fixed line divides indigenous and scientific knowledge [IC]. Indigenous knowledge is long-standing in its own right, on its own terms, and doesn't need a bioregional frame to count.
