Threads of a mycorrhizal fungus that connect the roots of two or more plants below ground, sometimes across species.

Most land plants live in partnership with mycorrhizal fungi. The fungus takes sugars from the plant and gives water and nutrients in return. Its threads, called hyphae, can colonise the roots of several plants at once, and so form a network between them. Suzanne Simard and colleagues reported in 1997 that carbon moved between trees of different species in a Canadian forest. Popular accounts since then have called these links the "wood wide web", and described mother trees feeding their seedlings through them.

Justine Karst, Melanie Jones and Jason Hoeksema (2023) reviewed the field studies. They accept that the networks exist. They found that three popular claims rest on weak or contested evidence: that the networks are widespread in forests, that resources moving through them help seedlings grow, and that mature trees send resources and warnings to their offspring through them. They also found that unsupported claims about the networks have doubled in the past 25 years [IC].

The wiki uses "mycelial" as a metaphor for a form of coordination, in [[glossary/m-s/mycelial-consciousness|Mycelial Consciousness]] and [[glossary/m-s/mycelial-coordination|Mycelial Coordination]]. The metaphor doesn't depend on how the forest debate ends, and pages shouldn't cite the biology as evidence for it.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **A mycelium** is the whole body of one fungus, a mass of hyphae in soil or wood. A common mycorrhizal network is the part of it that links plant roots.
- **A mycorrhiza** is the partnership between one fungus and one plant's roots. A network needs at least two plants.
- **The wood wide web** is the popular name, and often carries the stronger claims Karst and colleagues question.
