David Ricardo's 1817 theory that nations maximize aggregate output by specializing in what they produce relatively most efficiently and trading for the rest. The proposition is mathematically sound as a statement about relative efficiency and became the theoretical foundation for two centuries of trade liberalization.

The theory's failure mode, from a retention-economics perspective, is that it tends to lock in existing patterns of specialization. A country with a comparative advantage in raw materials is advised to remain a raw-materials exporter, foreclosing the move into the increasing-returns manufacturing activities that Erik Reinert argues are the actual mechanism of development. Comparative advantage is therefore structurally a doctrine that concentrates productive capacity in already-dominant economies. Its practical political effect during the acceleration phase of the industrial S-curve was to align peripheral factions (cotton-exporting American South, colonial raw-material extractors) with the dominant foreign power against domestic-industry advocates. This is the alignment mechanism the S-Curve Thesis takes as central.

## Contrast with adjacent terms

- **Absolute advantage** (Adam Smith, 1776): producing a good at lower absolute cost than another country. Comparative advantage holds even where one country has the absolute advantage in every good.
- **Factor endowments** (the Heckscher-Ohlin model, 1919 and 1933): a country exports goods that use its abundant factor intensively. A low-wage country specializing in labor-intensive assembly is a factor-endowment case, and is often misdescribed as Ricardian.
- **Trade in tasks** (Grossman and Rossi-Hansberg, 2008): firms offshoring stages of production as coordination costs fall. That is a decision of the firm; comparative advantage is a theory of trade between countries.

Ricardo assumed capital would stay at home. Where capital moves freely, Daly and Cobb argue it follows absolute advantage, which removes the ground of his argument; the [[research/glocalization-and-the-evolution-of-production|production-location brief]] sets out the debate.
