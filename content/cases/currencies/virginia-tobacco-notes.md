## What the sources show

Virginia's General Assembly passed its tobacco inspection act in May 1730. Tobacco was inspected and stored in public warehouses, and the owners of hogsheads that passed received notes. The act declared that "nothing shall be accounted a lawful tender, to discharge any debt, contract, or duty, paiable in tobacco" "unless paiment of the same be tendred in inspectors notes" (section XXIV). Tobacco the inspectors judged bad was burned in their presence, and an owner who would not sort a refused hogshead had the whole of it burned (section XXIX). Herndon (1957) records that the notes "circulated freely," were legal tender in the county of issue and adjacent counties, and were retired when a holder presented them at the named warehouse for tobacco. Notes served as currency until Virginia began printing paper money during the French and Indian War (1754 to 1763); the act expired in October 1775 and was reinstated a year later [IC]. Inspection and burning kept the quality and quantity behind each note honest.
