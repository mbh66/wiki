African mountain catchments appear in the register mostly as state land, water-fund project sites or contested territories, and the communities in them seldom hold the money.

Responses and corrections will be recorded on the case pages with their date. [MS] marks a claim that rests on the account of the party the claim is about.

## The cases

**[[abreha-we-atsbeha-watershed|Abreha we Atsbeha, Tigray]] (Ethiopia).** A 6,766-hectare watershed in which 4,644 hectares (69%) was placed in exclosures and 55 check dams were built. About 650 hand-dug wells now irrigate. The community received the UN Equator Prize in 2012. Reported groundwater figures differ by source. [[sources/haile-gebregziabher-2020-abreha-we-atsbeha|Haile and Gebregziabher (2020)]], citing [[sources/gebregziabher-2016-iwmi-watershed-management|Gebregziabher et al. (2016)]], state that groundwater once lay about 50 m below the surface and now lies about 5 m below it. A characterization study gives 15 m to 3 m and a 21.22% runoff reduction, and Negusse et al. (2013) report a roughly tenfold increase in groundwater availability in the Mendae catchment since 1993. The wiki's earlier briefs read Gebregziabher et al. (2016) as a rise of about 5 m. The sources agree that the water table rose; none has a control catchment, and the size of the rise is unresolved [IC on direction, TBV on size]. The work combined community labor with the state's food-for-work and later the Productive Safety Net Programme (PSNP).

**[[upper-tana-nairobi-water-fund|Upper Tana-Nairobi Water Fund]] (Kenya).** A trust created in 2015 with The Nature Conservancy, Nairobi's utility, KenGen, companies and donors. Its 2016 report claims 27 million more liters a day reaching Nairobi, and its 2022 to 2026 strategy cites 44,725 farmers and an 11% reduction in turbidity [MS]. Targets of 15% more dry-season flow and 18% less sediment are model projections. An IFAD supervision mission recommended better farmer representation at the governance level. Farmers receive inputs and training, and the board is held by contributors.

**[[ogiek-and-the-mau-forest|Ogiek and the Mau]] (Kenya).** The Mau Forest Complex (about 273,300 hectares) is a national [[glossary/t-z/water-tower|water tower]]. The African Court found in 2017 that evictions violated Ogiek rights, holding that conservation could not justify them and that the Ogiek were not responsible for degradation ([[sources/african-court-ogiek-2017-merits|African Court 2017]]). In 2022 it ordered reparations of about KES 157.8 million (about US\$1.3 million) and recognition of title ([[sources/african-court-ogiek-2022-reparations|African Court 2022]]) [IC]. In its first compliance judgment, of 4 December 2025 (Application No. 006/2012), the Court found that Kenya had complied only partly with the 2017 judgment and had paid none of the reparations ordered in 2022, and ordered immediate implementation ([[sources/african-court-ogiek-2025-compliance|African Court 2025]]) [IC]. In November 2023 a joint force moved to evict about 700 Ogiek households [IC]. Global Forest Watch data reported by Mongabay show the complex lost 19% of tree cover between 2001 and 2022 [IC]. The wiki treats this pattern under [[glossary/a-e/conservation-violence|conservation violence]] and [[glossary/f-l/fortress-conservation|fortress conservation]].

**[[invasive-clearing-in-table-mountain-group-catchments|Table Mountain Group catchments, Western Cape]] (South Africa).** [[sources/holden-2022-drought-streamflow|Holden et al. (2022)]] estimated that clearing invasive trees in the mountain catchments supplying Cape Town would increase streamflow, especially in drought. [[sources/tanner-2017-palmiet|Tanner et al. (2017)]] describe the hydrological functioning of palmiet wetlands, which in the Cape Fold Belt are fed by sandstone aquifers. State-funded teams and a water fund do the clearing. Communities hold neither the catchments nor the money and take part as paid labor [IC on hydrology from the prior run].

**[[sengwer-and-the-embobut-forest|Sengwer of Embobut (Kenya)]]**. Amnesty International (2018) found that the 2013 decision to resettle all residents of the 22,000-hectare Embobut Forest was taken without genuinely consulting the Sengwer, that up to 1,500 homes were burned in January 2014 and more than 300 after December 2017, and that a Sengwer man was shot dead on 16 January 2018; the EU suspended its Kenya Water Towers programme the next day [IC].

**[[lesotho-highlands-water-project|Lesotho Highlands Water Project]]**. International Rivers (2001) reported more than 30,000 rural farmers affected, no compensation before displacement and declining living standards for most affected people, while royalties and customs dues made up 27.8% of government revenue [IC].

**[[maloti-drakensberg-payment-proposals|Maloti-Drakensberg payment proposals (South Africa)]]**. Blignaut and colleagues (2010) modeled grassland restoration in a range that covers under 5% of the country and yields about 25% of its runoff, and found benefits above costs in low to medium degraded land [IC]; no implemented community scheme was found.

**[[living-lands-in-the-baviaanskloof|Living Lands in the Baviaanskloof]]** and **[[umngeni-ecological-infrastructure-partnership|the uMngeni partnership]]** (South Africa). The Baviaanskloof work runs through a 2014 partnership and a farmer-owned development company, by its partners' account [MS]; the uMngeni partnership, begun with a 2013 memorandum signed by 21 parties and coordinated by SANBI and eThekwini, has no rural community or land-rights holder among its members, and its water gains are modeled (Pringle et al. 2023; Hughes et al. 2018) [IC]. In each of these five cases the community appears as evictee, resettled household, worker, landowner-shareholder or project site, and none holds the catchment's water value.

**ABCD cases.** The region holds the register's only three cases that are [[glossary/a-e/asset-based-community-development|ABCD]] by name or ABCD-framed, and none of them governs a mountain catchment. In the [[coady-abcd-program-in-ethiopia|Coady ABCD program in Ethiopia]], groups grew from five in 2003 to 24 in 2011 and put natural resources on their asset maps; comparing the 2008 and 2011 maps "did not reveal many changes in the natural resource base or physical infrastructure" (Peters 2013) [MS], and no water measurement was made. At [[makutano-water-points|Makutano]] in semi-arid Yatta, Kenya, each of 26 water points is run by an elected committee with its own bank account, and members pay 250 Kenya shillings a year (Mahomed and Peters 2011) [MS]; the site lies outside any mountain catchment. ICRAF's [[asset-based-regreening-in-nyando|asset-based regreening in Nyando]] concerns agroforestry on smallholdings (Fuchs, Peters and Neufeldt 2019), and no water outcome was measured. The Ethiopian and Makutano cases both rest on evaluations compiled by Brianne Peters at the Coady Institute, and a second enrichment run found no independent evaluation of either beyond one 2014 radio report on Makutano.
