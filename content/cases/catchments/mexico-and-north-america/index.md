Mexican comunidades hold their forests and the payments made for them, New Mexico acequias run a centuries-old irrigation commons, and Catskills farmers supply New York City under the city's agreement.

## The cases

**[[mexicos-payments-for-hydrological-services|Hydrological payments]] (PSAH, Mexico).** The program started in 2003 with MXN 200 million (US\$18.2 million) earmarked from federal water fees. Rates were 300 pesos per hectare per year for forest and 400 for cloud forest. In early years, 64% of enrolled land was at low or very low deforestation risk (Muñoz-Piña et al. 2008). Alix-Garcia and colleagues found small to moderate avoided deforestation (3ie Impact Evaluation 20) [IC]. Payments go to the ejido or comunidad assembly, which decides use.

**[[community-forestry-in-the-sierra-norte-of-oaxaca|Sierra Norte, Oaxaca]].** Zapotec and Chinantec communities such as Ixtlán de Juárez and Capulálpam de Méndez run community forest enterprises on communal land. In the Sierra de Juárez, forest cover fell until 2001 and then recovered, so that in 2014 it covered about 84% of the area and slightly exceeded its 1979 extent; the authors attribute the trend to community organization, forest enterprises and payment programs (Navarro Cerrillo et al. 2019) [IC]. [[sources/von-thaden-2025-post-pes-deforestation|Von Thaden et al. (2025)]] found that parcels at least ten years past the end of PSAH payments had significantly higher deforestation than controls, and that nearly 70% of enrolled parcels were in areas of low to medium deforestation risk [IC]. The payment effect faded while the community forestry institutions persisted.

**[[acequias-of-northern-new-mexico|Acequias of northern New Mexico]]**. Field studies found that about 60% of water diverted into a main acequia returns to the river as surface flow, 33% as shallow groundwater and 7% is lost to evapotranspiration, with seepage recharging the aquifer at about 1,169 mm a year and extending the runoff season downstream (Rango et al. 2013) [IC]. In Taos valley the acequias produce less and have mostly lost their common pastures; Cox (2014) attributes much of this to demographic change, markets and public policy, and finds that members rated pressure to sell water rights lower than drought, newcomers and falling participation [IC]. Cox and Ross (2011) link weaker performance to the subdivision of land among new residents [IC].

**[[new-york-city-watershed-agreement-and-the-catskills|The Catskills and New York City]]**. Under the Whole Farm Program from 1992, the city pays cost-share through the farmer-led Watershed Agricultural Council. The Revised 2017 Filtration Avoidance Determination, issued in December 2022, requires a US\$11 million contract with the council, and the city's 2022 report states that no new whole farm plans were approved West of Hudson that year [IC]. The council's board composition and the watershed towns' objections were not read.

**[[san-luis-valley-groundwater-subdistrict-1|San Luis Valley Subdistrict 1]] (Colorado).** Irrigators on about 160,000 irrigated acres have paid a fee on their net groundwater pumping since 2011, rising from US\$45 to US\$150 an acre-foot by 2017, and part of the income pays for fallowing. Farmers in the fee district pumped about 32% less a year than comparable farmers outside it (Smith et al. 2017) [IC]. Most of the aquifer storage gained was lost in the 2018 drought (Allen and Smith 2023), storage in 2023 was 1.21 million acre-feet below 2000 (Smith and Andersson 2025), and the aquifer reached a record low in August 2026; a plan to raise the fee from US\$150 to US\$500 an acre-foot went to water court that year, with groundwater-only producers arguing that it puts the burden on them (Inside Climate News 2026) [IC].

**[[california-drought-water-bank-of-1991|California's 1991 Drought Water Bank]].** The state bought over 820,000 acre-feet, mainly from Delta farmers, and sold almost 390,000 to 12 agencies, about half of the allocated water going to the Metropolitan Water District of Southern California; the state estimated that about 150,000 acres would be taken out of production (Legislative Analyst's Office 1991; Lund et al. 1992) [IC]. It is in the register as a tradeable-right counterpoint.
