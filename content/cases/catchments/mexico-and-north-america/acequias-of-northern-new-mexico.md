## What the sources show

Field studies in an acequia-irrigated valley of northern New Mexico, summarized by Rango and colleagues (2013), found that about 60% of the water diverted from a main acequia returns to the river as surface flow, about 33% returns as shallow groundwater, and only about 7% is lost to evapotranspiration. Seepage and deep percolation recharge the shallow aquifer at about 1,169 mm a year, and the aquifer releases water slowly back to the river, extending the runoff season for downstream users [IC]. In Taos valley, Cox (2014) finds from satellite imagery, surveys and interviews that the acequias produce less than they did and have mostly lost their common livestock pasturing. Part of the change follows falling water availability, but much of it comes from social drivers: demographic change, regional and global markets, and public policy. Members ranked drought as the worst disturbance, followed by newcomers who do not recognize acequia traditions and lack of participation by existing members. "Water markets and pressure to sell water rights" ranked seventh of eleven, and Cox notes that this pressure "was lower than expected." Hispano members were much more concerned than newcomers about the Abeyta adjudication, which since 1968 has quantified and prioritized each member's individual water right, and Cox finds that the boundaries between who can and cannot use the water have become "more porous" [IC]. Cox and Ross (2011) find that acequias whose land had been more subdivided since 1970, as new residents bought and split parcels, performed worse, while water-sharing agreements and access to groundwater helped [IC]. A companion study maps how the acequias' governance nests onto the ditches: at the farm level, land parcels and headgates are privately owned, but strong community pressure, and in some acequias written rules, govern whether they can be sold out of the community; the main canals, their water and the land around them are common property of each acequia; and agreements between acequias (*repartimientos*) govern the shared headgates off the rivers. Cox names the penetration of water markets among the threats the acequias face, with economic development, changing demographics and conflict with the state's water management (Cox 2014, *Human Ecology*) [IC].
