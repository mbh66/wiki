## What the sources show

Subdistrict No. 1 of the Rio Grande Water Conservation District was formed in 2006, when a majority of landowners inside the proposed boundary, counted both by number and by acreage, signed the petition. It is the valley's largest subdistrict, with 160,000 irrigated acres and 70% of the wells. From 2011 its irrigators paid US\$45 for each net acre-foot pumped, with credit for any surface water rights they put toward aquifer recharge; the fee rose to US\$75 in 2012 and US\$90 in 2015, and has stood at US\$150 since 2017. Interviewees told researchers that the fee was chosen because revenue was needed for surface-water payments required by the state, and because those who used more groundwater should pay more (Smith and Andersson 2025; Allen and Smith 2023) [IC].

Smith, Andersson, Cody, Cox and Ficklin (2017) found that farmers in the fee district cut their groundwater pumping by 32% against comparable farmers outside it. A simpler difference-in-differences calculation gives 78.86 acre-feet per farm unit, a 30.4% reduction from pre-fee levels; the nominal reduction was closer to 10%, because pumping outside the subdistrict rose while surface supplies were 24% lower. A well-level analysis for 2011 to 2021 found the effect held at about 30% and, if anything, shrank slightly as the fee doubled. Irrigators also fallowed 12 more acres per farm against a mean of 16. The subdistrict targets 40,000 of its 160,000 acres for retirement, using fee income for local fallowing and federal Conservation Reserve Enhancement Program funds from 2016; fallowing accounts for about 9% of annual pumping reductions since then. The district helped arrange the first groundwater conservation easement in 2022 (Smith and Andersson 2025) [IC].

The aquifer has not recovered. It fell to its lowest point in 2013, regained ground through 2017, and lost most of those gains in the 2018 drought, which was worse than that of 2002, with diminished recharge and increased pumping (Allen and Smith 2023) [IC]. In 2023 the unconfined aquifer held 1.21 million acre-feet less than in 2000; subtracting the estimated savings from the fee and fallowing, the authors estimate it would otherwise have lost 2.38 million. They conclude that the fee "has not delivered on stabilizing, let alone recovering, the aquifer" (Smith and Andersson 2025) [IC]. Inside Climate News (Payne, 4 October 2026) reports a record low in August 2026 [IC].

Farm values within the subdistrict fell after its formation in 2006, recovered 86% of that loss after the fee began, and remain about 6% below their pre-2006 values after general valley trends are accounted for (Smith and Andersson 2025) [IC]. Five more subdistricts formed in the valley from 2016 to 2019; participation in them is voluntary, none had a fallowing program, and their fees are based on gross pumping (Allen and Smith 2023) [IC].

In 2024 the irrigators decided to raise the fee to US\$500 an acre-foot. Smith and Andersson (2025) read this as a shift toward a de facto cap-and-trade system, because farmers with surface water left over could sell pumping allowances to those who want to pump more, and note that groundwater users with no surface rights, about 14% of parcels, would have to buy more allowances [IC]. The plan went before a Colorado water court in the summer of 2026, with a ruling expected later in the year and an appeal to the Colorado Supreme Court likely; the state engineer can curtail groundwater diversions if the subdistrict misses its sustainability goals by 2031. Groundwater-only producers argue that the plan puts the burden on them; one prefers a pumping cap like that of Subdistrict 5, and another argues that reductions should follow water rights and priority (Inside Climate News 2026) [IC].
