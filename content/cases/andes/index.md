Andean comunidades have revived pre-Inca recharge canals and restored high wetlands, and Bolivian municipalities have set up [[glossary/m-s/reciprocal-water-agreement|reciprocal water agreements]] with downstream users; in most of these cases a utility, a water fund or an NGO holds the money.

## The cases

**[[cases/andes/amunas-of-huarochiri|Amunas of Huarochirí]] (Peru).** Pre-Inca canals divert wet-season water onto fractured slopes above 3,800 m. Comunidades campesinas hold the land under communal title and maintain the canals through festival labor. [[sources/ochoa-tocachi-2019-amunas|Ochoa-Tocachi et al. (2019)]] measured a mean subsurface retention of 45 days with tracers in Huamantanga. They modeled that scaling up across Lima's source areas could delay 99 million m³ a year and raise dry-season flows by 7.5% on average, with up to 33% at the start of the dry season [IC]. Press coverage repeated the 33% figure as the headline. The researchers knew of three communities still maintaining amunas.

Aquafondo has supported San Pedro de Casta since 2015 with company and donor money. It reports 1,562 people trained, and a Ministry of Environment release cites 17.7 km of amunas rehabilitated and a "retribution" of 3.9 million m³ [MS]. Aquafondo has proposed that Sedapal contract the communities for year-round maintenance, and no executed contract was found.

**[[cases/andes/merese-and-the-milloc-bofedales|MERESE and Sedapal]] (Peru).** Law 30215 (2014) created retribution mechanisms, and Sedapal has charged a MERESE tariff on Lima water bills since 2015. La República reported the tariff at 1% of the bill and more than S/50 million collected in five years. In March 2020, Emergency Decree 036-2020 authorized Sedapal to use MERESE collections for operations and maintenance [IC].

Sedapal's first project restored the Milloc bofedales in the comunidad campesina of Santiago de Carampoma. Reported costs are S/2.96 million (La Razón) and S/3,174,018 (Forest Trends); the two figures conflict. Sedapal's chair cited 12,000 person-days of local labor, and the community was given guardianship, operation and maintenance [MS]. This is the clearest example in the Andes of a community contracted as a provider of water outcomes ([[glossary/m-s/needed-vs-needy|needed versus needy]]), with terms set by the utility.

**[[cases/andes/qochas-of-san-juan-de-tarucani|Qochas of Arequipa]] (Peru).** Sedapar's MERESE contributions, routed through Profonanpe and executed by the NGO Descosur under Sernanp supervision, funded 25 qochas for more than S/8 million in San Juan de Tarucani [MS]. The communities receive works and do not hold the money. Sunass, the national regulator, recorded the financing and inauguration of two of the microdams in October 2022, which confirms the arrangement [IC]; a 2024 report gives 25 qochas storing more than 600,000 m³ [MS].

**[[cases/andes/los-negros-reciprocal-water-agreement|Reciprocal Water Agreements]] (Bolivia).** In 2003 at Santa Rosa de Lima (Los Negros), Natura facilitated a deal in which five downstream irrigators compensated upstream landholders. Compensation is in kind (beehives, fruit seedlings, irrigation pipe, barbed wire). The trial's data deposit gives it as US\$1 to 10 per hectare per year plus a US\$100 joining incentive [IC]. Natura states that every dollar in the municipal fund goes upstream and that cooperative accounts are open to members [MS]. Natura's executive director, María Teresa Vargas, told Mongabay in January 2023 that 24,000 farmers protect almost 600,000 hectares in around 80 municipalities [MS].

The randomized trial allocated 129 communities, 65 to treatment and 64 to control, at a 2010 baseline. [[sources/pynegar-2018-watershared-water-quality|Pynegar et al. (2018)]] found that excluding cattle reduced E. coli at the site, while at the scale of the communities' water supplies Watershared had no detectable effect. Uptake was low and the land around water intakes was seldom enrolled [IC]. Wiik et al. (2019) found no effect on deforestation over the first five years. Grillos (2017) found that in-kind payments increased environmental values (crowding in). Natura's policy director co-authored the water paper, and the authors disclose this.

**[[cases/andes/fonag-water-fund-quito|FONAG (Ecuador)]]**. Quito's utility pays 1% of monthly water sales into the fund; upstream farmers receive project support and no cash. FONAG's own trust records and Chafla and Cerón (2016) show a board of six institutional constituents (EPMAPS, Empresa Eléctrica Quito, The Nature Conservancy, Tesalia CBC, Cervecería Nacional and Consorcio CAMAREN) with votes proportional to their contributions; páramo communities take part through conservation agreements and as paid guardapáramos, and hold no seat [IC]. An earlier IIED profile that listed community representatives is outweighed by these records. No measured water result was found.

**[[cases/andes/potato-park-pisac|The Potato Park (Peru)]]**. Peru's Ministry of Agriculture recognized the park in March 2020 as an agrobiodiversity zone of 7,238.33 hectares across four comunidades campesinas, Chahuaytire, Amaru, Paru Paru and Pampallacta (RM 0081-2020-MINAGRI) [IC]. More than 1,330 potato varieties are kept in fields and a communal gene bank. Livelihood outcomes rest on the partners' account [MS], and water outcomes are unmeasured.
