English-born political theorist, pamphleteer, and revolutionary, whose _Common Sense_ (January 1776) supplied the collective story that made American independence thinkable and whose subsequent works (_Rights of Man_, 1791; _The Age of Reason_, 1794; _Agrarian Justice_, 1797) extended the argument to republican government, religious liberty, and the redistribution of landed wealth. Paine arrived in Philadelphia in late 1774 with a letter of introduction from Benjamin Franklin. Within fourteen months he had written the most influential political pamphlet in American history.

Paine was not an economist. He belongs on this list because his work demonstrates, more clearly than any contemporary text, how a collective coordination story is built. _Common Sense_ did not discover independence as a policy option. The idea was circulating. What Paine accomplished was to make it feel obvious, inevitable, and morally necessary to a mass audience. The pamphlet sold an estimated 500,000 copies in a colonial population of roughly 2.5 million. George Washington wrote that it was "working a powerful change in the minds of many men." The change was not informational. It was narrative. Paine supplied the story that made a coordination event (revolution, state formation, constitutional self-governance) possible at scale.

## The collective story Paine built

_Common Sense_ appeared in the same year as [[smith-adam|Adam Smith]]'s _Wealth of Nations_. The coincidence is structural. Both texts were published at the base of the industrial [[s-curve|S-curve]], and both supplied foundational stories that the acceleration phase required. Smith supplied the economic story: markets self-regulate, production generates wealth, and the role of government is limited. Paine supplied the political story: sovereignty resides in the people, hereditary monarchy is an absurdity, and republican self-governance is the natural condition of a free society. Together, the two stories formed the narrative substrate of the liberal order that powered the steep part of the curve.

Paine's method is worth studying for the collective-story thesis because he was explicit about what he was doing. He was not arguing before a learned audience. He was constructing a shared reality for farmers, tradesmen, and soldiers. His prose was deliberately plain. His arguments moved from common experience to political conclusion without passing through theory. He treated monarchy not as a complex institution to be reformed but as an obvious absurdity to be discarded. The effect was to make the reader feel that independence was simple common sense, always available, merely obscured by habit and deference. This is how coordination stories work: they make the coordination they enable feel natural, as though the alternative were the aberration.

Paine's fiscal thinking is relevant to the MMT/S-curve thesis in a specific way. In _Common Sense_, he calculated the cost of building a continental navy and argued that the colonies could afford independence because their trade revenues would fund the new government. In _Rights of Man_ (Part II, 1792), he went further: he proposed progressive taxation, family allowances, old-age pensions, public education, and funeral expenses funded from tax revenue, an early blueprint for the welfare state. His _Agrarian Justice_ (1797) proposed a national fund, financed by an inheritance tax on landed property, to pay every person a lump sum at age twenty-one and an annual pension from age fifty. These proposals all operate within the "taxes fund government" frame. Paine assumed, as Smith and every other thinker of his era assumed, that the state's spending capacity derives from its revenue. The innovation was in what the revenue should be spent on, not in where it comes from.

This makes Paine a double witness. He demonstrates how a collective coordination story is constructed (the political story of republican sovereignty), and he demonstrates the depth at which the fiscal coordination story (taxes fund the state) was embedded, even in the most radical political imagination of the period. Paine could imagine overthrowing monarchy, establishing universal rights, and redistributing landed wealth. He could not imagine that the sovereign state creates its own currency. The Chartalist insight (Knapp, 1905) was more than a century away. The fiscal story was so deep in the substrate that the most radical thinker of the age operated entirely within it.

## Paine and the S-curve

Paine's position on the [[s-curve|S-curve]] is the base of the acceleration phase, the same position as [[hamilton-alexander|Hamilton]]. The difference is that Hamilton was building the economic infrastructure for the steep part (tariffs, national bank, domestic manufacturing) while Paine was building the political story that legitimated the institutional form within which that infrastructure would operate. Republican self-governance, popular sovereignty, the social contract funded by taxation: these are the +[[i-form-institutional|I-form]] coordination stories that the +[[m-form-market|M-form]] economy of the acceleration phase required. The market needed a state. The state needed a legitimacy story. Paine wrote the story.

Paine's later career illustrates the limits of the story he built. _The Age of Reason_ (1794) attacked organized religion and cost him most of his popular support. He returned to America in 1802 and died in poverty in 1809, with only six mourners at his funeral. The coordination story he had built for the republic did not require its author. This is consistent with the [[glossary/substrate-hypothesis|Substrate Hypothesis]]: once a portable coordination story is embedded in the institutional substrate (the Constitution, the Bill of Rights, the fiscal architecture), it survives the withdrawal of its founder. Paine's story outlived Paine by the same mechanism that Benedict's Rule outlived Benedict.
