German philosopher, economist, and revolutionary, author of _Das Kapital_ (Volume I, 1867; Volumes II and III published posthumously by Engels in 1885 and 1894) and, with Friedrich Engels, _The Communist Manifesto_ (1848). Marx spent most of his productive life in London, researching in the British Museum's reading room while living in poverty. His work is the most thorough structural critique of capitalist production ever produced, and it shares with the system it critiques the deepest assumption of the acceleration phase: that industrial production is the engine of history.

Marx built on Ricardo's labor theory of value and transformed it into a theory of exploitation. The value of a commodity is determined by the socially necessary labor time required to produce it. The capitalist pays the worker less than the value the worker produces; the difference is surplus value, the source of profit. Accumulation of surplus value drives the expansion of production, the concentration of capital, the displacement of labor by machinery, and the periodic crises of overproduction that punctuate capitalist development. The analysis is structurally precise. It describes the internal dynamics of the throughput engine with a rigor that Smith and Ricardo did not attempt and that [[mill-john-stuart|Mill]] deliberately softened.

## The collective story Marx inverted and preserved

Marx is the critical case for the collective-story thesis, because his work simultaneously exposed the throughput story and reinforced its deepest premise.

The exposure was devastating. Marx demonstrated that the "free market" was a system of structured extraction, that the wage relation was a form of compulsion, that the state served the interests of the owning class, and that the collective stories told about freedom, opportunity, and meritocracy were ideological constructions that legitimated exploitation. His concept of ideology, the systematic production of beliefs that serve the interests of the dominant class while appearing as universal truths, is itself a theory of collective coordination stories. The "taxes fund government" story, the "household budget" metaphor, the "self-regulating market" narrative: Marx would have recognized each of these as ideological constructions that discipline behavior in the interests of capital.

The preservation was structural. Marx accepted the factory as the unit of economic analysis. He accepted that industrialization was historically progressive: the bourgeoisie, he wrote in the _Manifesto_, "has created more massive and more colossal productive forces than have all preceding generations together." He accepted that the productive forces developed under capitalism were the material precondition for socialism. His proposed solution was to change the ownership of the means of production, from private to collective, while keeping the throughput logic intact. The factory would continue to operate. The surplus would be collectively appropriated. The growth imperative would persist, redirected toward collective welfare.

This is the [[glossary/substrate-hypothesis|Substrate Hypothesis]] problem in its earliest recognizable form. Marx proposed to change who controls the substrate while leaving the substrate itself in place. The coordination architecture (industrial production organized around throughput, measured by output, powered by the extraction of natural resources and human labor) would remain. Only the distribution of its products would change. In the vocabulary of this wiki, Marx performed the most sophisticated version of Mill's production/distribution move: he radicalized the distributional critique while leaving the production substrate untouched.

The consequence for the collective story of the acceleration phase was paradoxical. Marxism became the primary opposition to capitalism, and the opposition reinforced the shared premise. Both sides agreed that industrial production was the foundation of civilization. Both measured progress by output. Both treated nature as raw material for human transformation. The Cold War was a contest between two management philosophies for the same throughput engine. The Soviet Union industrialized behind protective barriers, extracted natural resources at scale, and measured success by steel production, wheat harvests, and GDP growth. The substrate was identical. The story about who should own it differed.

This is why Marx belongs in the throughput lineage and not in the retention or [[glossary/regenerative|regenerative]] lineage, despite the power of his critique. His analytical tools (ideology critique, the theory of surplus value, the analysis of primitive accumulation, the commodity fetishism concept) are permanently useful. His structural diagnosis of capitalism's internal contradictions has been largely confirmed by two centuries of data. His solution, collective ownership of the same production substrate, is the canonical illustration of the (+T+I+M)^+N problem: changing the political form layered over the monetary and productive substrate does not change the coordination behavior the substrate produces. A collectively owned factory consuming its watershed is still consuming its watershed.

Marx's fiscal thinking is less developed than Smith's or Ricardo's, because he treated the state as a superstructural expression of class power, not as an independent economic actor. Taxation, in Marx's framework, is one mechanism by which the state extracts resources from the working class to fund the apparatus that protects capitalist property relations. This framing is analytically distinct from the "taxes fund government" story (Marx would call that story ideology), but it arrives at the same behavioral conclusion: citizens experience taxation as extraction, and the state's spending capacity appears to depend on its revenue. The possibility that a sovereign state creates its own currency and that taxation serves a substrate-level coordination function (driving demand for the currency, shaping behavior, managing inflation) was as invisible to Marx as it was to Smith and Mill, because the Chartalist insight had not yet been articulated. Georg Friedrich Knapp's _State Theory of Money_ appeared in 1905, twenty-two years after Marx's death.
