Herbert Butterfield (1931). *The Whig Interpretation of History*. G. Bell and Sons, London.

Butterfield criticises historians who study the past with one eye on the present [TBV]. He objects to arranging history as a series of stages leading to the historian's own day, which shrinks the past to the story of how it produced the present [TBV]. The essay speaks to the writing of history and says nothing about the universe (inference).

Jack Haughton, in *The Historical Journal* (2026), DOI 10.1017/S0018246X25101155, reports that the book "is universally received as sceptical of 'whig' teleology" [TBV]. He argues that Butterfield attacked a fixed idea of progress that served one party, and that Butterfield still defended progress in an indeterminate form [TBV]. The essay was not read for this record, and the account of it here comes from secondary sources [TBV].

The wiki takes from it the classic case against reading a direction into the past, with Haughton's rereading beside it [TBV].
