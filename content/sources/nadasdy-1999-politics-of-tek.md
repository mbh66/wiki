Paul Nadasdy (1999). "The Politics of TEK: Power and the 'Integration' of Knowledge". *Arctic Anthropology* 36(1-2): 1-18. [JSTOR 40316502](https://www.jstor.org/stable/40316502).

Nadasdy, an anthropologist now at Cornell University, draws on fieldwork with hunters and wildlife managers in the Yukon [IC]. He argues that "integration" assumes that traditional knowledge fits Western ideas of what knowledge is [IC]. It treats that knowledge as new data for existing management bureaucracies [IC]. As a result, Aboriginal people must express themselves in the forms of state management, and power gathers in administrative centres [IC].

The paper is widely cited in co-management research and appears on the US National Park Service's page on traditional ecological knowledge [IC]. Nadasdy develops the argument at book length in *Hunters and Bureaucrats* (2003) [TBV].

The wiki takes from it the leading critique of joining knowledge systems through existing institutions. It is coded in its own terms, as a study of power in knowledge-sharing, with no step of the thesis read into the knowledge it describes [TBV].
