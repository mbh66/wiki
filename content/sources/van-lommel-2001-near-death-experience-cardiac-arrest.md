Pim van Lommel, Ruud van Wees, Vincent Meyers and Ingrid Elfferich (2001). "Near-death experience in survivors of cardiac arrest: a prospective study in the Netherlands". *The Lancet* 358(9298): 2039-2045. DOI: [10.1016/S0140-6736(01)07100-8](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(01)07100-8).

The team enrolled 344 consecutive patients resuscitated after cardiac arrest in ten Dutch hospitals [IC]. According to the abstract on PubMed (PMID 11755611), 62 of them (18 per cent) reported a near-death experience, and 41 of these a core experience [IC]. The experience was not associated with the length of the arrest or of unconsciousness, with medication, or with fear of death beforehand [IC]. The authors interviewed the groups again two and eight years later and report that changes in people's lives took several years to show, and differed from those of survivors without the experience [IC].

Van Lommel later argued, in *Consciousness Beyond Life* (HarperOne, 2010), that such experiences may point to consciousness continuing while the brain is not functioning; that is his reading, and the paper itself states no such finding [IC]. Critics hold that near-death experiences are hallucinations of a disturbed brain; Keith Augustine makes that case in *The Myth of an Afterlife* (2015) [IC].

The wiki takes from the study a measured rate of near-death experience in a prospective cardiac cohort, with no finding on survival [TBV].
