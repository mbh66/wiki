By 13 November 2026 the record supports the first link of the chain in form (a correction in AI shares and stress in AI debt), contradicts the third link at the run date (accelerator rental prices are rising and no AI asset has changed hands in distress), and holds no evidence at all that community currencies and AI have been combined.

## Overview

The [[research/dark-fiber-and-the-ai-overbuild|Dark Fiber and the AI Overbuild]] brief (7 November 2026) found that AI capital spending has passed the late-1990s telecom build-out relative to US GDP. Its financing repeats the old pattern of suppliers funding their customers. The part most likely to outlast a repricing is the slow layer: grid connections, permitted sites, buildings, open-weight models and cheap inference. Accelerators lose most of their value within a few years. That brief recorded no distressed sale of AI capacity and no rule giving anyone a right of access to compute.

This brief tests a longer hypothesis, drafted by Michael Haupt on 8 November 2026. It holds that a repricing will make the slow layer available to place-based organisations, and that [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHubs]] using it for sensemaking and local exchange can turn the [[glossary/a-e/deceleration-phase|deceleration phase]] into a period of regeneration. The brief grades each link against history and against the record to 13 November 2026. It doesn't forecast whether the hypothesis will come true, and it gives no view on buying or selling any security.

The short reading for a practitioner: markets have marked AI shares down by about a tenth and price AI debt as risky. Compute itself has got dearer over the past year. The place-based bodies that already run their own AI, with Te Hiku Media in Aotearoa New Zealand the clearest case, bought their hardware or were given compute credits outside any crash. The nearest thing in the record to a place-based inheritance is land. In Britain that land is being bought by natural-capital companies, and community land bodies have contested the purchases.

Two limits apply throughout. The research run reached most of its evidence through web search and trade reporting, and some market figures rest on trade press reading primary data feeds; each carries its grade. The run didn't reach the outcome literature on local economies (Preston, the Evergreen co-operatives, Mondragon, LM3), so the link on measured regeneration is graded mainly on the absence of tested evidence.

## The hypothesis and its chain

The headline, as Michael drafted it:

> If the AI build-out is repriced as earlier infrastructure booms were, the part of it that lasts (grid connections, sites, open-weight models and cheap inference) will become available to place-based organizations, and BioHubs that use it for local sensemaking and local exchange can turn the deceleration phase of the S-curve into a period of regeneration and development.

The headline is a `speculative-projection`. P marks a proposition: one link in the chain, stated so that evidence can bear on it. I marks the inheritance condition, which sits between P3 and P4 and states what must hold for cheap capacity to reach place-based bodies. The chain holds only if every link holds, and each link rests on a different kind of evidence.

- **P1. Repricing.** The AI build-out will be repriced in a financial correction comparable to the telecom crash of 2000 to 2002.
- **P2. Public refusal.** In the same period, the public will increasingly withdraw consent from surveillance-based business models, visible in votes, laws, procurement terms and user exits.
- **P3. Dark AI.** The repricing will open a period in which capacity built for frontier AI sits underused and passes to new users at a fraction of its cost, as dark fibre did after 2001.
- **I. The inheritance condition.** Place-based bodies gain a share of that capacity only where rules or institutions give them one. Without such rules, cheap capacity goes mainly to platforms, consolidators and the state.
- **P4. BioHub uptake.** In that period, BioHubs and similar place-based bodies will use small and open-weight models for place-based sensemaking and to run local exchange, including community currencies and [[glossary/a-e/commitment-pool|commitment pools]].
- **P5. Place-based renaissance.** Coordination at the scale of place will grow while the [[glossary/m-s/s-curve|S-curve]] tapers, and will produce measurable regeneration where it takes hold.

### Terms

**Correction, bear market, crash.** By market convention a fall of 10 per cent from a peak is a correction and a fall of 20 per cent a bear market. The Nasdaq Composite met the first test on 5 November 2026 and hadn't met the second by 12 November. In this brief a repricing is a sustained fall in the market value of an asset class or its debt. A crash is a fall past bear-market depth, with defaults.

**Dark fibre and dark AI.** Dark fibre is installed optical fibre with no transmission equipment attached, which after 2001 was leased or sold to new users. A definition of [[glossary/a-e/dark-ai|dark AI]] that matches what the evidence shows could survive: powered, permitted data centre sites and their grid connections, built for frontier AI, that sit unused or underused after a repricing and become available to new users below build cost. The definition leaves out accelerators, which lose value before they can wait, and open-weight models, whose price is zero whatever happens in the market. On the evidence below the term misleads more than it helps, because the cheap, long-lived surplus it names doesn't yet exist.

**Surveillance capitalism.** Shoshana Zuboff ([[sources/zuboff-surveillance-capitalism|2019]]) defines it as the claiming of human experience as raw material for behavioural data, processed into prediction products. The wiki's [[concepts/surveillance-capitalism|Surveillance Capitalism]] page widens it to a data-fusion complex of platform, state and programmable-money systems [MS].

**Sensemaking.** In organisation studies, Karl Weick (1995) uses [[glossary/m-s/sensemaking|sensemaking]] for the process by which people give meaning to ambiguous events and act on that meaning. Collective-intelligence tools (Polis, Talk to the City) use it for gathering many views into a shared picture. "Place-based sensemaking" fits the collective-intelligence use, with Weick's stress on acting while things are still unclear.

**Community currency, complementary currency, commitment pool.** These follow the wiki's [[concepts/commitment-pooling|Commitment Pooling]] page and the glossary entries for [[glossary/a-e/community-inclusion-currency|community inclusion currency]] and [[glossary/a-e/bioregional-currency|bioregional currency]] [MS]. A study of the Sarafu Network describes Grassroots Economics' commitment pool as "a smart contract mechanism that functions as a liquidity pool" (Evite, arXiv 2512.04030).

**Small model, open-weight model, local-first.** A small model here is a language model of roughly 0.1 to 10 billion parameters that runs on one consumer or workstation GPU; Lelapa AI's InkubaLM, at 0.4 billion parameters, is an example. An [[glossary/m-s/open-weight-model|open-weight model]] is one whose trained weights are published for download, under licences that range from permissive to restricted. [[glossary/f-l/local-first|Local-first]] software keeps the main copy of data on the user's own device, works offline and syncs when connected.

**Deployment period, golden age, turning point.** These follow Carlota Perez, *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital* (2002); see [[glossary/a-e/deployment-period|deployment period]]. Each surge has an installation period led by financial capital, a turning point (usually a crash and recession) and a deployment period in which new institutions spread the technology across the economy.

The record supports the claim that great infrastructure booms ended in investor losses and left usable assets. It doesn't support the claim that every boom did. Andrew Odlyzko's study of the British railway mania of the 1830s describes "a giant and wildly speculative investment episode that was profitable for investors."

The one case in the table with a primary price record for the sale of a data centre is El Segundo. A building passed to a consolidator at about 43 per cent of build cost, four years after the bankruptcy, and carried commercial colocation afterwards. Local, co-operative or public use appears in the record through institutions created by statute: rural electric co-operatives after 1936, and British nationalisation in 1948. Both came a decade or more after the crash that preceded them.

## P1. Repricing

What the record shows to 13 November 2026.

**Shares.** The Nasdaq Composite set a record close of 27,599.89 on 6 October 2026. It fell 5.5 per cent on 5 November to close at 24,315, which was 11.6 per cent below the record and its third correction of 2026 (CNBC). It closed at 25,366.09 on 12 November, about 8 per cent below the record. The Nasdaq-100 closed at 28,289.19 on 12 November against a 52-week high of 31,361.37 [IC]. This is a correction by market convention. It is far from a bear market and far from the 78 per cent fall in the Nasdaq from 2000 to 2002.

**Triggers reported.** CNBC tied the 5 November fall to the midterm results. Reports on 12 November tied a further fall in Nvidia shares to turmoil over its board and its chief executive; this brief found no primary record of a board decision [TBV]. Yahoo Finance put the 10-year Treasury yield near 5.07 per cent on 12 November.

**Credit.** US high-yield spreads reached 348 basis points during the 5 November selloff, the widest since May 2025, on ICE BofA data reported by Reuters (as summarised by IndexBox) [IC]. Galaxy Helios Data Centers II priced US\$3.507 billion of 9.875 per cent senior secured notes due 2031 in July 2026, to build part of a CoreWeave-leased campus in Dickens County, Texas (company filing, reported by Dow Jones) [IC]. Those bonds are reported to have fallen to 89.25 cents on 5 November, a yield of about 13 per cent [TBV, single report]. CoreWeave's 9.625 per cent notes due 2032 last traded at 76.42 cents on FINRA TRACE, as read by FourWeekMBA on 5 November; CNBC and 24/7 Wall St reported the same notes yielding more than 16 per cent on 6 November, which fits that price [IC]. CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps peaked above 1,289 basis points on 5 November and settled near 1,155 on 6 November, on Deutsche Bank data reported by CNBC (as summarised by TechJack Solutions; also Landgate) [IC]. Swaps on Oracle reached a record 288 basis points and on SoftBank 424, on S&P Global and LSEG data reported by Yahoo Finance [IC]. Oracle's new bonds of 2 November carried coupons of 7 to 8.65 per cent [TBV].

**Loan sales.** Bloomberg reported on 5 November that about US\$500 million of data centre loans, later reported as held by ING, were up for auction with bids due 19 November, including a US\$25 million loan tied to the Stargate site in New Mexico. Bloomberg also reported that banks including JPMorgan and MUFG had spent more than six months distributing US\$38 billion of construction debt for Oracle-leased campuses (both as reported by FourWeekMBA and Startup Fortune) [IC]. Loans for Oracle's Project Jupiter were reported quoted at 89 to 91 cents, a figure attributed to the *Financial Times* in commentary [TBV].

**Cancellations and delays.** A tracker kept by Programs.com counts 69 large US projects (at least 100 MW or US\$1 billion) cancelled since 2024, to 4 November 2026 [TBV, single tracker]. Data Center Watch counts at least 45 projects worth about US\$68 billion blocked or delayed by local opposition from April to June 2026 (as reported by XenoSpectrum) [TBV]. Net Power's quarterly filing records that Texas paused data centre permits on 21 September 2026 [IC, SEC filing]. Nebius said on 12 November that its third-quarter contracts covered all of the associated capex (Data Center Dynamics) [MS].

**Capex.** No cut to spending guidance by the largest buyers was found. Alphabet's 10-Q for the second quarter of 2026 reported US\$122.8 billion of assets not yet in service, up from US\$78.5 billion at the end of 2025 [TBV, figure as reported in the research run].

**Defaults and distressed sales.** None found for AI data centres or accelerators to 13 November 2026.

**Anthropic.** Anthropic filed an amended S-1 on 10 November 2026 proposing 825 million shares at US\$70 to US\$78, a valuation reported at about US\$1.4 trillion, with listing reported for 19 or 20 November (Yahoo Finance; tastytrade) [IC that the filing was made]. Reports of the filing cite third-quarter revenue of US\$17 billion, an operating loss of US\$567 million and about US\$895.4 billion of purchase commitments (Tech Insider) [TBV until the S-1 itself is read].

**Reading.** P1 is supported in form. AI shares are in correction, AI debt is priced as risky, and banks are selling loans at a discount. The size of the move is far below 2000 to 2002, and the largest buyers haven't cut capex. The strongest evidence against the link is the cash those buyers hold, which lets them absorb a repricing without selling assets.

## P2. Public refusal

**Opinion.** Pew Research Center found in June 2025 that 50 per cent of US adults were more concerned than excited about the growing use of AI in daily life, up from 37 per cent in 2021, with 10 per cent more excited (as reported by *Forbes*, 22 June 2026) [IC]. A later Pew short read put the share at 52 per cent in a survey of late June 2026 [TBV, the page wasn't opened]. In a Pew survey of February 2026, 71 per cent of US adults said the growing use of AI would make their personal information less secure, against 3 per cent who said more secure [IC, Pew record]. These are surveys of attitude and stated reasons. The run found no measured series of users leaving surveillance-based platforms.

**Votes on surveillance.** On 3 November 2026 voters in York, Maine, approved a citizen-led measure that removes the town's automated licence plate reader cameras and requires voter approval for any future surveillance technology (Newsweek; Texas Tribune) [IC]. GadgetReview reports the margin as 72.5 per cent [TBV, single report]. Voters in League City, Texas, rejected continued police use of the cameras in an advisory vote, and the city council had agreed in October to abide by the result (Newsweek) [IC]. The Ash Center also lists a measure setting rules on surveillance technology in Pflugerville, Texas, without its outcome. Earlier in 2026 the South Portland city council in Maine voted to end its Flock camera contract before expiry (Maine Wire; *Portland Press Herald*) [IC]. Newsweek reports that Texas's governor directed state agencies in August 2026 to stop funding Flock cameras [TBV, single report]. Right of reply: Newsweek reports it asked Flock Safety for comment; no published response was found. Supporters of the cameras describe them as investigative tools (Newsweek).

**Votes on data centres.** Ballotpedia records that voters favoured limiting data centre development in 40 of 41 local measures on 3 November, approving 27 restrictive measures and defeating 13 supportive ones; the exception was Harwood, North Dakota [IC, with the Ash Center]. Six earlier 2026 measures also favoured limits, so Ballotpedia's count for the year is 46 of 47, against the 44 of 45 recorded in the early November snapshot from another tally. Ohio held 21 of the 41. The side favouring limits won more than 70 per cent in 30 measures (Ash Center) [IC]. Several Maine towns approved data centre moratoriums (News From the States, 5 November 2026). An NBC News exit poll, as cited by the Motley Fool, found 76 per cent of voters would oppose a new data centre in their community [TBV]. The Ash Center reports NBC exit polling in which more than half of voters rated AI and data centres a crucial or major factor in their congressional vote.

**Law and procurement.** The EU AI Act's prohibited practices, including untargeted scraping of facial images to build recognition databases, have applied since 2 February 2025 (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5) [IC]. San Francisco banned city use of facial recognition in 2019 [IC]. Anthropic's dispute with the US Department of Defense, called the Department of War in the court record, followed its refusal to drop contract limits on military use of its models (dark fibre brief). Judge Rita Lin of the Northern District of California granted Anthropic a preliminary injunction against its designation as a supply chain risk on 26 March 2026 (AP; Axios), and on 27 August 2026 granted summary judgment finding the designation unlawful (Taft Law) [IC]. On 25 September 2026 the D.C. Circuit, 2 to 1, upheld a parallel designation made under a different statute, the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act, and stayed its decision so that Anthropic could seek rehearing (ABC News; Taft Law) [IC]. Right of reply: a senior Pentagon official said on 3 September 2026 that Anthropic remained a designated supply chain risk (*Washington Examiner*; Unite.AI); Anthropic said it disagreed with the appellate decision (dark fibre brief).

**Counter-evidence.** AI-focused super PACs aligned with OpenAI and Anthropic had spent US\$55.7 million by late September 2026, mostly in the primaries (*New York Times*, cited by the Ash Center) [IC]. The Ash Center names affordability, electricity bills, data centres and AI's effect on jobs as the central concerns of the election, with no mention of surveillance among them. Revenue at the largest data-driven firms continued to grow through 2026 on figures seen in this run; the run didn't build a series. The "privacy paradox" literature, which finds stated concern a weak predictor of behaviour, wasn't reviewed here [TBV].

**Reading.** Refusal of surveillance is measurable in opinion, in a few local votes and council decisions, and in EU law. User exit is unmeasured. The data centre votes are a separate matter, taken up in Q4.

## P3. Dark AI

**Accelerator prices.** Rental prices for the Nvidia H100 rose through 2026. The Silicon Data H100 index went from US\$2.00 per GPU-hour on 9 December 2025 to US\$3.01 on 9 November 2026 and US\$3.06 on 12 November. The Ornn Compute Price Index settled at US\$3.25 on 11 November, up 8.3 per cent in a month. GetDeploying's median on-demand H100 price across 44 providers was US\$4.00 on 12 November, up about 12 per cent in 90 days [IC, three independent indexes]. This is the reverse of the price path dark fibre took after 2001.

**Asset transfers.** The secondary market found in this run trades AI debt at a discount: loans near 89 to 91 cents, CoreWeave notes near 76 cents, and a US\$500 million loan auction. No sale of an AI data centre or accelerator fleet at a distressed price was found to 13 November 2026.

**What lasts.** David Rosenthal estimates that about 40 per cent of an AI data centre's cost is buildings, power connections and cooling, and about 60 per cent is accelerator racks ("Dark GPUs", DSHR's Blog, 15 October 2026) [MS, commentator's estimate]. He writes that with fibre "the surplus initial capacity was both long-lived and cheap", which AI data centres are not, and he notes the carrying cost of an idle building, which a buried cable lacked. Power sites from the crypto crash have already passed to AI use: Galaxy's Helios site now carries data centre bonds [TBV for the conversion terms].

**Inference prices.** Prices per token keep falling as model makers compete and models get more efficient. The run found no part of that fall that can be traced to idle hardware, and the rental indexes show hardware is scarce.

**Open-weight models.** Lelapa AI publishes InkubaLM (0.4 billion parameters) openly with its datasets [IC]. The run didn't assemble download counts for the leading open-weight models, and found no open-weight model released or abandoned because its maker ran into financial trouble [TBV].

**Reading.** P3 is contradicted at the run date. The durable layer can be named, and the market is pricing the debt against it, but compute is getting dearer.

## I. The inheritance condition

**Historical rules.** Electricity reached US farms through the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 and member-owned co-operatives, and British railways came under public ownership in 1948. Both came by statute, years after the crash [TBV, unchecked in this run]. Common-carrier duties and the unbundling rules of 1996 governed who could use telecom capacity; the dark fibre brief found the fibre shares unproven [MS, wiki brief].

**Present rules.** No open-access rule for compute was found. The rules voters passed in November 2026 govern siting and local benefit. Perry, Ohio's Issue 16 requires hyperscale developers seeking tax incentives to post financial guarantees to the community. Grove City, Ohio's Issue 28 requires voter approval for developments over 50 acres, 20 MW or 500,000 gallons of water a day (Ballotpedia) [IC]. New York's Empire State Development is reported to have recommended community investment of US\$1 million per MW in September 2026 guidance [TBV]. OpenAI published a policy document after the election on data centres, which it now calls "compute campuses" (Ash Center) [IC that it was published]. The public-compute programmes and stranded-asset proposals recorded in the dark fibre brief (Vanderbilt's *After the AI Crash*, the Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives, EU AI Factories, NAIRR, Apertus) weren't re-checked here and stand as that brief recorded them.

**Data governance.** Te Hiku Media's Kaitiakitanga License governs data. The [[sources/carroll-2020-care-principles|CARE Principles]] govern Indigenous data. Neither reaches compute, and no licence or principle found in this run governs access to compute. Te Hiku's compute is its own. *MIT Technology Review* reported in 2022 that Te Hiku bought its own machine to train its language models [IC, with NVIDIA 2024]. Its chief technology officer told NVIDIA in 2024 that it runs eight A100 GPUs in its building in the far north of New Zealand [MS for the count].

**Capital allocators buying whole landscapes.** The research instructions asked whether capital allocators are moving money into whole-ecosystem regeneration, naming Oxygen Conservation. The clearest present case of durable assets moving to new owners is British land bought for natural capital.

- Oxygen Conservation bought 23,000 acres in Scotland in 2023 (Blackburn and Hartsgarth on Langholm Moor, and Invergeldie near Comrie) with a £20.55 million Triodos Bank loan (Triodos; *Scottish Financial News*; *Daily Record*) [IC]. Its purchase of the Kinrara estate in the Cairngorms took it past 50,000 acres under management (*Scottish Financial News*; Burges Salmon) [IC]. Its portfolio value is reported as £275 million (*Just Drinks*) and as about £400 million with about two million carbon credits (a company director, *Renewable Matter*, 7 April 2026) [MS; the two figures conflict]. Community Land Scotland reports Oxygen Conservation's stated aim of 250,000 acres worth £1 billion by 2030, and then selling the whole portfolio [MS, as reported].
- Gresham House's Forest Fund VI closed at £375 million in May 2025 (Gresham House; Reuters; *IPE Real Assets*) [IC]. Gresham House's own February 2025 submission to the Scottish Parliament puts land registered in its name or its funds' names at about 120,000 hectares, about 1.5 per cent of Scotland [MS, checked against the document]. Andy Wightman's analysis of Land Register records counts at least 66,879 hectares held through Gresham House partnerships by August 2025, which makes it Scotland's second-largest private landowner on his count [IC, independent analysis of public records]. Right of reply: Gresham House says the land is ultimately owned by its investors (*The Guardian*).
- Highlands Rewilding completed its purchase of the 1,300-hectare Tayvallich estate in Argyll on 16 May 2023, with crowdfunding, private investment and a bridging loan from the UK Infrastructure Bank (UK Infrastructure Bank; *Inverness Courier*) [IC]. Its memorandum with the community body, the Tayvallich Initiative, commits it to no evictions, to keeping at least the current number of jobs, to Rural Housing Burdens on land it sells, and to a community right of first refusal on future sales (*The Scotsman*; *The National*) [IC]. The Scottish Land Fund gave the Tayvallich Initiative £565,608 to buy 34 acres for affordable housing, and half the estate (635 hectares) was later sold to the Barrahormid Trust, which keeps the memorandum (*The National*; *Scottish Construction Now*) [IC]. Highlands Rewilding reports £11 million of equity raised and 1,785 hectares operated (September 2025) [MS].
- Community Land Scotland described the new natural-capital buyers, Oxygen Conservation and Gresham House among them, as "mega-lairds" in December 2025, and returned to Oxygen Conservation in April 2026. Right of reply: Oxygen Conservation's response, quoted in *The Scotsman*, is that land use change will always draw a range of opinion, and its own "Criticise Us" page concedes it doesn't spend enough time in local communities.

On this record, natural-capital buying is a trend among capital allocators in Britain. It is located in places without being governed by them. The contested cases fit the inheritance condition: without a rule, durable assets pass to whoever holds capital. The British cases are worked examples only; how such buying compares with the [[glossary/a-e/commons|commons]] cases on this wiki is a question for the register pages.

## P4. Place-based uptake

The research instructions separate three kinds of evidence: AI used for sensemaking, local exchange run without AI, and the two combined. They are kept apart here.

**Sensemaking with AI.** Te Hiku Media built its speech models with NVIDIA's open-source NeMo toolkit on its own eight A100 GPUs, and reports 92 per cent accuracy in transcribing te reo Māori (NVIDIA blog, January 2024; Waatea News, September 2024) [MS, Te Hiku's own figure as reported]. Te Hiku has written that it would not use OpenAI's Whisper in production, because the model conflicts with the terms of the Kaitiakitanga License (Papa Reo blog) [MS]. Lelapa AI trained InkubaLM on 1.9 billion tokens in five African languages, using compute credits from Microsoft's AI for Good Lab, and sells its Vulavula service as a paid API; its datasets were built with the Masakhane research community (*MIT Technology Review*, 11 November 2024; ITWeb) [IC]. The dark fibre brief found that Digital Green's Farmer.Chat depends on GPT-4 [MS, wiki brief]. EarthRanger, Polis and Talk to the City weren't re-checked in this run.

**Local exchange without AI.** The open Sarafu dataset ([[sources/mattsson-criscione-ruddick-sarafu|Mattsson, Criscione and Ruddick 2022]]) covers about 55,000 registered accounts from January 2020 to June 2021 [IC]. The research run reported about 400,000 transactions among about 24,000 transacting users, worth about 300 million Sarafu (about US\$2.8 million), and attributed the figures to Mattsson et al. (2026); the abstract of that paper doesn't give them [TBV]. Grassroots Economics now runs vouchers and commitment pools on the Celo blockchain with open-source tooling. Its Sarafu Wallet announcement describes infrastructure and pools with no AI component [MS]. Records for WIR, the Chiemgauer, Banco Palmas and the Bristol Pound (closed 30 September 2021) weren't re-checked; the WIR case is treated in [[research/pooling-across-timn|Pooling Across TIMN]].

**Where the two meet.** No community currency, mutual credit network or commitment pool was found using AI for matching, credit assessment, translation or reporting. The run searched records for Grassroots Economics and Sarafu, Circles UBI, the Chiemgauer and WIR. [[people/practitioners/ruddick-will|Will Ruddick]] of Grassroots Economics has written that "we are starting to see GROUPS of AI cooperating using Commitment Pooling", a statement about prospects that cites no deployment [MS]. The nearest case is Nattergal's biodiversity credits, validated with Earthly using AI pre-screening (Carbon Herald) [MS]. That is a credit market for land, and it falls outside local exchange as defined here.

**Compute needs.** The record shows two patterns. Te Hiku bought modest hardware and owns it. Lelapa trained on donated hyperscaler credits and sells an API. Neither depends on a repricing.

**BioHubs.** Metabolic, Anura Capital, Artistree and Basin Collective's report *[[sources/gladek-metabolic-biohubs|BioHubs: A Pathway to Regional Resilience]]* (May 2026, [biohubs.earth](https://biohubs.earth)) maps 152 initiatives in 44 countries working under the coordination logic the wiki calls a BioHub [MS, the authors' own census]. [[essays/what-is-a-biohub|What Is a BioHub]] draws on it. Neither that page, [[concepts/the-bioplace-layer|The BioPlace Layer]] nor the wiki's tools pages record a BioHub or Bioregional Development Organisation using AI tools for sensemaking or exchange. The AI tools on the wiki are research instructions run by practitioners, and the [[tools/bioregion/planet-ai-app|Planet AI App]] runs on frontier models [MS].

## P5. Place-based renaissance

Perez's golden ages ran largely through national institutions. Her postwar deployment of mass production went through welfare states and large firms [TBV, unchecked in this run]. The institutions that carried electricity to rural places in the 1930s were federal loans and member co-operatives, a mixed case of national money and local ownership.

This run didn't reach measured outcome studies for Preston, the Evergreen co-operatives, Mondragon, Emilia-Romagna, LM3 or community land trusts, so it reports no measured regeneration against comparison areas. The wiki's own evidence pages are mixed. [[research/water-retention-evidence|Retention Works on the Slope, Unproven at the River]] finds plot-scale gains and no catchment-scale proof. [[research/landscape-restoration-sub-saharan-africa|Community-Led Landscape Restoration in Sub-Saharan Africa]] grades sixteen cases, many on their own reporting [MS].

The [[frameworks/s-curve-thesis|S-Curve Thesis]], a `working-hypothesis` on this wiki, places present civilisation in the deceleration phase and reads the retention lineage (Polanyi, Ostrom, Raworth, Daly) as that phase's intellectual expression [MS]. The run found no historical test of the implication tested here: that deceleration is when place-based coordination gains ground. The count of 152 initiatives is evidence that the form exists. It isn't a series showing growth, and it says nothing about outcomes.

## Prior art

- **Carlota Perez** argues that a crash ends the installation period and that institutions then steer deployment. Her later work calls for a green golden age [TBV in this run]. Her published view, quoted in the dark fibre brief, is that AI belongs to the information revolution that began half a century ago, which weighs against reading AI as a new installation period.
- **Andrew Odlyzko** documents both the losses of the 1840s railway mania and the profitable 1830s mania, which together make the strongest historical case against treating crashes as universal.
- **David Rosenthal** (DSHR, October 2026) is the strongest critic of the dark fibre analogy: the fibre surplus was long-lived and cheap, and AI data centres are neither.
- **Commentators on the AI build-out** repeat the dark fibre argument in commercial form. Doug Levin writes that "the eventual surplus could become the foundation for a new wave of AI-native products", with no place-based element.
- **[[people/theorists/bauwens-michel|Michel Bauwens]]** and the P2P Foundation argue for [[glossary/a-e/cosmo-local-production|cosmo-local production]], and Grassroots Economics uses the word "cosmo-local" in its Sarafu Wallet announcement.
- **[[people/practitioners/ruddick-will|Will Ruddick]]** writes on commitment pooling, and has written about AI agents using it as a prospect.
- **[[people/theorists/raworth-kate|Kate Raworth]], Douglas Rushkoff, degrowth and post-growth authors, and public-AI advocates** weren't reviewed in this run.

No author found makes the full chain, from an AI crash through place-based inheritance to community currencies. The hypothesis is new as a combination. Each of its parts has prior art.

## Questions graded

**Q1 (P1). Every major infrastructure boom since about 1790 ended in a repricing that cut investors' returns far below expectations.**
For: the British railway mania of 1845 to 1849 (share index down 64 per cent); telecoms 2000 to 2002; dot-com data centres. Against: Odlyzko's 1830s railway mania was profitable for investors. Grade: [IC] for the cases; contradicted as a universal. Status: `academic-inference`. Practitioner: most large booms have ended in investor losses, and "every" is false.

**Q2 (P1). The AI build-out shows the financial signs that preceded past repricings.**
For: an 11.6 per cent Nasdaq correction (5 November 2026); high-yield spreads at 348 basis points; data centre loans auctioned and quoted at 89 to 91 cents; CoreWeave notes near 76 cents. Against: no defaults; hyperscaler capex intact; the Nasdaq back within about 8 per cent of its record by 12 November. Grade: [IC]. Status: `documented-fact` for the signs, `structural-inference` for what they mean. Practitioner: the signs are present, and where they lead is open.

**Q3 (P2). Public refusal of surveillance-based business models has grown measurably since 2018, in law, votes or behaviour.**
For: the Pew trend in AI concern; 71 per cent expecting less secure personal information (2026); the York vote and the South Portland decision; the EU AI Act's prohibitions. Against: no measured user exit found; industry political spending. Grade: [IC] for opinion, votes and law; [TBV] for behaviour. Status: `working-hypothesis`. Practitioner: concern and some law are measurable, and exit isn't yet shown.

**Q4 (P2). The November 2026 data centre votes were driven in part by concern about surveillance.**
For: surveillance measures were on some of the same ballots. Against: the measures' texts address megawatts, water, acreage, zoning and financial guarantees, and the Ash Center names electricity bills, data centres and jobs as the drivers. Grade: no evidence for the claim. Status: `working-hypothesis`, unsupported. Practitioner: treat the votes as decisions about power, water and land.

**Q5 (P3). After past repricings, the durable residue passed to new owners at a fraction of its build cost and was used at lower prices.**
For: the El Segundo centre at about 43 per cent of build cost (Equinix 8-K, 2005); railway amalgamations; crypto sites converted to AI. Against: transfers took years, and the buyers were consolidators. Grade: [IC] for the data centre case. Status: `academic-inference`. Practitioner: residue does pass below cost, slowly, to whoever holds capital.

**Q6 (P3). The AI build-out has a durable residue that a secondary market is beginning to price.**
For: AI debt trades at a discount; loans are being auctioned. Against: H100 rental prices rose about 50 per cent in a year; no distressed asset sales. Grade: [IC] for the price data. Status: `documented-fact` that the claim is contradicted at the run date. Practitioner: there's no dark AI to inherit as of 13 November 2026.

**Q7 (I). After past repricings, the rules in force decided how much of the residue reached public, co-operative or local users.**
For: rural electric co-operatives under the 1936 Act; British nationalisation; land-reform objections to natural-capital buyers today. Against: the dark fibre brief found no institution that decided fibre shares [MS]. Grade: [TBV]. Status: `structural-inference`. Practitioner: plan on the assumption that, without a rule, capital takes the residue.

**Q8 (P4). Place-based bodies already use small or open-weight AI models for sensemaking.**
For: Te Hiku Media's own models on its own GPUs; Lelapa's InkubaLM. Against: Farmer.Chat runs on GPT-4; Lelapa trained on hyperscaler credits. Grade: [IC] for the cases; [MS] for performance figures. Status: `documented-fact`. Practitioner: language and land bodies already run their own models.

**Q9 (P4). Some community currencies or commitment pools already use AI in their operations.**
For: none found. Against: Grassroots Economics' published tooling has no AI component. Grade: no evidence. Status: `speculative-projection`. Practitioner: don't claim this exists.

**Q10 (P4). Place-based AI use depends little on frontier compute, so it would survive a repricing.**
For: Te Hiku runs eight A100s; InkubaLM has 0.4 billion parameters. Against: training credits came from hyperscalers; some services call frontier APIs. Grade: [IC] for the cases. Status: `structural-inference`. Practitioner: P4 can go ahead without P3, which makes the middle of the chain optional.

**Q11 (P5). Past deployment periods after a crash strengthened coordination at the scale of place.**
For: rural electric co-operatives. Against: Perez's postwar golden age ran through nation-states and large firms. Grade: [TBV]. Status: `academic-inference`. Practitioner: history favours national institutions, with local exceptions created by statute.

**Q12 (P5). Place-based economies and programmes show measured regeneration against comparison areas.**
For and against: not assembled in this run; the wiki's own evidence pages are mixed. Grade: no evidence gathered. Status: `working-hypothesis`. Practitioner: cite only outcome studies with comparison areas.

**The headline.** Best supported: P1, in form. Weakest: P3, contradicted by rising compute prices at the run date. No evidence either way: AI combined with local exchange (Q9), and measured regeneration (Q12). Q10 bears on the structure of the chain: if place-based AI runs on modest hardware, P4 doesn't need P3. The headline stays a `speculative-projection`.

## Evidence the hypothesis must face

1. **Repricing without transfer.** AI shares fell about a tenth and AI debt trades at a discount, yet capex guidance stands and no asset has changed hands.
2. **The chips don't wait.** On Rosenthal's estimate, 60 per cent of an AI data centre's cost is accelerators. Amazon shortened the useful life of a subset of its servers in 2025, citing the pace of AI development (dark fibre brief) [IC].
3. **Concentration after crashes.** Equinix bought Exodus buildings. Morgan reorganised the railroads. British natural-capital land is going to funds, one of which is Scotland's second-largest private landowner on an independent count of the Land Register.
4. **Refusal may stay local and partial.** The data centre votes concern power, water and land. Only a handful of votes addressed surveillance, while industry political spending reached tens of millions of dollars.
5. **Place-based AI may not need the overbuild.** Te Hiku bought its own GPUs before any repricing.
6. **Local currencies have a high failure rate.** The Bristol Pound ceased on 30 September 2021; ITV News reported the closure as due in part to the spread of contactless card payments. Studies of closure rates weren't retrieved in this run.
7. **Deployment periods have favoured national institutions.** Perez's postwar golden age is the standard case.
8. **The renaissance may be measured in claims.** Oxygen Conservation's and Highlands Rewilding's figures are their own.
