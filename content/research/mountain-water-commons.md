[[glossary/a-e/asset-based-community-development|Asset-Based Community Development]] (ABCD) as practiced has almost never treated a mountain's capacity to hold and release water as a community asset; the communities that do so govern their catchments through [[glossary/a-e/commons|commons]], customary, Indigenous and reciprocity traditions, and the evidence they offer is strongest for persistence and decision rights and weakest for measured downstream water.

## Overview

This brief codes 46 cases on six continents in which a community mobilized a living system in a mountain or upland catchment. For each case it records who began the work, who holds the land and the rules, who pays whom and on what trigger, what was measured, and what failed. It builds on three earlier runs: [[research/water-retention-evidence|Retention Works on the Slope, Unproven at the River]], [[research/landscape-restoration-sub-saharan-africa|Community-Led Landscape Restoration in Sub-Saharan Africa]] and [[concepts/exclusion-from-water-finance|Exclusion from Water Finance]]. It uses their hydrology grades and does not re-argue them.

The case base splits cleanly. Only three ABCD cases involve natural assets at all, and all three treat land, trees or water points as inputs to livelihoods. The cases in which a community names and governs the mountain's water function belong to other traditions: the acequias de careo of Sierra Nevada in Spain, the amunas (mamanteo) of the Peruvian Andes, the Alpine commons of Törbel and Cortina d'Ampezzo, the acequias of northern New Mexico, Bali's subak, Sikkim's dhara (springs) and the Tigray exclosures. Several of these peoples hold the mountain as kin or deity, and the brief reports that relationship in their terms.

A practitioner can safely take the following from the evidence. Water benefits are measurable at springs, wells and aquifers. Catchment-scale benefits are modeled or claimed. Governance outcomes, meaning who decides and for how long, are documented for centuries in some commons and for under a decade in most payment schemes. Arrangements funded by a utility or donor under rules set elsewhere have repeatedly been redirected, suspended or ended.

The [[concepts/water-retention-landscapes|Water Retention Landscapes]] concept supplies the physical reading of a mountain as a sponge (see also [[glossary/t-z/water-retention-landscape|water retention landscape]]). This brief asks the question the physical reading leaves open: who holds the sponge.

## ABCD and its limits

Kretzmann and McKnight's 1993 asset map has six categories: individual gifts, associations, local institutions, physical space, economic exchange and culture. Natural systems appear only inside "physical space". The Community Capitals Framework (Emery and Flora 2006) adds natural capital as a separate capital, but its cases are mostly rural United States towns.

MacLeod and Emejulu (2014) argue from Scottish practice that ABCD is "neoliberalism with a community face", fitting austerity because it asks communities to supply what the state withdrew. DeFilippis, Fisher and Shragge (2010) argue more generally that community-building approaches avoid conflict over ownership and power.

The cases here support a narrower version of that critique. ABCD rarely touches tenure, so it rarely reaches the question of who owns a mountain. Mathie, Cameron and Gibson (2017) defend asset-based practice as a way of building citizen-led power.

## What the syntheses conclude

**Coady Institute ABCD evaluations.** In Ethiopia, the ABCD program run with Oxfam Canada, Hundee, KMG Ethiopia and Agri-Service Ethiopia grew from five groups in 2003 to 24 in 2011. The groups mapped natural resources on their asset maps. Comparing the 2008 and 2011 maps "did not reveal many changes in the natural resource base or physical infrastructure" (Peters 2013, p. 19). The reported changes were one group terracing land, one group securing extra land, and Ilu Aga reviving a traditional irrigation practice, planting one hectare of trees and digging three wells and three boreholes, mostly with its own resources [MS]. No water measurement was made.

The Kenyan Makutano case shows the strongest ABCD-framed water governance found. It has 26 water points (nine dams and 17 sub-surface wells), each run by an elected committee registered as its own community-based organization with its own bank account, and members pay 250 Kenya shillings a year (Mahomed and Peters 2011) [MS]. Makutano is in semi-arid lowland Yatta, outside any mountain catchment. *From Clients to Citizens* (Mathie and Cunningham 2008) contains no case that governs a catchment.

**Commons scholarship.** Ostrom (1990) drew her design principles partly from Alpine commons (Törbel) and irrigation systems (Spanish huertas, Philippine zanjeras). Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás (2010) reviewed 91 studies and found the principles broadly supported. They reformulated them by splitting the boundary and congruence principles, and they noted acequias in New Mexico as an example. The reviewers flag that the principles describe structure and say less about trust and legitimacy, and that they are prone to being generalized beyond their cases.

**Mountain water towers.** Immerzeel et al. (2020) ranked 78 global [[glossary/t-z/water-tower|water towers]] by importance and vulnerability. Viviroli et al. (2020) project that about 1.5 billion people, 24% of the world's lowland population, will depend critically on runoff from mountains by the mid-twenty-first century, up from about 0.2 billion (7%) in the 1960s. South Africa's equivalent category is the [[glossary/m-s/strategic-water-source-area-swsa|strategic water source area]]. This literature establishes the asset physically and is silent on community tenure.

**Payments and reciprocity.** The water finance exclusion run found that water funds mostly keep rural stewards off boards, and that Mexico's hydrological payments reached poor ejidos because ejidos hold the forest. The cases here add one randomized test of a [[glossary/m-s/reciprocal-water-agreement|reciprocal water agreement]], a form of [[glossary/m-s/payment-for-ecosystem-services-pes|payment for ecosystem services]] (Watershared, Bolivia) and one long-term persistence test (Oaxaca, Mexico), both summarized below.

**Territories of life and biocultural heritage.** The ICCA Consortium and the UNEP-WCMC registry document community-conserved areas, which the Consortium calls [[glossary/t-z/territory-of-life|territories of life]]. IIED and ANDES document biocultural heritage territories such as the Potato Park in Cusco. These sources record governance, and none of them was designed to measure water.

## The distinctions

- **ABCD by name versus kindred practice.** Three rows are ABCD-labeled or ABCD-framed. The other 43 follow commons, customary, Indigenous, reciprocity, state-program or water-fund lineages.
- **Mapping versus governing.** The Ethiopian ABCD groups mapped resources (weak form). Törbel, the Regole d'Ampezzo, New Mexico acequias and the subak hold rules, rights and sanctions (strong form).
- **Naming versus measuring.** Huamantanga's comuneros located amuna outflow springs accurately before tracers confirmed them (Ochoa-Tocachi interview, National Geographic 2019). Sikkim's Devithans are sacred springs protected from disturbance, recorded by Tambe et al. (2012) without discharge figures. Both are forms of [[glossary/f-l/indigenous-knowledge|Indigenous knowledge]] about the retention function.
- **Local versus catchment gain, timing versus volume.** Amunas and careo shift timing: water infiltrated in the wet season resurfaces weeks to months later. Neither adds rain.
- **Commons versus market with a community owner.** Bolivia's reciprocal agreements pay individual landholders in kind through municipal water funds, a market-like contract inside community norms. Sierra Nevada careo is a commons run by irrigators' communities.
- **Kin versus infrastructure.** For Tūhoe, Te Urewera is an ancestor; for Ngā Iwi o Taranaki, Taranaki Maunga (Mount Taranaki) is a tupuna. The settlements write that relationship into law. Sedapal describes the same kind of slope as "natural infrastructure".
- **Community-led versus externally led.** Dhara Vikas is a state program under India's rural employment scheme. Watershared was designed by Fundación Natura Bolivia. Abreha we Atsbeha combined community labor with state food-for-work and regional bureau planning.

Coordination is coded with the [[frameworks/time-framework|TIME Framework]]: [[glossary/t-z/t-form-tribal|T-form (Tribal)]], [[glossary/f-l/i-form-institutional|I-form (Institutional)]], [[glossary/m-s/m-form-market|M-form (Market)]] and [[glossary/a-e/e-form-emergent|E-form (Emergent)]].

## Kindred traditions

### Commons and irrigation communities

Users hold use rights and set rules on rotation, maintenance labor and sanctions (Ostrom 1990). Main cases: Törbel, Valais bisses, acequias de careo, New Mexico acequias, subak, aflaj. These differ from ABCD in starting from a bounded resource and a rulebook, and they count members by rights, which also excludes people.

### Territories of life (ICCAs)

A people or community governs an area with a close relationship to it and achieves conservation outcomes (ICCA Consortium). Main cases: the Ogiek claim to the Mau, Te Urewera, the Potato Park. These start from self-determination and tenure claims.

### Biocultural heritage territories

ANDES and IIED's model combines customary law, landscape and genetic resources. The Potato Park, run by Quechua communities near Pisac, is the reference case. Water is part of the territory and has not been measured.

### Reciprocal water agreements

Downstream users pay upstream landholders in kind to protect forest and springs, through a local fund under municipal and cooperative rules (Asquith, Vargas and Wunder 2008). Bétrisey and Mager (2014) describe ayni and minka reciprocity norms shaping the agreements in Florida Province.

### Springshed management

Hydrogeological mapping of a spring's recharge area (its [[glossary/m-s/springshed|springshed]]) is followed by recharge works, often with para-hydrogeologists trained from the village (Tambe et al. 2012; ACWADAM; People's Science Institute). It names the retention function most explicitly of any tradition found, and is usually state-led or NGO-led. The wiki's [[glossary/t-z/watershed-mapping|watershed mapping]] entry covers the mapping step.

### Rights of nature and legal personhood

Statute makes the mountain or river a legal person with a joint iwi-Crown voice (Te Urewera Act 2014, Te Awa Tupua Act 2017, Taranaki Maunga Collective Redress Act 2025). It changes standing and governance, and so far nothing has been measured about water.

### Siembra y cosecha de agua (sowing and harvesting water)

The Andean and Iberian practice of diverting wet-season or snowmelt water onto permeable slopes to recharge aquifers (amunas, careo, qochas). Jódar, Martos-Rosillo and colleagues (2022) link the Iberian and Andean forms explicitly.

### Traditions not reached

Indigenous Protected Areas, community land trusts holding catchment land and watershed councils were in the run's search list. Eigg is the only land trust row, at [TBV], and no watershed council or Indigenous Protected Area case was verified.

## Cases by mountain system

The case narratives for each region are on the regional pages of the [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/index|case register]]. Each page tells how its cases began, who holds the mountain, how value flows, what the evidence shows and what failed, and embeds a table of the region's cases.

- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/andes/index|Andes]] (7 cases). Andean comunidades have revived pre-Inca recharge canals and restored high wetlands, and Bolivian municipalities have set up reciprocal agreements with downstream users; in most of these cases a utility, a water fund or an NGO holds the money.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/mexico-and-north-america/index|Mexico and North America]] (4 cases). Mexican comunidades hold their forests and the payments made for them, New Mexico acequias run a centuries-old irrigation commons, and Catskills farmers supply New York City under the city's agreement.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/iberia-mediterranean-and-arabia/index|Iberia, the Mediterranean and Arabia]] (5 cases). Irrigators' communities in Sierra Nevada maintain snowmelt recharge channels with measured aquifer effects, while customary systems in the Atlas and Oman are weakening under state forestry and private wells.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/alps-and-europe/index|Alps and Europe]] (6 cases). Alpine commons such as Törbel and the Regole d'Ampezzo have held forests, pastures and water under their own statutes for centuries, with membership rules that also exclude newcomers.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/himalaya/index|Himalaya]] (5 cases). Springshed programs in the Indian Himalaya name the mountain's recharge function most precisely of any tradition found, and most are led by the state or NGOs.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/southeast-and-east-asia/index|Southeast and East Asia]] (4 cases). Terrace and water-temple systems in Bali, the Cordillera and Yunnan have coordinated mountain water for centuries and are now losing land and water to tourism and labor to out-migration.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/africa/index|Africa]] (12 cases). African mountain catchments appear in the register mostly as state land, water-fund project sites or contested territories, and the communities in them seldom hold the money.
- [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/oceania/index|Oceania]] (3 cases). Aotearoa New Zealand's settlements made a forest, a river and a mountain legal persons with iwi-Crown governance; no change in water has yet been measured.

## The nine predictions

**P1. ABCD practice rarely counts living systems as assets.**
For: 3 of 46 rows are ABCD by name or framing, and none names A8 or governs a mountain catchment. The Coady Ethiopia maps showed few natural-resource changes. Against: Coady's groups did put natural resources on asset maps, and the Kenyan ICRAF work is about trees. Grade: [IC] for the Coady record; the count is this brief's coding. Status: academic-inference. The wiki can say: "In the cases found, ABCD-labeled work mapped land, trees and water points, and none governed a mountain catchment."

**P2. Secure community tenure over a mountain catchment goes with maintained retention function over decades.**
For: Törbel, the Regole, careo, New Mexico acequias and Oaxaca's community forests persisted under secure collective rights. In the Sierra de Juárez, forest cover under community management fell until 2001 and then recovered, and by 2014 slightly exceeded its 1979 extent (Navarro Cerrillo et al. 2019). The Ogiek case shows the reverse: insecure tenure, evictions and a 19% tree cover loss. Against: no study measures the retention function itself against tenure. Remoteness, low population and abandonment confound the Alpine and Iberian cases, and Tigray shows retention restored on state land. Grade: [TBV] as a causal claim. Status: working-hypothesis. The wiki can say: "Long-lived mountain water commons share secure collective rights; the link to measured retention has not been tested."

**P3. Communities that hold the rules and the money keep arrangements going longer.**
For: Oaxaca's assemblies, which hold both, outlasted the PSAH effect. MERESE money held by the state was redirected in 2020, and Slovakia's program ended with its funding. Makutano's committees hold accounts and persisted [MS]. Against: few cases separate rule-holding from money-holding, and survivors dominate the old commons. Grade: [TBV]. Status: working-hypothesis. The wiki can say: "In the cases found, arrangements with money and rules held elsewhere were redirected or ended more often."

**P4. Reciprocal arrangements with downstream users produce measured water outcomes.**
For: local E. coli fell where cattle were excluded (Watershared), and careo and amunas show recharge with timing shifts. Against: the one randomized test found no detectable effect at the scale of the communities' water supplies. Amunas catchment gains are modeled, and UTNWF flow claims are self-reported. Grade: [IC] against. Status: documented-fact for the null result. The wiki can say: "The only randomized test of a reciprocal water agreement found local gains and no landscape-scale water-quality effect."

**P5. Communities with a named mountain water asset move from needy to needed.**
For: Carampoma's guardianship and maintenance role at Milloc, and the Catskills farmers under the city agreement. Against: in both, the payer set the terms; the amunas maintenance contract was proposed and not found executed. Grade: [MS]. Status: working-hypothesis. The wiki can say: "A few utilities now pay mountain communities to maintain water works, on terms the utility sets."

**P6. Legal personhood or rights of nature changes water outcomes.**
For: governance shifted in Te Urewera to a Tūhoe-majority board. Against: no water measurement exists for any of the three settlements. Te Awa Tupua holds no water rights, and consents to take water proceed without its consent. Grade: [IC] that governance changed and water is unmeasured. Status: documented-fact (governance); speculative-projection (water). The wiki can say: "Legal personhood changed who speaks for the mountain or river; no change in water has been measured."

**P7. Beyond-market arrangements get pulled back into market or state forms.**
For: the MERESE redirection, Ogiek evictions despite rulings, subak land and water lost to tourism, aflaj undercut by wells, and Tyrol disputes over value. Against: Törbel, the Regole and the Oaxaca assemblies resisted, protected by legal recognition of collective title and membership rules. Grade: [IC]. Status: structural-inference. The wiki can say: "State and market pressure has eroded several mountain water commons; legally recognized collective title protected others." The wiki's [[glossary/f-l/green-grabbing|green grabbing]] entry covers one form of this pull.

**P8. Cultural and sacred relationships with the mountain sustain stewardship where money does not.**
For: Lansing's water temples coordinated irrigation for centuries, Sikkim's Devithans springs are protected, and the Māori settlements rest on ancestral relationship. Against: Balinese farmers cite ritual costs as a burden, and sacred status did not stop subak land conversion. The evidence is ethnographic. Grade: [TBV]. Status: working-hypothesis. The wiki can say: "Ethnographies record sacred relationships alongside long stewardship; causation is untested."

**P9. Practitioner and program claims match independent measurement.**
Watershared: NGO accounts of conservation benefits set against a null randomized result on water quality. Amunas: "33%" in the press against a 7.5% mean in the paper. Abreha we Atsbeha: the water table is given as rising from about 50 m to about 5 m below the surface, from 15 m to 3 m, or by about 5 m, depending on the source. UTNWF: 27 million liters a day [MS] with no independent gauge found. Dhara Vikas: E3 gains, plus a Manipur brief showing declines. Grade: [IC] that gaps exist. Status: documented-fact. The wiki can say: "Flagship claims overstate or outrun independent measurement in most cases checked."

## Evidence the claims must face

1. **Survivorship.** Törbel, the Regole and the subak are famous because they survived. Iriai, many aflaj and most abandoned careo are the failed or faded counterparts.
2. **Romanticism and exclusion.** The Regole are hereditary. Törbel excluded non-citizens. Acequia and falaj rights follow land or shares. Water committee and temple membership in Bali follows land ownership. Women's access is rarely reported.
3. **Remoteness.** Alpine and Andean commons persisted where outsiders had little interest; the Mau, which Kenya values as a water tower, saw evictions.
4. **Outside money.** Watershared, Aquafondo, UTNWF, Dhara Vikas and Abreha we Atsbeha all relied on NGO, utility, donor or state funding. The communities supplied labor and land.
5. **Downstream loss.** Careo and amunas move water in time, and careo also moves it between basins. No case measured what downstream users lost.
6. **Rainfall confounding.** Sikkim's spring gains and Tigray's groundwater rise come from before-after designs without rainfall controls.
7. **Self-reported totals.** Farmer counts, liters per day and hectares from UTNWF, Natura and Aquafondo are [MS].

Governance: the long-lived cases combine bounded membership and user-made rules with recognized collective title. Value: where money arrives from outside, the community holding the account (Oaxaca, Makutano) is associated with persistence, and the state or fund holding it (MERESE, Slovakia) with redirection or ending. Persistence: payments alone did not persist in effect after they stopped (Oaxaca).

## Applying the findings (the wiki's reading)

This section is the wiki's own reading of the cases.

- **Secure the boundary and the title first.** Törbel, the Regole and the Oaxaca comunidades held recognized collective rights before any payment. The Ogiek won in court and still lost the forest on the ground. A community preparing to hold a catchment should treat title and membership rules as the first asset.
- **Keep the account inside the community's rules.** Oaxaca's assemblies and Makutano's committees held money. MERESE showed what happens when the payer holds it. A [[glossary/a-e/commitment-pool|commitment pool]] in which downstream users' commitments are held under upstream rules matches the B4 and B8 pattern; the evidence supports that structure and does not test the pool itself.
- **Measure at the spring and the well, with a control.** Sikkim and Tigray show what before-after records can show; Watershared shows that randomization by community fails when catchments cross community lines. Pair a treated and an untreated spring or sub-catchment, and log rainfall.
- **Name timing honestly.** Careo and amunas shift water in time. Claims of new water invite the downstream objection the earlier run documented.
- **Avoid single-funder dependence.** Slovakia's program and the PSAH effect ended or faded with funding. A [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] that coordinates several downstream payers under one community rulebook would address the failure pattern seen here; no case tests that design.

## Provenance

Written on 9 November 2026 under version 0.1 of `abcde-mountain-water-commons-deep-research.md`. The research run made 18 web searches and one delegated check of Coady ABCD sources, mostly in English with some Spanish. The multilingual search the instructions call for (French, Italian, German, Nepali, Hindi, Indonesian, Chinese, Japanese, Amharic and te reo Māori) was not done.

The research tools could not open the wiki corpus pages during the run, so the brief was written without reading the current text or `epistemic_status` of the pages it builds on; section 7 of the instructions was used as given. Rows citing "Instruction lead, not re-verified" rest on the instructions' leads and standard literature and are graded [TBV]. Climate fields are indicative and were not checked against station records. The run's own text gave 45 rows; the table holds 46, and the counts here follow the table. Makutano and Valencia are included despite a weak mountain fit. Forest-cover outcomes (PSAH, Oaxaca) are graded as proxies and are not water measurements. The Abreha we Atsbeha groundwater conflict is reported and left unresolved. Citation details added at writing for works the run named without full entries (Kretzmann and McKnight, Immerzeel, Feng, Asquith, Grillos, Lansing, Netting, McKean, Cox 2014, Domínguez, Auclair, Muñoz-Piña, the New Zealand statutes and UNESCO listings) should be checked before source pages are created. No place-specific material from Ward 2 or the surrounding valley was used. Revised the same day: the Markdown case table and its spreadsheet link were replaced by an embedded base, `mountain-water-commons.base`, built from one page per case in the new [[bioconomy/cases/catchments/index|case register]], so that later enrichment runs update the cases in one place. An enrichment pass the same day checked the 25 cases then graded [TBV] (the hand-back had counted 22) with web search, mostly in English sources, with Austrian court records in German: 13 were raised to [IC], 5 set to [MS] and 7 left at [TBV]. It withdrew the coding of the High Atlas agdal as eroding, found a profile of FONAG that lists community representatives on its board, found no payment scheme at Paro (the documented Bhutanese case is Yakpugang), and added measured return-flow and recharge figures for the New Mexico acequias. A second, multilingual enrichment run the same day found a watershed payment at Paro (the Namey Nichu agreement of 2015), so the Paro case was rewritten as that agreement and Yakpugang was added as a 47th case; it also added Azheke as a named sub-entry of the Hani case and noted that the Coady Ethiopia and Makutano cases share an evaluator. Its grade changes were then applied to the case pages: uMngeni, FONAG, the Catskills, iriai, Eigg, the Potato Park and the Arequipa qochas were raised to [IC], the ACWADAM and People's Science Institute springsheds set to [MS], and six [MS] cases kept at [MS] with their new sources recorded. FONAG's own records show only institutional constituents on its board, which supports [[concepts/exclusion-from-water-finance|Exclusion from Water Finance]]. The regional subsections of "Cases by mountain system" then moved, without change to their wording, to the eight regional pages of the register, and this section now links to them. Checked the same day: the full citations added at writing were confirmed against publisher, DOI or repository records, except Kretzmann and McKnight, Netting, McKean, Lansing (2007), Auclair and Alifriqui, and the Domínguez et al. authors, which remain not re-read. The check corrected four entries. Bottazzi et al. (2014) is Bétrisey and Mager (2014). The 2018 Oaxaca land-cover study is Navarro Cerrillo et al. (2019); its abstract does not compare community and protected forests, so that comparison was removed. The Ogiek compliance judgment found partial compliance with the 2017 judgment and none with the 2022 reparations, where the brief had said no part of either. The New Zealand statute links were corrected. The Abreha we Atsbeha groundwater figures were reconciled with the two earlier briefs: Haile and Gebregziabher (2020), citing Gebregziabher et al. (2016), give a water table rising from about 50 m to about 5 m below the surface, where the research report had 45 m. Glossary entries were added for Asset-Based Community Development, territory of life, reciprocal water agreement, water tower and springshed, and fifteen source pages were created.
