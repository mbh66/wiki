From 2008 the number of South Africans holding degrees grew about twice as fast as managerial and professional employment, and the number of candidates for each municipal ward more than tripled between 2011 and 2026 while the number of wards rose 5 percent, so the record meets Turchin's definition of elite overproduction on two measured ratios; competition within the political class intensified over the same years, but South Africa's own literature on patronage and gatekeeper politics explains that competition without needing the ratio. [structural inference]

## Overview

Peter Turchin's structural-demographic theory names three pressures that, in combination, precede periods of political instability: 
1. popular immiseration, 
2. elite overproduction and 
3. state fiscal distress ([[sources/turchin-historical-dynamics|Turchin 2003]]; [[sources/turchin-ages-of-discord|Turchin 2016]]). 

[[research/south-africa-on-the-curve|South Africa on the Curve]] found measured evidence for two of them in South Africa: [[unemployment|unemployment]] rising to 33.6 percent in mid-2026 and public debt rising from 26.0 percent of GDP in 2008/09 to 78.9 percent in 2025/26 [IC]. It compiled no data on the third. This brief fills that gap.

Elite overproduction is a ratio: a larger elite counts as evidence only if aspirants grow faster than positions. The brief keeps three movements apart: elite expansion (more positions, and more people filling them), elite overproduction (aspirants outgrowing positions) and elite exit (members of the elite leaving through emigration or capital flight). The opening of elite positions to people apartheid had excluded is treated as expansion.

The evidence supports a narrower finding than the theory's full claim. Two ratios built for this brief rose after 2008, graduate unemployment doubled, and measures of competition within the political class rose in the same years. The timing does not show the ratio leading the competition, and the South African scholarship on patronage and "violent democracy" accounts for most of the same evidence. What the ratio adds is a count: more people pressing at each gate.

## Turchin's definition and measures

Turchin's research program describes the concept in these terms: 

> [!quote] Elite Overproduction
> *"Elite overproduction refers to growth in the number of wealthy or credentialed aspirants" ... "relative to the limited number of high-status positions,"* and the program expects the imbalance *"to increase competition within economic and political elites"* (Turchin, research program page, peterturchin.com) [IC]. 

In a 2023 interview on *End Times* he described the mechanism as "elite aspirants vying for a fixed number of power positions" and compared it to a game of musical chairs in which the chairs stay fixed and "you keep adding more and more players" (Grossmann 2023) [IC]. A peer-reviewed test of the theory quotes its formal statement: "Elite overproduction results when elite numbers and appetites exceed the ability of the society to sustain them" (Georgescu 2023) [IC].

The theory extends Jack Goldstone's demographic-structural account of state breakdown. *Secular Cycles* "elaborates and expands upon the demographic-structural theory first advanced by Jack Goldstone" and traces "elite numbers and incomes" beside population, prices, real wages, state finances and instability (Turchin and Nefedov 2009; Goldstone 2016 [1991]) [IC].

The measures vary by case. For the United States, Turchin's program used the largest private fortunes relative to median income, the cost of elite education relative to median income, and congressional polarization as an indicator of intra-elite fragmentation [IC]. Quantitative applications use Goldstone's Political Stress Indicator, in which Elite Mobilization Potential equals the elite fraction of the population divided by elite income relative to GDP per person; rising elite numbers raise it and rising relative elite income lowers it (Stachowiak and Pasek 2025) [IC].

So a count of aspirants means nothing until set against a count of positions, and rising top incomes reduce the pressure the formula records.

## The pre-1994 baseline

Before 1994 elite positions were reserved by law and practice for a racial minority. Section 77 of the Industrial Conciliation Act of 1956 legalized the reservation of skilled jobs for white workers [IC]. The Wiehahn Commission recommended the "abolition of statutory job reservation" in 1979 [IC]. The homeland system created a parallel set of offices: when the Government of National Unity rationalized the state, it had to merge "eleven of former public services" into one (Department of Public Service and Administration 1995) [IC]. The scale of those homeland administrations as a route into office for black South Africans before 1994 was not compiled [TBV].

Public universities enrolled between 495,356 and about 525,000 students in 1994, on the figures of the Department of Higher Education and Training and the Council on Higher Education (Africa Check 2019) [IC]. No series on the size or racial composition of the senior public service, corporate management or the professions in 1993 or 1994 was found in a form comparable with later data [TBV].

Growth in the number of black professionals, managers and office-holders after 1994 is the correction of a legal cap, and this brief does not count it as overproduction.

## The indicators

### E1. Elite aspirants

Higher education roughly doubled. Public university headcount enrolment rose from about 0.5 million in 1994 to 1,036,984 in 2017, 1,094,808 in 2020 and 1,068,046 in 2021 (DHET, as compiled in the *PSET Monitor* 2024) [IC].

The stock of degree holders more than doubled after 2008. Weighted Quarterly Labour Force Survey data give 1,140,448 people whose highest qualification was a bachelor's degree or higher in 2008, 1,392,863 in 2012, 1,603,585 in 2015, 1,933,449 in 2019 and 2,435,285 in 2023 (MacGinty 2024, a DHET-funded master's assignment, not peer-reviewed) [IC for the survey estimates]. That is growth of about 5.2 percent a year.

Graduate unemployment roughly doubled over the same years. The broad graduate unemployment rate, which counts discouraged work-seekers, was 5.8 percent in 2008, 9.2 percent in 2019 and 11.8 percent in 2023 [IC]. The official rate for graduates was 12.2 percent in the second quarter of 2025 and 12.4 percent in the second quarter of 2026, against 33.6 percent for the whole labor force ([[sources/stats-sa-qlfs-q2-2026|Statistics South Africa 2026]]) [IC]. The rise is concentrated: broad unemployment among African graduates went from 11.3 to 17.9 percent between 2008 and 2023, and among graduates in administration it reached 24.7 percent in 2023 [IC]. MacGinty attributes 6.4 of the 9.6-point fall in the graduate employment rate to the changing composition of the graduate pool (younger, more female, more African, different fields) [IC]. A study of the 1995 to 2011 surveys called graduate unemployment "a much exaggerated problem" (van der Berg and van Broekhuizen 2012) [IC]; the rise came after its window.

Candidates for elected office multiplied. In municipal elections the Electoral Commission certified: 
- 30,081 candidates in 2000, 
- 45,189 in 2006, 
- 53,757 in 2011, 
- 61,014 in 2016, 
- 95,427 in 2021 and 
- 136,790 for the elections of 4 November 2026 [IC]. 

Ward candidates alone went from 
- 13,236 in 2000 to 
- 29,700 in 2011, 
- 61,111 in 2021 and 
- 98,219 in 2026 [IC].

Parties multiplied. 19 parties were on the national ballot in 1994, 48 in 2019 and 52 in 2024 [IC]. Parties contesting municipal elections rose from 
- 121 in 2011 to 
- 200 in 2016, about 
- 325 in 2021 and 
- 546 in 2026, out of 
- 732 registered in September 2026 [IC]. 

Party membership figures were not compiled; parties publish them inconsistently, and they would be [MS] in any case.

### E2. Elite positions

Managerial and professional employment grew fast before 2008, more slowly to 2019, and not at all after. The Labour Force Survey counted 664,000 managers in 2001 and the Quarterly Labour Force Survey 1,139,000 in 2012, annual growth of 5.0 percent against 1.8 percent for all employment (Bhorat, Goga and Stanwix 2014) [IC]. In the QLFS series, managers numbered 955,000 in the first quarter of 2008, 1,463,000 in the first quarter of 2019 and 1,459,000 in the second quarter of 2026; professionals numbered 744,000, 884,000 and 916,000 on the same dates (Statistics South Africa, P0211) [IC]. Managers and professionals together grew about 3.0 percent a year from 2008 to 2019 and about 0.2 percent a year from 2019 to 2026 [IC, computed for this brief]. The QLFS occupational series is volatile from quarter to quarter, and the 2001 figure comes from a different survey, so the growth rates carry caveats [TBV for the size of survey breaks].

The senior public service is small and flat. The Senior Management Service, created under a policy statement of August 2000 [IC], had 9,607 members at levels 13 to 16 on 30 June 2020, excluding Defence and State Security (Minister for the Public Service and Administration, reply to question NW1424, 2020) [IC], and 9,278 on 31 December 2023 (Business Day 2024) [IC]. An SMS series for 2001 to 2019 was not found [TBV].

High-paid public positions grew fast after 2013. National Treasury reports that the number of public servants earning above R1 million a year rose from just above 10,000 in 2013/14 to over 55,000 in 2023/24, and that average remuneration per public servant rose from R278,000 to R493,000 over the same years, mainly through above-inflation increases (National Treasury 2023) [IC]. Upper limits for full-time municipal office-bearers in 2024/25 ranged from R649,187 to R1,585,052 a year depending on office and municipal grade (Notice 5446 of 2024 under the Remuneration of Public Office Bearers Act) [IC].

Elected positions barely grew. Municipal wards numbered 4,277 in 2011, 4,392 in 2016, 4,468 in 2021 and 4,488 in 2026 [IC]. The 2021 elections filled 9,473 local and metropolitan seats before district seats were added, about 10,478 in all, and the 2026 elections fill 10,526 [IC]. The National Assembly has 400 seats throughout.

### E3. The ratio

Two ratios can be built from the data above. Neither measures Turchin's elite in full.

| Ratio | 2008 | 2011 | 2016 | 2019 | 2021 | 2023 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Degree holders per managerial or professional job | 0.67 | n.c. | n.c. | 0.82 | n.c. | 0.95 | n.c. |
| B. Ward candidates per municipal ward | n.a. | 6.9 | 7.8 | n.a. | 13.7 | n.a. | 21.9 |

n.c.: not compiled; n.a.: no election that year.

How each is built:

- **Ratio A** divides the weighted QLFS count of degree holders (MacGinty 2024) by QLFS managers plus professionals in the first quarter of the same year (P0211): 1.140 million over 1.699 million in 2008, 1.933 million over 2.347 million in 2019, 2.435 million over 2.574 million in 2023 [IC for the inputs; the ratio is computed for this brief]. It omits non-graduate aspirants, counts graduates who never sought elite posts (teachers and nurses among them), and treats every manager as elite. It shows direction; its level means little.
- **Ratio B** divides IEC ward candidates by the number of wards [IC for the inputs]. It is the cleanest count of people competing for a fixed set of positions. Its weakness is that each party usually fields one candidate per ward, so the ratio tracks the number of parties contesting as much as the number of individuals seeking office. Under the electoral rules, ward ballots also count toward each party's proportional share of seats [TBV for the size of that effect on the ratio].

No ratio could be built for 1994 to 2007, because graduate stocks and occupational counts on a consistent basis begin in 2008, and the 2000 and 2006 ward counts were not confirmed [TBV]. The expansion phase is visible in the growth of positions (managers at about 5 percent a year from 2001) and of enrolment, but its ratio cannot be measured.

## The predictions

### Q1. Aspirants outgrew positions after the expansion phase

**Prediction.** Elite positions expanded fast from 1994 and slowed with the economy after about 2008, while aspirants kept rising.

**Evidence for.** Managers grew about 5 percent a year in 2001 to 2012 [IC]. From 2008 degree holders grew about 5 percent a year while managerial and professional jobs grew about 3 percent a year to 2019 and barely at all after [IC]. Ratio A rose from 0.67 to 0.95 [structural inference]. Graduate unemployment doubled [IC]. Ward candidates per ward more than tripled from 2011 to 2026 while wards rose 5 percent [IC]. The slowdown in positions coincides with the 2008 break in the economic arc recorded in [[research/south-africa-on-the-curve|South Africa on the Curve]] [IC].

**Evidence against.** The expansion phase cannot be measured as a ratio, so the claim of a turn after 2008 rests on positions alone. Most of the fall in graduate employment reflects who the graduates are, which fits continued widening of access as much as a shortage of positions [IC]. Ratio B rises mainly through the number of parties [structural inference], and much of that rise came in 2021 and 2026, after the slowdown in positions had already lasted a decade.

**Grade.** Supported for 2008 to 2026, with caveats on measurement. Not testable for 1994 to 2007.

**Recommended status.** `academic-inference` for the component series; `structural-inference` for the ratios.

**Public essay.** Safe to say that from 2008 degree holders grew about twice as fast as managerial and professional jobs, that graduate unemployment doubled, and that candidates per ward more than tripled while wards barely changed, naming the sources.

### Q2. Intra-elite competition intensified, and followed Q1 in time

**Prediction.** Splits, contested selections, political killings and executive turnover rose after the aspirant-to-position ratio rose.

**Evidence for.** The Global Initiative Against Transnational Organized Crime recorded 488 political assassinations from 2000 to 2023 and finds that killings have risen steadily since around 2010, with local councillors the majority of victims and peaks of 42 in 2019 and 30 in 2021 (Matamba and Thobela 2024, not peer-reviewed) [IC]. The Moerane Commission, reporting on 21 September 2018 on political killings in KwaZulu-Natal, found access to tenders to be the root cause and called on parties to answer for violent competition among their members for positions [IC]. In 2022 the IEC's deputy chief executive told Parliament that the 2021 elections showed "increased intra and inter-party rivalries that play out in the electoral process" [IC]. The number of parties winning National Assembly seats rose from 7 in 1994 to 14 in 2019 and 18 in 2024, and the governing party's seats fell from 252 to 159 [IC]. Parties formed by former members of existing parties include COPE (2008) and the EFF (2013) from the ANC and the NFP (2011) from the IFP [IC], and GOOD (2018), ActionSA (2020), BOSA (2022) and the MK party (2023), led by former office-holders of the DA or the ANC [TBV against the IEC register]. Since the 2021 local elections, 15 of 71 [[municipality|municipalities]] governed by coalitions have had three or more mayors, four of them metros (The Outlier 2024, data journalism) [TBV, one source]; Johannesburg has had nine mayors and eight coalition administrations since 2016 (Centre for Development and Enterprise, reported September 2026) [IC]. In 2026 candidate selection reached the courts, through a party's challenge over candidates excluded from the ballot and councillors' challenges to their own party's lists [IC for the filings; the councillors' claims are untested].

**Evidence against.** Splits began long before the ratio rose: the UDM in 1997 and the Independent Democrats in 2003 [IC for the founding years; TBV for their origins]. The GI-TOC dates the rise in killings to factional conflict after the governing party's 2007 national conference, the same year the ratio series begins, so the two move together and neither leads [IC for the dating]. Political violence in KwaZulu-Natal predates 1994 [TBV for its scale]. Metropolitan coalition churn follows from voters fragmenting the vote in 2016 and 2021.

**Grade.** Intensification supported. The sequence the theory predicts, ratio first and competition after, is not established.

**Recommended status.** `academic-inference` for intensification; `working-hypothesis` for the sequence.

**Public essay.** Safe to say that every measure of competition within the political class compiled here rose after about 2008. Do not say the competition followed from overproduction.

### Q3. The state became the contested channel

**Prediction.** With private-sector elite positions growing slowly, competition concentrated on public office and procurement, and intensified as fiscal room narrowed.

**Evidence for.** The scholarship agrees on the first half. Southall identifies the governing party, acting as a "party-state," as the main force behind the new black middle class (Southall 2016) [IC]. Von Holdt argues that because the constitution limits redistribution of private assets, elite struggle moved into the state, "the key site" of black economic empowerment, and took violent forms (von Holdt 2013) [IC]. Beresford finds that "access to channels of public authority shapes patterns of elite accumulation" and that councillor posts carry influence over local contracts and jobs (Beresford 2015) [IC]. The Moerane Commission and the GI-TOC both trace killings to competition for positions and municipal contracts [IC]. The [[sources/state-capture-commission-report|State Capture Commission]] put state spending tainted by state capture at about R57 billion, more than 97 percent of it at the state power and freight utilities [IC]. Public servants earning above R1 million a year rose about fivefold in the decade to 2023/24 [IC]. The Auditor-General reported R40 billion of irregular expenditure in municipalities in 2024-25 and said "the trend is still upwards" (The Citizen 2026) [IC].

**Evidence against.** The second half is not shown. The public wage bill fell as a share of consolidated spending, from 35.7 percent in 2013/14 to 32.1 percent in 2022/23 [IC], but the count of high-paid public positions kept rising. The state's elite positions did not shrink as debt rose. Fiscal room narrowed, yet the years of most intense contest for state resources (2009 to 2018) were years of deficit-financed spending growth [IC for the debt path; structural inference for the reading]. Private-sector management is also contested territory, through empowerment ownership deals that von Holdt describes as leaving black partners dependent on the governing party [IC], so the line between public and private routes is blurred.

**Grade.** Supported that the state is the main contested channel. Not supported that competition intensified because fiscal room narrowed.

**Recommended status.** `academic-inference` for the state as channel; `working-hypothesis` for the fiscal link.

**Public essay.** Safe to say that South African scholars and two official inquiries trace competition for office and contracts to the state's role as the main route into the elite. Do not tie it to the fiscal squeeze without caveat.

### Q4. Overproduction adds explanatory power

**Prediction.** The pattern is better explained with elite overproduction than by patronage, clientelism or factionalism accounts alone.

**Evidence for.** The patronage literature describes the gate; the ratio counts the people at it. Candidates per ward tripling while wards stay fixed, and graduates outgrowing managerial jobs, are supply-side pressures the patronage accounts imply and do not measure.

**Evidence against.** Lodge (2014), Beresford (2015), von Holdt (2013), Chipkin, Swilling and colleagues (Bhorat et al. 2017) and the official inquiries explain the same splits, killings, procurement contests and turnover through rents, gatekeeping, factional control of the state and criminal networks, with documented mechanisms and dates [IC]. Their explanations hold even if the number of aspirants had stayed constant: a fixed number of people fighting over a fixed set of rents produces the same evidence once the rents rise or the gatekeepers change. Lodge finds that neo-patrimonial practice in the governing party arises from "the confluence of these trends": habits from before and during exile, links to criminal networks, and the pressures of electoral politics [IC]. On the theory's own formula, rising top incomes lower the elite mobilization score: the top 1 percent's pre-tax income rose about 50 percent from 1993 to 2019 (Chatterjee, Czajka and Gethin 2021) [IC], which offsets part of the rise in elite numbers.

**Grade.** Weak. "Elite overproduction" largely relabels a phenomenon South African political economy already describes, and adds one quantitative dimension.

**Recommended status.** `structural-inference`.

**Public essay.** With caveat: the ratio adds a measurement to the patronage accounts and does not replace them.

### The combined test

From 2008 to 2021 all three pressures rose together. Unemployment rose and stood at 33.6 percent in mid-2026; public debt rose from 26.0 to 78.9 percent of GDP; and both aspirant ratios rose [IC for each series]. Immiseration is the weakest of the three: the poverty rate fell from 46.7 percent in 2015 to 37.9 percent in 2023 and life expectancy rose about twelve years from its 2005 low, as [[research/south-africa-on-the-curve|South Africa on the Curve]] records [IC].

The July 2021 unrest is the episode a reader would test. The Expert Panel found that "the persistent levels of poverty, served to provide the ripe environment" for the violence, that "the instability within the governing party" compounded problems in crime intelligence, and that there was "a significant intelligence failure to anticipate, prevent or disrupt the planned and orchestrated violence" ([[sources/expert-panel-july-2021-civil-unrest|Expert Panel 2022]]) [IC]. The South African Human Rights Commission found that "organised groups and individuals opportunistically exploited" conditions that included "high unemployment," and that it "did not receive clear evidence identifying specific groups or individuals as primary actors" (SAHRC 2024) [IC]. Those findings name mass hardship, division in the governing party and organized instigation, the three elements the theory combines. One episode fitted after the fact is the kind of reading the theory's critics call retrofitting.

**Grade.** Consistent with the theory, and untestable from one episode.

**Recommended status.** `speculative-projection` for any forward reading; `working-hypothesis` for the fit with 2021.

**Public essay.** No, except as a question.

## Evidence the reading must face

**Elite exit.** Emigration does not offset aspirant growth. United Nations migrant-stock data show South African-born residents abroad rising from about 330,000 in 1990 to about 900,000 in 2017, near 21,000 a year, and destination-country statistics give a similar rate (as summarized in Buckham 2019, an opinion article) [IC for the underlying series]. That outflow, across all skill levels, is small beside the 1.3 million rise in degree holders from 2008 to 2023. New World Wealth estimates that dollar millionaires living in South Africa fell from about 46,800 in 2014 to about 37,400 in 2024, and Henley & Partners estimate a net outflow of about 250 millionaires in 2024 (BusinessTech 2025) [TBV: both firms publish weak methods and neither is peer-reviewed]. Reported SARS data show 569,351 people earning above R1 million in 2024 [TBV], so exit at the very top coexists with growth in high incomes below it.

**Expansion from a racially capped base.** The growth in enrolment, in managers and in the high-paid public service after 1994 includes the entry of people previously barred. MacGinty's finding that composition explains most of the fall in graduate employment is consistent with this. The ratios above are built after 2008, when the cap had been gone for fourteen years, and they measure the whole pool.

**Patronage accounts.** Reported under Q4.

**Small numbers.** The positions that carry real power are few: about 9,300 senior public managers, about 10,500 municipal seats, 400 National Assembly seats, and 3,500 individuals in the top 0.01 percent of wealth holders, who hold about 15 percent of household net worth (Chatterjee, Czajka and Gethin 2022) [IC]. Ratio A measures pressure on a broad professional stratum, as Turchin's American measures (law graduates, decamillionaires) do; only ratio B measures pressure on office.

**Critiques of the theory.** A peer-reviewed test for industrialized societies found that with empirical proxies for elite numbers and incomes the Political Stress Indicator for the United States stays roughly flat from 1960 to 2020, that the model's variables explain about 18 percent of the variance in instability, and that "the model can be used for understanding rather than forecasting" (Georgescu 2023) [IC]. Mounk, citing Fukuyama, argues that Turchin "is able to detect elite overproduction in so many historical eras because he has a flexible definition of 'elite'" (Mounk 2024, not peer-reviewed) [IC for the argument as published]. A study of Poland found the Polish case could be explained "without any mention of elite overproduction" (Stachowiak and Pasek 2025) [IC]. These critiques apply here: ratio A depends on a broad definition of elite, and the 2021 fit is retrospective.

**Other applications.** Korotayev and Zinkina's analysis of Egypt's 2011 revolution used the size of the youth cohort and the education of the unemployed, finding that more than 43 percent of the unemployed held university degrees (Korotayev and Zinkina 2011) [IC]. The method transfers to South Africa, and the result differs. In South Africa graduate unemployment, at 12.4 percent, is about a third of the national rate [IC], so graduates are a small share of the unemployed and the mass of the unemployed are not frustrated elite aspirants [structural inference]. Goldstone's comparative work, which links state breakdown to "elite divisions and fiscal crises" (Goldstone 2016) [IC], supports reading the pressures together.

**Explanations left aside.** This brief does not test institutional-design accounts in the tradition of Acemoglu and Robinson, the literature on electoral-system incentives and party fragmentation, accounts of organized crime and the taxi industry as independent drivers of political killings, labor-market accounts of graduate unemployment centered on skills mismatch and the quality of qualifications, or the comparative literature on dominant-party decline. Each could explain part of the same evidence.
