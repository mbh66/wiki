When people who coordinate through different forms sit down to govern something together, they read the same facts through different lenses. This brief asks whether a person's orientation toward the four [[frameworks/time-framework|TIME]] forms can be measured, so that a facilitator knows which coordination logics are present at the table. No validated instrument exists. The most credible path is a domain-by-form profile: continuous scores for T, I, M and E within each domain a group governs (land, water, money, family, public office, religion, or whatever the group's own agenda holds). It should be piloted first as an unscored facilitation tool built on Q-methodology card sorts. A scored instrument is worth building only where a group wants one and can meet its jurisdiction's legal and psychometric requirements.

A costed application of this brief to a specific forum in the Western Cape, South Africa, sits at the Valley of Grace BioHub: [TIME Profile: Application to the Ward 2 Development Forum](https://at12-vog.bioconomy.earth/research/time-profile-w2df).

## TL;DR

- **No TIME profile exists.** [[ronfeldt-david|David Ronfeldt]] has proposed no individual assessment, and none was found in the literature citing him. The nearest measured neighbor is [[fiske-alan|Alan Page Fiske]]'s Relational Models Theory: its communal sharing, authority ranking and market pricing map strongly onto T, I and M. No existing instrument has a good counterpart for E.
- **Measure a profile; never assign a type.** Taxometric research finds that normal personality variation is dimensional. MBTI-style labels are unstable near their cut points. Relational Models research shows people combine models within a single relationship. A profile that varies by domain also makes "of course she says that, she's an M" harder to say.
- **Start with an unscored Q-sort.** Q-methodology has a track record in deliberation research for making shared viewpoints visible without scoring individuals. Scoring named individuals raises data-protection questions in most jurisdictions, because the answers can reveal beliefs and political opinion.
- **The comparison exposes two gaps in TIME.** There is no place for the fatalist position (people who experience themselves as shut out of every form of coordination), and equality matching (turn-taking and balanced reciprocity) has no settled home between T and E.

## Why a profile

The question comes from applying the [[concepts/substrate-hypothesis|Substrate Hypothesis]] to deliberation. [[argyris-chris|Chris Argyris]]'s [[ladder-of-inference|ladder of inference]] describes how a person climbs from observable data through selected data, added meaning, assumptions and conclusions to beliefs and action, with a reflexive loop in which beliefs decide which data gets selected next time. Two further elements adapt the ladder for governance:

- **[[lens|Lens]]:** the collective interpretive frame a person inherits by belonging to a TIME form, a community, a profession or a history. It works on the lower rungs, where data is selected and given meaning. People who share a lens rarely see it.
- **[[self-talk|Self-talk]]:** the individual's private narrative about what is happening, who is responsible and what comes next. It works on the upper rungs, where cognitive biases operate, and it carries the reflexive loop.

The coordination substrate sits beneath both. Price, for example, decides which data reaches anyone at all before any lens is applied.

Each older TIME form carries a built-in tie-breaker. In the [[t-form-tribal|T form]] the elder settles it; in the [[i-form-institutional|I form]], the office-holder; in the [[m-form-market|M form]], price. The [[e-form-emergent|E form]] has none. When perspectives meet as equals, disagreement can only be resolved by working through the layers, and knowing which lenses are present becomes a governance need. A TIME profile is one candidate tool for that job.

The ladder of inference is Argyris's. Lens and self-talk entered the model in a Zoom dialogue between Michael Haupt and [[keller-michael|Michael Keller]] (Ecology of Design in Human Systems) on 25 September 2026. The lens and self-talk elements as developed here, their placement on the ladder and their use as a governance model are the author's construction (working hypothesis).

## Attribution

Ronfeldt wrote in October 2025 that he had "wondered whether 'equinets' or 'exonets' might be more accurate than plain 'networks'," so that "the acronym TIMN would aptly become TIME." He did not insist on the change. His E therefore stands for exonets (outward-reaching networks) or equinets, offered tentatively; the word "Emergent" does not appear in that post. The reading of E as Emergent used across this wiki, the pairing of TIME with the ladder, lens and self-talk, and any profile instrument built on them are Michael Haupt's constructs. Mappings of TIME onto other instruments in this brief are the author's inferences and are labeled as such.

## 1. Does a TIME profile exist?

No (supported). Searches of Ronfeldt's RAND papers, his *Visions from Two Theories* blog, his Substack and the practitioner literature citing TIMN found no person-level assessment. Ronfeldt's work is societal and evolutionary. It describes forms that grow, over history, into realm-defining organization and belief, and his comparisons are with other four-form theories of social change (Bauwens's peer-to-peer model, Karatani's modes of exchange, Raworth's doughnut economics). None of these works measures individuals.

## 3. Mapping the instruments onto TIME

Every rating in this section is the author's inference (speculative unless noted).

### Relational Models Theory

| Model | TIME | Fit | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communal sharing | T | Strong | A bounded group whose members are treated as equivalent; mirrors kinship and belonging. |
| Authority ranking | I | Strong | Linear ordering of rank and precedence; mirrors hierarchy and legitimacy. |
| Market pricing | M | Strong | Ratio-based exchange; a direct counterpart. |
| Equality matching | T or E | Partial | Turn-taking and balanced reciprocity. Irrigation turn systems, in which each property holds a timed turn on a shared furrow, are equality matching inside a T-form community. Peer-to-peer parity is equality matching in E. |

| Null / asocial | (none) | Gap in TIME | TIME has no category for non-coordination. |

### Spiral Dynamics / Graves

| Value system | TIME | Fit |
|---|---|---|
| Purple (tribal) | T | Strong at face level |
| Red (power) | (none) | Gap; closest is a degraded I |
| Blue (order) | I | Strong at face level |

The face-level fit is high, and misleading. Spiral Dynamics orders its systems as developmental stages. In Ronfeldt's usage the forms coexist and accumulate. Importing the stage order would import a ranking of persons, which is corrosive in a divided room. See [[graves-clare|Clare Graves]] and [[sources/beck-cowan-spiral-dynamics|Beck and Cowan]].

## 4. What the comparison reveals about TIME

- **The fatalist gap.** Grid-group theory has a position for people who experience rules as imposed and groups as closed to them. TIME does not. In places shaped by dispossession or classification, this may be the most common lens in the room. A profile that cannot register it will misread those participants as low scorers on every form.
- **Equality matching.** Turn-taking and balanced reciprocity are old (T-form irrigation turns) and new (E-form peer parity). Either TIME treats equality matching as spanning both forms, or the profile needs a separate reciprocity scale.
- **E as configuration.** If E is a way of combining the other logics across group boundaries, a profile might need three form scales plus a bridging index, with no fourth scale of the same kind.
- **Forms coexist.** TIME's cumulative logic (each form adds to the earlier ones) fits a profile that expects everyone to hold every form somewhere. Stage models do not fit.

## 5. Design recommendations

### 5.1 State-like and domain-sensitive (supported)

Zakharin and Bates (2023) write that "a given human relationship typically reflects combinations of two or more relational models": a family can be mostly communal sharing while children also defer to parents, share chores and get paid for some of them. They also found a general factor associated with relationship closeness, loading positively on communal sharing, equality matching and market pricing and negatively on authority ranking. Closeness to the other party therefore shifts the whole profile.

The same holds for coordination forms. One farmer may treat irrigation water as membership, as a licensed allocation and as a priced input, depending on the setting.

**Design:** a domain-by-form matrix. For each domain the group governs, four continuous scores. The group chooses its domains; the profile is reported as a pattern across domains, never as a single letter.

### 5.2 Dimensional over typological (established)

- Haslam, Holland and Kuppens (2012) reviewed 177 articles, 311 findings and 533,377 participants. After correcting for method weaknesses they estimated the true prevalence of taxonic (categorical) findings at 14%, and concluded that most latent variables in personality and clinical psychology are dimensional.
- Pittenger (1993, 2005) documented MBTI type reclassification on retest, concentrated among people scoring near the cut points. Defenders note that continuous preference scores are more stable. Both sides agree the instability comes from cutting a continuum into categories.
- McCrae and Costa (1989) showed that MBTI scales correlate with four of the Big Five factors, which means MBTI's useful information is dimensional.

**Design:** continuous scores per domain, shown as bars or a radar with confidence bands. No cut points, no letter codes.

### 5.3 The label becomes a lens (speculative to supported)

The risk that a type label turns into a new lens has not been tested directly with coordination forms. The adjacent evidence points one way:

- Wu, Hao, Zeng and Du (2024; 469 Chinese young adults) found that using MBTI as a social label raised social anxiety through ego identity and impression management. People felt pressure to perform their type.
- Siy and colleagues (2025, CSCW) found that four-letter labels on social media profiles help people understand one another and can also reinforce stereotypes in first impressions.
- Ross and Ward (1996) describe naive realism: people take their own view as objective and explain disagreement as the other side's bias. A type label hands that explanation a vocabulary. Reactive devaluation (Ross and Stillinger 1991) predicts that proposals from a labeled "other" get discounted. The ultimate attribution error (Pettigrew 1979) predicts that a rival group's good moves get credited to circumstance and its bad moves to its nature, or its "type."

**Design:** frame every item and every report as "which logic you think this domain calls for." Never frame it as "what kind of person you are." A profile that shows everyone holding every form somewhere is hard to weaponize.

### 5.4 Item formats

| Format | Strengths | Weaknesses | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Likert | Simple; normative scores; standard reliability statistics | Acquiescence and social desirability; literacy load; agree/disagree anchors drift in translation | Use for a scored instrument, with balanced keying |
| Forced-choice, ipsative | Resists faking; mirrors real trade-offs | Classical scoring makes all-high or all-low profiles impossible and invalidates alpha (Brown and Maydeu-Olivares 2011, 2013) | Avoid classical scoring. Thurstonian IRT recovers normative scores but needs large samples and has limits of its own (Bürkner, Schulte and Holling 2019). |
| Situational judgment scenarios | Contextual; locally written (a boundary dispute, a missed water turn, a public tender) | Hard to score, since no form is correct; items load on several forms | Best for domain items: score which response the person favors, per form |
| Q-methodology | Built for subjective viewpoints; works with 20 to 60 people; statements drawn from local speech; can be administered orally or with pictures | Finds shared viewpoints; produces no individual trait scores; needs a skilled analyst | Best first tool |

## 6. Unscored alternatives

A group can make lenses visible without scoring anyone. Four tools have evidence behind them or behind their mechanism:

1. **Q-sort rounds.** Participants rank statements about a live issue. By-person factor analysis reveals the shared viewpoints, and the group often sees that viewpoints cut across the lines it expected. Niemeyer and Dryzek (2007) proposed meta-consensus, agreement about the nature of an issue that need not extend to the outcome, as an ideal outcome of deliberation, and used Q-methodology to measure it. Niemeyer's (2011) mini-public studies found that deliberation increased the consistency between participants' reasons and their preferences (supported).
2. **Issue-by-issue self-placement.** For each agenda item, each person privately marks which form should govern it (T, I, M, E or a mix). The aggregate is shown without names. Domain variation becomes visible, and no individual profile is stored.
3. **Acknowledgment rounds.** Sherman, Nelson and Ross (2003) found that "hearing the other side explicate their own position did nothing to attenuate the false polarization effect," while hearing them acknowledge arguments from the opposing side that they found at least somewhat persuasive "significantly decreased" it (supported). A round in which each person names the strongest point from another form's logic applies the finding directly.
4. **Teaching the bias.** Nasie, Bar-Tal, Pliskin, Nahhas and Halperin (2014) found that teaching Jewish Israeli participants about naive realism increased openness to the Palestinian narrative among hawkish participants, who were also better able to recognize the bias in themselves (supported, with that moderation). Teaching the ladder, lens and self-talk alongside any lens tool may help the least open participants most.

The Lewis Method of Deep Democracy, developed in South Africa by Myrna and Greg Lewis, points the same direction from practice. It surfaces minority positions and unspoken objections in the room without typing anyone. Its evidence base is case studies (speculative for outcomes).

## 7. Development pathway

A scored instrument would follow the *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (AERA, APA and NCME 2014), which treat validity as an argument about a specific use. Here the use is facilitation, so the evidence that matters most is consequential: whether the profile reduces or increases misattribution at the table. No existing instrument supplies that evidence.

| Phase | Goal | Main activities | Indicative sample |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Construct definition | Define T, I, M and E at the level of a person in a domain | Construct map anchored to Ronfeldt's texts and Relational Models Theory; the group agrees its domain list | n/a |
| 1. Unscored Q-sort pilot | Make lenses visible without scoring anyone | Statements drawn from the group's own minutes, interviews and texts in each working language; sorts before and after a deliberation round; by-person factor analysis | 20 to 40 |
| 2. Item generation and review | Draft domain-by-form items | Expert review; translation and back-translation; cognitive interviews across literacy levels | 6 to 10 experts; 15 to 30 interviews per language |
| 3. Minimal credible version | Items hang together and are stable | Exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis; reliability; 2 to 4 week retest; convergent validity against the revised MORQ and cultural cognition short forms; discriminant validity against a Big Five short form | 250 to 400 |
| 4. Fully validated version | Fair comparison across languages; prediction of meeting behavior | Multi-group invariance testing (configural, metric, scalar); coded meeting behavior; technical manual | 200+ per language group |

Phases 0 and 1 cost little and answer the prior question: does the group want a scored instrument at all, or a better conversation? The sample sizes are the author's estimates from common psychometric practice.

## 8. Safeguards for governance use

### 8.1 How a label feeds each bias, and how a domain profile can counter it

| Bias | A type label feeds it | A domain profile can counter it |
|---|---|---|
| Naive realism | "I see the issue as it is; she sees it as an M." Disagreement gets explained away. | Everyone's profile shows mixed forms; the facilitator asks which form a person is using for *this* domain. |
| Reactive devaluation | Proposals from the "M camp" are discounted before they are heard. | Blind proposal rounds: rate the proposal before revealing who tabled it. |
| Ultimate attribution error | A rival group's generosity is situational; its obstruction is its nature. | Forms are situational by design; individual profiles stay private to the individual. |

Naming a lens helps when it comes with self-application (Nasie et al. 2014) and acknowledgment (Sherman et al. 2003). It harms when it is used to classify others (speculative inference from these studies).

### 8.2 Safeguards in rooms with power asymmetries

1. No individual scores at the table. Plenary sees only aggregated, anonymous patterns; an individual's profile goes only to that individual.
2. Self-application first. Participants map their own forms before discussing anyone else's.
3. Forms describe arguments and domains, never persons. This is a facilitation ground rule.
4. Participation is voluntary and revocable, and refusal carries no consequence.
5. The facilitator names power asymmetries between lenses, and welcomes the fatalist position as a legitimate lens.
6. Any data is held by a party with no stake in the decisions the group is making.
7. Avoid collecting belief-related data in the run-up to elections.

### 8.3 Jurisdiction checklist

Anyone adapting this brief should check five things locally:

- **Data protection.** Many regimes treat data revealing political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or racial or ethnic origin as a special category (for example, GDPR Article 9 in the European Union and POPIA section 26 in South Africa). A named, scored TIME profile plausibly reveals beliefs and political opinion, and T-form items on kinship and identity may reveal ethnic origin. An unscored, anonymous Q-sort avoids most of these obligations.
- **Regulation of psychological testing.** Some jurisdictions reserve personality assessment to registered psychologists. A low correlation with Big Five traits strengthens the case that a TIME profile measures views on governance and falls outside that reservation.
- **Employment law**, if an employer uses the tool on its own staff.
- **Language.** Scores can only be compared across language groups after invariance testing. The SAPI (Fetvadjiev et al. 2015) shows how to build items from local speech in several languages at once.
- **History.** Anywhere a population has lived under state classification, a tool that sorts people into four labeled groups risks being read as a new classification scheme.

## Open questions

- Is E an elementary form at the person level, or a configuration of the other three? The answer decides between four scales and three scales plus a bridging index.
- Should TIME, or at least the profile, include a fatalist or excluded position?
- Does equality matching belong to T, to E, or to both?
- Can predictive validity for meeting behavior be established without recording and coding meetings, which raises its own consent and power questions?
- Would Ronfeldt accept "Emergent" as a reading of his "exonets," and how should the wiki show both terms?
- What does a given group actually want: a scored instrument, or a better conversation?

### Organizational culture

- Cameron, K. S., and Quinn, R. E. (2011). *Diagnosing and Changing Organizational Culture: Based on the Competing Values Framework* (3rd ed.). Jossey-Bass. The OCAI and its clan, hierarchy, market and adhocracy types. The most widely used four-form instrument, and it works at organization level. [[sources/cameron-quinn-organizational-culture|Source entry]].
- Heritage, B., Pollock, C., and Roberts, L. (2014). "Validation of the Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument." *PLOS ONE*, 9(3), e92879. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0092879. A validation study showing both the OCAI's usefulness and the limits its ipsative format imposes.
- Hartnell, C. A., Ou, A. Y., and Kinicki, A. (2011). "Organizational culture and organizational effectiveness: A meta-analytic investigation of the competing values framework's theoretical suppositions." *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 96(4), 677-694. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021987. A meta-analysis across 84 studies. Shows what a mature evidence base for a four-form instrument looks like.

### Values, morals and development

- Schwartz, S. H. (2012). "An overview of the Schwartz theory of basic values." *Online Readings in Psychology and Culture*, 2(1). https://doi.org/10.9707/2307-0919.1116. The circumplex of personal values. Shows why values cut across coordination forms.
- Schwartz, S. H., and Cieciuch, J. (2022). "Measuring the refined theory of individual values in 49 cultural groups: Psychometrics of the revised Portrait Value Questionnaire." *Assessment*, 29(5), 1005-1019. https://doi.org/10.1177/1073191121998760. The benchmark for cross-cultural measurement a TIME profile would eventually need to meet.
- Graham, J., Nosek, B. A., Haidt, J., Iyer, R., Koleva, S., and Ditto, P. H. (2011). "Mapping the moral domain." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 101(2), 366-385. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021847. The Moral Foundations Questionnaire. Its loyalty and authority foundations partly track T and I.
- [[sources/graves-levels-of-existence|Graves, C. W. Levels of existence]]. The developmental value systems behind Spiral Dynamics. Relevant here as a caution against ranking forms.
- [[sources/beck-cowan-spiral-dynamics|Beck, D. E., and Cowan, C. C. (1996). *Spiral Dynamics*]]. Popularized Graves as a color-coded stage model. Its face-level fit with TIME comes with a hierarchy of persons attached.
- [[sources/kegan-in-over-our-heads|Kegan, R. (1994). *In Over Our Heads*]]. Orders of consciousness. Measures complexity of meaning-making, which is a different construct from coordination form.
- [[sources/cook-greuter-developmental-perspective|Cook-Greuter, S. Developmental perspective]]. Ego-development stages measured by sentence completion. Same caution as Kegan.

### Typology and psychometrics

- Haslam, N., Holland, E., and Kuppens, P. (2012). "Categories versus dimensions in personality and psychopathology: A quantitative review of taxometric research." *Psychological Medicine*, 42(5), 903-920. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0033291711001966. Estimates true taxonic findings at 14%. The main evidence for a dimensional TIME profile. [[sources/haslam-holland-kuppens-2012-categories-dimensions|Source entry]].
- Pittenger, D. J. (1993). "The utility of the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator." *Review of Educational Research*, 63(4), 467-488. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543063004467. Documents type instability on retest. The cautionary tale for any four-letter scheme.
- Pittenger, D. J. (2005). "Cautionary comments regarding the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator." *Consulting Psychology Journal*, 57(3), 210-221. https://doi.org/10.1037/1065-9293.57.3.210. Updates the critique for practitioners who use MBTI with teams.
- McCrae, R. R., and Costa, P. T. (1989). "Reinterpreting the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator from the perspective of the five-factor model of personality." *Journal of Personality*, 57(1), 17-40. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6494.1989.tb00759.x. Shows MBTI's information is dimensional. Supports the discriminant check against the Big Five.
- Brown, A., and Maydeu-Olivares, A. (2011). "Item response modeling of forced-choice questionnaires." *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 71(3), 460-502. https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164410375112. The Thurstonian IRT solution to ipsative scoring. Relevant if the profile uses trade-off items.
- Brown, A., and Maydeu-Olivares, A. (2013). "How IRT can solve problems of ipsative data in forced-choice questionnaires." *Psychological Methods*, 18(1), 36-52. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030641. Sets out the distortions of classical ipsative scoring that the OCAI inherits.
- Bürkner, P.-C., Schulte, N., and Holling, H. (2019). "On the statistical and practical limitations of Thurstonian IRT models." *Educational and Psychological Measurement*, 79(5), 827-854. https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164419832063. The counterweight: Thurstonian scoring needs conditions a small forum cannot supply.
- American Educational Research Association, American Psychological Association, and National Council on Measurement in Education. (2014). *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing*. AERA. Treats validity as an argument about a use. Frames the consequential evidence this brief asks for.
- Fetvadjiev, V. H., Meiring, D., van de Vijver, F. J. R., Nel, J. A., and Hill, C. (2015). "The South African Personality Inventory (SAPI): A culture-informed instrument for the country's main ethnocultural groups." *Psychological Assessment*, 27(3), 827-837. https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000078. Built from interviews in 11 languages and replicated across groups. The model for developing items from local speech. [[sources/fetvadjiev-2015-sapi|Source entry]].
- Wu, W., Hao, W., Zeng, G., and Du, W. (2024). "From personality types to social labels: The impact of using MBTI on social anxiety among Chinese youth." *Frontiers in Psychology*, 15, 1419492. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1419492. Evidence that type labels create pressure to perform the type. Supports the label-becomes-lens concern.
- Siy, et al. (2025). "One MBTI does not fit all: Perceptions and usage of MBTI in social media profiles." *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* (CSCW). arXiv:2409.15293. https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15293. Finds type labels both aid understanding and reinforce stereotypes in first impressions.

### Bias, deliberation and facilitation

- Keller, M., and Haupt, M. (2026, September 25). Zoom dialogue on the ladder of inference, lens and self-talk. Unpublished conversation. Michael Keller is co-founder of Ecology of Design in Human Systems: https://www.ecologyofdesigninhumansystems.com/about-us/about-michael/. The dialogue in which lens and self-talk were named as additions to the ladder.
- Argyris, C. (1990). *Overcoming Organizational Defenses*. Allyn and Bacon. The ladder of inference and the reasoning habits that seal beliefs against evidence. The base model this brief extends with lens and self-talk.
- Senge, P., Kleiner, A., Roberts, C., Ross, R., and Smith, B. (1994). *The Fifth Discipline Fieldbook*. Currency Doubleday. The version of the ladder most practitioners know, with the left-hand column exercise.
- Ross, L., and Ward, A. (1996). "Naive realism in everyday life: Implications for social conflict and misunderstanding." In E. S. Reed, E. Turiel and T. Brown (Eds.), *Values and Knowledge* (pp. 103-135). Erlbaum. Defines naive realism. Explains why a type label would give disagreement a ready-made explanation. [[sources/ross-ward-1996-naive-realism|Source entry]].
- Ross, L., and Stillinger, C. (1991). "Barriers to conflict resolution." *Negotiation Journal*, 7(4), 389-404. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1571-9979.1991.tb00634.x. Reactive devaluation. The case for blind proposal rounds.
- Pettigrew, T. F. (1979). "The ultimate attribution error: Extending Allport's cognitive analysis of prejudice." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 5(4), 461-476. https://doi.org/10.1177/014616727900500407. The group-level attribution bias a TIME label could feed.
- Sherman, D. K., Nelson, L. D., and Ross, L. D. (2003). "Naive realism and affirmative action: Adversaries are more similar than they think." *Basic and Applied Social Psychology*, 25(4), 275-289. https://doi.org/10.1207/S15324834BASP2504_2. Acknowledging the other side's persuasive arguments reduces false polarization; explaining one's own position does not. The basis for acknowledgment rounds. [[sources/sherman-nelson-ross-2003-naive-realism|Source entry]].
- Nasie, M., Bar-Tal, D., Pliskin, R., Nahhas, E., and Halperin, E. (2014). "Overcoming the barrier of narrative adherence in conflicts through awareness of the psychological bias of naive realism." *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 40(11), 1543-1556. https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214551153. Teaching the bias opened the least open participants to the adversary's narrative. Supports teaching lens and self-talk alongside any tool. [[sources/nasie-2014-naive-realism-narrative|Source entry]].
- Niemeyer, S., and Dryzek, J. S. (2007). "The ends of deliberation: Meta-consensus and inter-subjective rationality as ideal outcomes." *Swiss Political Science Review*, 13(4), 497-526. https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1662-6370.2007.tb00087.x. Meta-consensus as a deliberative goal, measured with Q. The theoretical case for the unscored pilot. [[sources/niemeyer-dryzek-2007-ends-of-deliberation|Source entry]].
- Niemeyer, S. (2011). "The emancipatory effect of deliberation: Empirical lessons from mini-publics." *Politics and Society*, 39(1), 103-140. https://doi.org/10.1177/0032329210395000. Evidence that deliberation aligns reasons with preferences. What a Q-sort before and after deliberation would track.
- Stephenson, W. (1953). *The Study of Behavior: Q-Technique and Its Methodology*. University of Chicago Press. The origin of Q-methodology.
- Watts, S., and Stenner, P. (2012). *Doing Q Methodological Research: Theory, Method and Interpretation*. Sage. The practical manual for running the pilot.
- Lewis, M., with Woodhull, J. (2008). *Inside the No: Five Steps to Decisions That Last*. The Lewis Method of Deep Democracy. A South African practice that surfaces positions in a room without typing people.

### Law

- Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (General Data Protection Regulation), Article 9. Special categories of personal data, including political opinions and religious or philosophical beliefs. The reference point for the data-protection check outside South Africa.
- Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013 (South Africa), sections 26 and 27. Prohibits processing special personal information, including religious or philosophical beliefs and political persuasion, except under listed authorizations. The South African counterpart; applied in detail on the BioHub page.
