Transitions research explains well how protection for young initiatives is made and argued for; its evidence that protection leads to lasting survival or wider change comes mostly from single studies of initiatives that survived, and the largest comparative studies find that resources, context and the terms of exit matter as much as the shelter itself.

## Overview

New practices and organisations often fail early because they meet the full weight of the existing system before they're ready: its prices, rules, measures and expectations. Transitions research studies long changes in how societies meet needs such as energy, food and transport. It calls the protected conditions in which something new can develop a [[glossary/m-s/niche|niche]], or a [[glossary/m-s/protective-space|protective space]], and since 1998 it has built a detailed account of how such spaces are made, what happens inside them and how they end.

The central model comes from Adrian Smith and Rob Raven (2012). [[glossary/m-s/shielding|Shielding]] holds back the harshest pressures. [[glossary/m-s/nurturing|Nurturing]] helps the initiative learn, build networks and form shared expectations. [[glossary/a-e/empowerment|Empowerment]] prepares it to survive outside, either by becoming competitive on existing terms or by changing those terms [IC]. Grassroots innovation research applies these ideas to community groups, co-operatives and movements, and finds that they fit only in part [IC].

Most studies follow one initiative, usually one that lasted, and several were written by the people who ran the work. Two bodies of work compare many cases. A survey of 276 Transition initiatives in 23 countries, active and inactive, found that less successful groups tended to underestimate context and material resources [IC]. A ten-year matched comparison of 371 firms from five German business incubators found no site where incubated firms survived longer, and three where they survived less well [IC].

For a bioregional forum or [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]], the result is modest and usable. Protect openly, and write the terms down. Tend the network as carefully as the budget. Monitor in ways that help the group learn. Agree in advance when and how exposure begins. The research gives no reason to think protection must be hidden to work.

## The distinctions

- **Niche, regime and landscape.** In the multi-level perspective, a [[glossary/m-s/regime|regime]] is the settled set of rules, technologies, practices and expectations in a sector; a *niche* is a space where something different can be tried; the *landscape* is the wider context (population, ideas, the economy at large) that neither controls (Geels 2002) [IC]. This brief uses "landscape" only in that technical sense. Lawhon and Murphy (2012) argue that the field treats the regime as too singular and too detached from place and power [IC].
- **Passive and active shielding.** Smith and Raven separate spaces that existed before anyone set out to protect an innovation (off-grid areas, general research funding, green consumers) from spaces advocates create on purpose (a subsidy, a pilot permission, a solar co-operative) [IC].
- **Fit-and-conform and stretch-and-transform.** The first makes an innovation competitive in an unchanged selection environment. The second changes that environment in the innovation's favour (Smith and Raven 2012) [IC].
- **Technological and grassroots niches.** The founding work studies technologies and firms. Grassroots innovation research (Seyfang and Smith 2007 and after) studies community initiatives run on voluntary work and grants [IC]. Each finding below names its body of work.
- **Protection, incubation and experimentation.** *Protection* is any arrangement that lowers selection pressure on a young initiative. *Business incubation* houses and advises young firms for a period. A [[glossary/t-z/transition-experiment|transition experiment]] is a bounded, practice-based trial meant to feed wider change. A *living lab* is a user-centred testing arrangement in a real setting. A [[glossary/t-z/transformative-space|transformative space]] is a convened setting where people try new social-ecological arrangements (Pereira and others 2018). A *pilot programme* is a time-limited trial of a policy or service. Their evidence is kept separate below.
- **Outcomes and how they're known.** An initiative can survive without spreading, and spread without changing the regime; the case table records which outcome each study measured, and whether it was measured with a stated design, reconstructed from documents, or stated by the initiative or its funder.
- **The essay's claim and the research's claim.** Eisenstein's essay makes an ontological claim: some phenomena are not yet settled, and sceptical exposure changes them [MS]. Transitions research makes a social and economic claim: young initiatives are fragile under selection pressure. The Ward 2 Development Forum (W2DF) has adopted the second for its young initiatives [MS]. This brief tests neither the essay's claim nor the network's [[concepts/major-evolutionary-transitions|Major Evolutionary Transitions]] frame, which is Michael Haupt's position [MS].

## The field: from strategic niche management to protective space

**Strategic niche management (1998).** Kemp, Schot and Hoogma (1998) proposed that governments and firms could create technological niches on purpose, where users, producers and regulators learn together, as a route to regime shifts toward sustainability [IC]. Later reviews read these niches as temporary, with protection meant to fall away as the technology matures [IC on the later reading; the 1998 text wasn't reread].

**The multi-level perspective (2002 to 2007).** Geels (2002) built the multi-level perspective on the shift from sailing ships to steamships: niche innovations break through when landscape pressure loosens a regime [IC]. Geels and Schot (2007) set out several pathways by which this can happen [TBV on detail].

**Ten years of findings (2008).** Schot and Geels (2008) named three processes inside a niche (shaping expectations, building networks, learning) and concluded that they must be read together with processes outside it [IC]. These are the nurturing codes N1 to N3 in the case table.

**Protective space (2012).** Smith and Raven (2012) named shielding, nurturing and empowerment, called empowerment the least developed, and argued that protection is political: advocates must tell persuasive stories to win and keep it [IC]. They named its risks. Protected industries can be captured by those who gain from the rents; governments find it hard to withdraw protection from infant industries that aren't improving; and there's a risk of protective space "becoming an institution that shields poor innovation" ([[sources/smith-raven-2012-protective-space|Smith and Raven, 2011 working-paper version]]) [IC]. The glossary entry on [[glossary/f-l/infant-industry-protection|infant-industry protection]] records the older economic form of the argument.

**Testing the model on cases (2013 to 2016).** Verhees, Raven, Veraart, Smith and Kern (2013) traced Dutch solar photovoltaics (PV) from the 1970s to 2012 through five protected spaces, including research programmes, off-grid uses and local solar co-operatives, asking how it survived very harsh policy periods [IC]. Raven, Kern, Verhees and Smith (2016) compared six low-carbon cases (solar PV, offshore wind and carbon capture in the United Kingdom and the Netherlands), observing that "few studies pause to consider how and by whom" protective space is made ([[sources/raven-et-al-2016-niche-empowerment|Raven and others 2016]]). Their propositions on this socio-political work held, with finer distinctions needed [IC]. Secondary summaries say advocates used passive spaces before building active ones, and mostly chose fit-and-conform [TBV].

**Transition management and Three Horizons.** Loorbach (2010) set out *transition management*, a cycle of transition arenas, agendas, experiments and reflexive evaluation drawn from the Dutch national energy transition programme [IC]. Sharpe and colleagues (2016) describe *Three Horizons*, a facilitation practice that holds three patterns together in the present: the dominant pattern in decline (the first horizon), the emerging pattern (the third) and the contested space of transition between them (the second). They state that the paper draws on their own facilitation [IC]. Sharpe (2013) calls emerging practices pockets of the future in the present [IC]. It's the one framework in this field that invites a reading against the network's frame, since it holds the declining and the emerging together; that reading is the wiki's inference.

**Forms of protection.** Protection takes eight forms in the coding frame: financial (P1), regulatory (P2), market (P3: a sympathetic buyer), spatial (P4: a place beyond the regime's reach), temporal (P5: a fixed period before exposure), social (P6: a supportive network), cognitive (P7: a shared story) and informational (P8: a low profile). Financial and regulatory protection dominate the technology cases; social and cognitive protection dominate the grassroots cases. No study here treats a low profile as a designed tool; it appears as a passive condition of early niches [inference from the cases]. For civil-society initiatives, the grassroots studies point to social and cognitive protection, and to intermediaries, as the forms they can most often provide for themselves [IC].

## Grassroots and civil-society niches

Seyfang and Smith (2007) argued that community-led *grassroots innovations* differ from market niches. They arise from social need and ideology, rely on voluntary work and grants, and seek benefits that markets don't price. The authors set out a research and policy agenda for them [IC].

Seyfang and Haxeltine (2012) applied strategic niche management to the UK Transition Towns movement. They found that the movement had built a working niche of many local projects, with "significant weaknesses in the niche processes" ([[sources/seyfang-haxeltine-2012-growing-grassroots-innovations|Seyfang and Haxeltine 2012]]), including the difficulty of passing learning from one project to another. They concluded that the framework needed adapting for civil-society niches [IC].

Hargreaves, Hielscher, Seyfang and Smith (2013) studied UK community energy, starting from the observation that such groups struggle "in simply surviving" ([[sources/hargreaves-et-al-2013-community-energy-intermediaries|Hargreaves and others 2013]]). They examined how intermediary organisations collect and pass on lessons. Intermediaries helped consolidate the niche. The knowledge most groups needed was local and hard to transfer through toolkits [IC].

Feola and Nunes (2014) surveyed 276 Transition initiatives in 23 countries, active and inactive, which makes it a rare study that includes ones that stopped. Most rated themselves successful, defining success by social connection, empowerment and environmental impact. Less successful initiatives may underestimate contextual factors and material resources. The authors also identified an incubation period in which an initiative consolidates, and linked spread to a mix of local and global learning [IC]. Success was self-reported, so the outcome is a claim gathered by survey [IC].

Smith, Fressoli, Abrol, Arond and Ely (2017) examined six grassroots innovation movements: Socially Useful Production (United Kingdom), Appropriate Technology (South America), the People's Science Movements and the Honey Bee Network (India), the Social Technology Network (Brazil), and hackerspaces and makerspaces [IC]. Their histories show movements opening space for alternatives, and being absorbed or sidelined by more powerful actors [TBV on the outcome of each case].

No study found for this brief follows a community energy group, food co-operative or local currency after a defined protection period ended. That gap is itself a finding.

## Experiments, living labs and transformative spaces

Sengers, Wieczorek and Raven (2019) systematically reviewed the literature on experiments and separated five kinds: niche, bounded socio-technical, transition, sustainability and grassroots experiments [IC]. The review maps concepts and doesn't pool outcome data.

Pereira, Karpouzoglou, Frantzeskaki and Olsson (2018) set out the idea of transformative spaces [IC]. Pereira and fourteen co-authors (2020) drew five lessons from nine such spaces, mainly in the Global South and including Southern African work, on how they are designed, convened and opened [IC]. The authors convened or facilitated the cases, so the paper is an account of their own work. The transformation laboratory (T-lab) work of Charli-Joseph and others belongs to the same family [TBV on detail].

The run didn't reach the urban experimentation literature (Bulkeley, Castán Broto, Evans and others) or living-lab evaluations, and found no African living-lab evaluation.

## The evidence on outcomes, by design level

The brief grades each outcome claim on a five-level ladder: **E1** a comparative study of many initiatives with systematic coding, or a survival or matched-comparison analysis; **E2** a longitudinal study with a stated baseline, followed over years; **E3** a retrospective case study by independent researchers; **E4** a framework, review or model with illustrative cases; **E5** an account by a practitioner, funder or the initiative itself.

**E1.** Two bodies of work.

- Feola and Nunes (2014): a survey of 276 Transition initiatives in 23 countries, including inactive ones, with statistical and cluster analysis [IC].
- Business incubation. Schwartz (2013) matched 371 graduates of five German incubators with 371 non-incubated firms over ten years; no site showed higher survival for incubated firms, and three showed significantly lower survival [IC]. Murillo-Rojas, Brinckmann and van Essen (2026) pooled 39 studies covering 55,219 firms and found clearer gains for innovation and growth than for survival, where effects were weak or inconsistent [IC]. Seitz, Buratti, Lehmann and Eberle (2026) pooled 22 studies and 65 effect sizes and found a positive overall effect on performance, moderated by specialisation, public sponsorship and region [IC]. These are business firms, so they bear on community initiatives only by analogy.

**E2.** None found that set a baseline and followed a protected community initiative forward. Verhees and others (2013) cover four decades as a historical reconstruction from documents and interviews [IC].

**E3.** Dutch solar PV (Verhees and others 2013); the six-case synthesis by the same team (Raven and others 2016); UK Transition Towns (Seyfang and Haxeltine 2012); UK community energy intermediaries (Hargreaves and others 2013); six grassroots movements (Smith and others 2017); South Africa's renewable energy community trusts (Wlokas 2015, a report).

**E4.** Kemp, Schot and Hoogma (1998); Geels (2002); Schot and Geels (2008); Smith and Raven (2012); Sengers and others (2019); Wieczorek (2018); Lawhon and Murphy (2012).

**E5.** Accounts by people who ran the work: Lynedoch, by its co-founders (Swilling and Annecke 2012); Three Horizons, from the authors' facilitation (Sharpe and others 2016); transformative spaces the authors convened (Pereira and others 2020); Dutch transition management, which Loorbach's institute advised (Loorbach 2010).

Outcome claims about protection rest on E3 to E5. The only E1 designs come from the Transition movement and from business incubation [IC on the pattern; inference on its meaning].

## The exit

Smith and Raven (2012) say empowerment is the least developed property, and warn of capture and of protection that outlives its purpose [IC]. Raven and others (2016) treat empowerment as outward socio-political work, which depended on aligning the innovation's story with the agendas of powerful actors [IC]. Dutch PV survived by moving between protected spaces as each was won and lost (Verhees and others 2013) [IC].

The incubator studies give the clearest numbers on what happens when protection ends:

- Schwartz (2009), 352 graduates: leaving the incubator brings an immediate fall in survival that lasts up to three years [IC].
- Schwartz (2011), 324 graduates: early growth after graduation, then no continued growth for most firms [IC].
- Schwartz (2012), 149 graduates: longer incubation and an older incubator both lower survival after graduation; he reads this as support for strict limits on incubation time [IC].
- Schwartz (2013), the matched comparison: no survival advantage after graduation, and lower survival at three of five sites [IC].

The wiki's [[research/landscape-restoration-sub-saharan-africa|restoration synthesis]] records that the job model of South Africa's Working for Water programme produced dependency [MS]. No study here compares protected spaces with and without a written exit rule.

## The politics and the critiques

**Who decides.** Smith, Stirling and Berkhout (2005) argued that the direction of a transition depends on the resources and adaptive capacity of regime actors, and that steering it is political [IC]. Smith and Stirling (2010) argued that transitions and resilience research both tend to close down governance around expert framings, and called for opening it up [IC]. Raven and others (2016) found that advocates won protection by tying their technology to powerful actors' agendas [IC].

**Political ecology.** Lawhon and Murphy (2012) named four limitations of transitions research and drew on political ecology to address them, with a case on genetically modified crops. In their reading the field neglects how problems are defined, who wins and loses, and the role of place [IC].

**Power in the multi-level perspective.** Geels (2014) replied by adding politics and power to the framework, describing how incumbents in the UK electricity sector resist low-carbon change [IC]. This answers the charge that power is missing, and leaves open the critics' wish for a different starting point [inference].

**Grassroots movements.** Seyfang and Smith (2007) and Smith and others (2017) argue that for movements contesting mainstream framings of innovation, empowerment on the regime's terms can mean losing their purpose [IC]. Smith and Raven (2012) make a related point about fit-and-conform [IC].

Both sides agree that protection is political. They differ on whether the multi-level perspective can hold that politics or needs replacing [inference].

## The Global South and Africa

Wieczorek (2018) reviewed transitions studies of developing countries. Her review suggests that regimes there are often less uniform than in Europe, and that niches may need a degree of regime stability to develop [IC]. The editors of the same special issue (Hansen and others 2018) argue that the field's frameworks need adapting to high inequality and informality [IC], and Ramos-Mejía and others (2018) describe socio-technical change among people living in poverty [IC].

### South Africa

**Lynedoch.** Swilling and Annecke (2012) read transitions from the Global South with inequality at the centre. Their Chapter 10, "Pioneering Liveable Urbanism: Reflections on an Invisible Way", covers Lynedoch EcoVillage and the Sustainability Institute near Stellenbosch, which they co-founded in 1999 [IC on the record; E5 on the content]. Their 2006 article describes Lynedoch as South Africa's first socially mixed, ecologically designed intentional community [MS].

**Renewable energy community trusts.** South Africa's Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPPP) requires projects to spend on socio-economic and enterprise development and to share some ownership with local communities, usually through community trusts [IC]. Read as protection, the trusts are financial shielding (P1) created by the state and run by firms. Wlokas (2015), in a report for WWF-SA covering the 79 projects approved by May 2015, found that companies mostly planned their community duties in isolation, often through private service providers, and treated community members mainly as beneficiaries. She judged that the arrangements appear to reinforce existing power structures ([[sources/wlokas-2015-reipppp-community-development|Wlokas 2015]]) [IC as a report finding; not peer-reviewed]. The programme office reports 88,317 job years and R3.4 billion in socio-economic development spending by 2025 [MS, as cited in a 2025 *New Agenda* article].

**Inclusive innovation at Stellenbosch.** Grobbelaar, Tijssen and Dijksterhuis (2017) studied 15 university-driven inclusive innovation activities in the Western Cape and proposed a framework of innovation regimes for them [IC]; Botha, Grobbelaar and Bam (2016) proposed a framework for evaluating inclusive innovation systems [IC]. Professor Sara Grobbelaar's group publishes on inclusive innovation and innovation platforms. The search found no published study, by the group or anyone else, of a grassroots niche or living lab in a former mission station [IC on the absence within this search].

### Where the formal regime doesn't reach

No study found addresses protection on communal or mission-station land, in informal economies, or in South African food and seed networks read as niches. The wiki's synthesis of [[research/landscape-restoration-sub-saharan-africa|sixteen restoration cases in sub-Saharan Africa]] found that durable cases grew from existing local practice and secure local rights, with long apprenticeships before formal institutions [MS]. That describes passive, social and temporal protection that communities provided for themselves, and it's the closest evidence available here. The run didn't reach evidence from elsewhere in Africa on living labs, hubs and incubators.

## Measuring the young

**Reflexive monitoring in action.** Van Mierlo and colleagues (2010), at Wageningen University and VU University Amsterdam, designed a method in which a monitor works inside a system innovation project, helping participants reflect on the system they want to change and adjust their actions as they go. It's built for learning during the work and claims no outcome measure of its own [IC].

**Developmental evaluation.** Patton (2011) proposed evaluation for innovations in complex, changing settings, where the evaluator helps the work adapt and the goals may change. It differs from formative evaluation, which improves a settled model, and summative evaluation, which judges a finished one [IC].

**The politics of results.** Eyben, Guijt, Roche and Shutt (2015) collected practitioners' accounts of how demands for results in international aid shape programmes, and how staff comply, adapt or resist [IC]. Their cases describe work drifting toward what can be counted [TBV on specific cases].

**Motivation.** Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) reviewed 128 experiments and found that tangible rewards tied to a task reduced people's own wish to do it, while positive feedback tended to raise it [IC].

The wiki's [[frameworks/political-economy-analysis|Political Economy Analysis]] method studies who wants what and why. The protective-space literature adds nurturing processes (expectations, networks, learning) that a monitor can track without scoring the initiative, and reflexive monitoring as a method built for that [inference]. No comparative study shows that either method harms young initiatives less than standard impact metrics; the claim rests on design reasoning and practitioner accounts (E4 to E5).

## The counterpart: the research beside Eisenstein's layers of shielding

Eisenstein's essay of 8 February 2026 proposes *ontological shielding* for research into phenomena that consensus reality treats as impossible. It describes five layers, from no shielding to complete shielding, and likens the most shielded experiment to "a baby in an incubator" ([[sources/eisenstein-2026-ontological-shielding|Eisenstein 2026]]) [MS]. The mapping below is the analyst's inference.

| Layer (Eisenstein) [MS] | Nearest research concept | Fit | What the essay adds | What the research adds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0: no shielding; hostile observers, strict protocols | Full exposure to the regime's selection environment | Close in structure | Exposure changes the phenomenon; a positive result signals a shift in the wider reality | Exposure tests fit; premature exposure ends an initiative by selection, with no change to the thing itself |
| 1: rigorous protocols with a deliberate flaw left for sceptics | None; nearest is fit-and-conform presentation | Weak | Strategic imperfection as a shield | Empowerment through open narratives (Raven and others 2016); no work endorses concealment |
| 2: deliberately informal, for a sympathetic public | Passive space among sympathetic users; grassroots niches | Moderate | Lower rigour as a condition for the phenomenon | Sympathetic users as passive space (Smith and Raven 2012) |
| 3: a bubble visible only to participants | Strong shielding; a low profile (P8); a closed transition arena | Moderate | The phenomenon matures only inside the bubble | Closed spaces aid learning and need planned links outward |
| 4: shielded even from the experimenter | None | None | Results travel by story and an unseen field | No term; unobserved outcomes are unknown |

The parallel societies research map called the fit "Close". On this reading it's close only at layer 0, moderate at layers 2 and 3, weak at layer 1 and absent at layer 4 [inference]. The essay also considers deliberately flawed or fraudulent experiments as shielding, and says sincere researchers need not use them [MS]. The W2DF has dropped hidden steering, and no recommendation here draws on those elements.

## Evidence the claims must face

1. **Survivorship.** Almost all the cases are survivors. Feola and Nunes (who included inactive initiatives) and Schwartz (matched controls, and firms that closed) are the only exceptions found [IC].
2. **Authors studying their own work.** Lynedoch (Swilling and Annecke), Three Horizons (Sharpe and others), the transformative spaces (Pereira and others) and Dutch transition management (Loorbach) were written up by people who ran, convened or advised them. They're graded E5.
3. **Technology to community.** The core model comes from technology cases. Grassroots studies confirm that nurturing and intermediaries matter and that the model needs adapting [IC]. Findings on exit and empowerment haven't been tested on co-operatives or learning campuses.
4. **The counterfactual.** Only Schwartz (2013) and the incubator meta-analyses compare protected and unprotected ventures. No transitions study of a community initiative does.
5. **The case for exposure.** The incubator evidence shows no long-term survival gain from shelter, and a hazard at the moment of exit [IC]. Lean start-up practice holds that early contact with real customers finds failure cheaply [TBV; practitioner literature]. Open science answers the replication problem with more exposure, through registered plans and shared data; the Open Science Collaboration (2015) found 36 per cent of replications statistically significant [IC]. Smith and Raven's own warning about sheltering weak innovation points the same way [IC].
6. **The essay's case.** Eisenstein holds that sceptical scrutiny dilutes phenomena that aren't yet settled, so research can be designed with chosen layers of shielding, and designing an experiment becomes a creative act [MS]. This brief maps that case and neither endorses nor refutes it.

## Applying the findings (the wiki's reading)

The BioConomy wiki's reading for a bioregional forum or BioHub deciding to protect a young initiative. Each point is inference from the evidence, with its grade.

- **What to protect.** Favour initiatives that grow from practices people already use and whose local rights are secure (the restoration synthesis [MS]; Feola and Nunes on context [IC]). Write down why the initiative needs protection, and from which pressure.
- **Forms to offer.** Offer social (P6) and cognitive (P7) protection first: a network, a shared story, and an intermediary that carries lessons in (Hargreaves and others [IC]). Pair them with enough material resources to get through the start (Feola and Nunes [IC]). Money is necessary and isn't enough on its own.
- **Terms in public.** Record each protection's form, provider, purpose and review date in the forum's public record. The research gives no reason to hide protection (P9, supported) and warns of capture where terms are unclear (Smith and Raven [IC]). The [[frameworks/three-feature-test|Three-Feature Test]] asks a related question of patrons under "protection during maximum exposure".
- **Monitoring.** Use reflexive monitoring or developmental evaluation early, and track the nurturing processes. Don't pay per act or rank people (P7, weakly supported; Deci and others [IC]).
- **Exposure.** Agree and record a review point at which the initiative meets wider conditions, and plan support for that moment (Schwartz 2009, 2012 [IC]; P6 untested for community initiatives). Choose fit-and-conform or stretch-and-transform openly (P8, untested). [[concepts/entity-gestation|Entity gestation]] is already a temporal protection of this kind, with a planned end.
