On 25 November 2026 Anabranch run 9 reread the seven squares that rested on the earlier two-part wording, and confirmed ten others at their positions. Taking part in a cosmic process is a stronger claim than developing through stages. Montessori's To Educate the Human Potential (1948), read in its text, gives the closest statement in print: all beings, knowingly or unknowingly, serve a cosmic plan, and each child has a cosmic task. Wilber (2000) holds that each person's development unfolds within Spirit's evolution, read from reviews. Berry (1988) and Swimme and Tucker (2011) affirm, both read from summaries or descriptions. McIntosh (2007) ties personal growth to cosmic evolution and holds that most adults move little once grown, and Briggs (2021) calls on all who are capable; both set conditions and qualify. Smuts (1926) treats personality as the highest whole the holistic process has made, and Sahtouris holds that Indigenous peoples have long known the universe is participatory; neither states that every person takes part, so both qualify. The Big History Project (2011) places humanity as a whole in one cosmic story and qualifies; Conrad (2019), which finds its curriculum Eurocentric, does not take up the step. Loevinger, Kegan, Beck and Cowan, and Jung's account of individuation describe development with no cosmic claim, and qualify; Tomasello, Siegler, and Fischer and Bidell are silent. The squares of Loevinger (1976) and Kegan (1982) keep codes made against the earlier wording and await rereading; an earlier batch found silent a better fit for both. Monod (1970) dissents, holding that humans arose by chance in a nature without purpose; Dundes (2005) and Segal (1991) dissent against Campbell's claim of a hero story shared by every culture. No work found so far argues against universal participation as such. The strongest affirming work is Montessori (1948); the strongest dissent is Monod (1970).
