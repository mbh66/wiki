The sequence is amended: four production forms became dominant in the order Tribal, Institutional, Market, Emergent and all four operate now, but the costs each form externalized were not organized by the next form, and the Emergent form externalizes costs of its own.

## Overview

Michael Haupt's construct says production evolved the way the [[glossary/t-z/time|TIME]] framework says coordination did. There were four forms in sequence. Each solved problems its predecessors could not. Each pushed costs onto a substrate the next form would organize. Each was tied to a communications revolution, and all four still run together. This brief tests each property against economic history, official statistics and the wiki's own pages. TIME is [[ronfeldt-david|David Ronfeldt]]'s TIMN framework, renamed on the wiki; the production sequence is Michael's construct and appears nowhere in Ronfeldt.

Four of the five properties survive in some form. The forms are distinct logics of organizing the making of goods and services, and each has a literature: 
- household production (Reid 1934; Becker 1965; de Vries 2008), 
- guilds and chartered companies (Ogilvie 2019; Epstein), 
- the managerial corporation (Chandler 1977) and 
- commons-based [[glossary/m-s/peer-production|peer production]] (Benkler 2002, 2006). 

Each solved a problem its predecessors handled badly, though the guild's claim to have solved quality and skill is disputed. All four operate now. The International Labour Organization (ILO) finds 16.4 billion hours of unpaid care work every day, from time-use data in 64 countries covering 66.9 percent of the world's working-age population (ILO 2018) [IC]. It counts more than 2 billion workers in informal employment in 2023, 58.0 percent of the global workforce (ILOSTAT, reporting the ILO's *World Employment and Social Outlook: Trends 2024*) [IC]. One Harvard estimate puts the demand-side value of open source software at US\$8.8 trillion [TBV, disputed].

The property that fails is the one that gives the sequence its moral force: that each form's externalized costs became the next form's inheritance. The best-documented case points the other way. The costs the factory pushed onto children and onto the air were taken up by the state, the Institutional form, through the Factory Act 1833 and the Alkali Act 1863 [IC]. The founders of peer production said they were responding to proprietary software restrictions and to cheap computation. Neither cited the factory's costs.

A practitioner can use the four forms as a diagnostic vocabulary for which ways of making things are present in a [[glossary/a-e/bioregion|bioregion]]. A practitioner should not tell funders that the [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] is where history's debts come due because there is nowhere left to push them. The Emergent form pushes costs onto unpaid maintainers and onto power and water systems it does not govern.

## The five problems

### 1. Three sequences that disagree

Before 8 November 2026 the wiki held two sequences of nodes side by side, on the same pages. The TIME Framework page and the [[frameworks/evolution-of-coordination-nodes|Evolution of Coordination Nodes]] page both named the physical expression of each coordination form: "the sacred site" for Tribal, "the cathedral, the palace, the capitol" for Institutional, "the skyscraper and the trading floor, with the stock index as its legibility signal" for Market, and "the bioregional hub" for Emergent. The [[frameworks/time-physical-manifestations|Physical Manifestations of TIME]] page uses the same four. Both of the first two pages then carried a second sequence. The TIME page's section "The sequence and the debt" listed "The Farm is the first coordination node (approximately 12,000 years ago)", "The Stock Market is the second (approximately 250 years ago)", "The Data Center is the third (approximately 50 years ago)" and "The BioHub is the fourth". The Coordination Nodes page's "structural pattern" section said "The farm externalized onto soil and water. The stock market externalized onto labor and biosphere. The data center externalizes onto energy and local hydrological budgets," and its table assigned externalized costs that disagreed with the TIME page: none for the Tribal form, and "Commons the institution was carved from" for the Institutional form, a cost the TIME page gives the Farm. The research memo [[research/biohub-noosphere-research-brief|The BioHub as Where the Noosphere Lands]] repeated the Farm, Stock Market, Data Center, BioHub sequence.

Read by ordinal, the debt sequence put the Stock Market in the Institutional slot and the Data Center in the Market slot. Read by content, the Farm is a node of production, the Stock Market is the Market form's capital node (the TIME page itself calls the stock index the Market form's legibility signal), and the Data Center is infrastructure owned and run by Market-form firms that every form now uses. No Institutional production node appeared. The debt sequence was therefore a sequence of production and capital nodes, with the Institutional step missing, written alongside a sequence of [[glossary/a-e/coordination-node|coordination nodes]].

Resolution: the two are different sequences seen from the sides of coordination and production, and they share only their endpoints. The coordination nodes stay as TIME's sequence. The production nodes now live on one page, [[frameworks/sequence-and-the-debt|The Sequence and the Debt]], with an Institutional node added, the Data Center recorded as Market-owned infrastructure, and space reserved in the Emergent row for place-based AI. Table 5.2 records each conflict and the edit made.

### 2. Order and overlap

The order holds when each form is dated by the period in which it governed most production. It fails when dated by first appearance.

Ronfeldt's own account supports the overlap. His 1996 figure places "trading cos." between the institutional and market forms, and he writes that "the market system absorbs from the state some institutions that had been engaging in commerce and finance at the state's behest, like banks and trading companies." He dates "the market" as a concept to the eighteenth century while noting "marketplaces in ancient times (e.g., the Greek agora)." Coase's 1937 question, why firms exist inside markets, treats hierarchy and exchange as simultaneous modes of organizing production, and Benkler (2002) built peer production as a third mode beside "firms and markets." Ogilvie's guilds ran "from 1000 to 1880," overlapping with merchant putting-out and proto-industry for centuries.

Resolution: Institutional production begins with the temple and palace estates of the first states, where writing first served accounting. The Metropolitan Museum of Art dates the earliest Uruk tablets to around 3300 BCE and says they "record economic information"; one tablet in its collection "most likely documents grain distributed by a large temple" [IC]. The guild (from the twelfth century) and the chartered company (1600 and 1602) are late Institutional forms that operated inside growing markets, and the brief treats them as hybrids of the Institutional and Market forms. The Market form's dominance of production dates from the factory and the managerial corporation (the 1850s in the United States, per Chandler). The factory dates from Cromford Mill in Derbyshire, built by Richard Arkwright in 1771, which Cromford Mills calls "the world's first successful water powered cotton spinning mill" [MS].

### 3. What "externalized onto the substrate the next form must organize" means

Two senses need to stay apart. In the economist's sense (Pigou 1920; Coase 1960), a [[glossary/m-s/negative-externalities|negative externality]] is a cost of one party's activity that falls on another party and is not reflected in the price the first party faces. Pigou proposed taxing the divergence; Coase argued that the cost is reciprocal and that bargaining or the law can allocate it where transaction costs allow. TIME's sense is broader. It counts costs to communities, to labor and to the biosphere whether or not a price could be attached, and it includes unpaid inputs such as care and the [[glossary/a-e/commons|commons]].

Operational definition used here. A production form externalizes a cost when three conditions hold together: an identifiable harm or uncompensated input arises from its production; the party bearing it sits outside the form's decision and accounting boundary; and the form's own prices or rules do not charge for it. Each source below is labeled economic ($) or TIME (T) sense.

The second claim, that the next form arose at least partly to organize those costs, is tested on two kinds of evidence: timing (did the later form appear after the cost was recognized?) and stated purpose (did its founders say they were responding to it?).

### 4. The communications ties

Ronfeldt's pairing exists, and its wording shifts across his texts. A P2P Foundation compilation quotes him: "the rise of each major form... is associated with a different information and communications technology revolution," with tribes depending on "the emergence of language and early writing (runes, glyphs)." A 2012 summary of his 1996 work gives "The written word enabled institutions, the printed word fostered regional and global markets, and the electric (digital) word is empowering worldwide networks." His 2025 Substack gives "speech and storytelling for tribes, writing and printing for institutions, telegraphy and telephony for markets." In the pages of P-7967 read for this brief (pages 1 to 17), the explicit pairing did not appear; the 1996 text ties the network form to "fax machines, electronic mail (e-mail), and computer conferencing systems." Print therefore moves between institutions and markets across his versions.

Production lines up with different revolutions at two points. The factory came before the telegraph: its enabling technologies were power and transport (water and steam, then the railroad). Chandler pairs the managerial corporation with "the railroad in transportation and the telegraph in communication." The global value chain's second unbundling tracks the internet (Baldwin 2016, per [[research/glocalization-and-the-evolution-of-production|the glocalization brief]]). Peer production ties cleanly to networked information: Benkler names "the declining price of computation, communication, and storage."

### 5. Teleology

A sequence written from the present tends to make the present its destination. Popper's *The Poverty of Historicism* (1957) rejects laws of historical development that predict where history must go. Gerschenkron (1962) showed that late industrializers such as Russia did not pass through Britain's stages; reviewers summarize his position as "There were no equivalent stages of economic growth in all participants," set against Rostow's five stages (1960). McCloskey credits him with undermining "the metaphor of social stages."

The TIME page has already defended itself against part of this: "TIME is not a developmental ladder. The four forms are not stages that a community passes through and leaves behind. They are layers that accumulate." The debt section reintroduced the teleology the typology disclaims: "The BioHub is where the running debt comes due, because there is nowhere left to push it." Ronfeldt goes further than the wiki, writing (as quoted by the P2P Foundation) that "No society can escape the TIMN progression." The production sequence survives the critique only as an accumulation of layers dated by dominance, with Gerschenkron's point kept in view: a bioregion can adopt peer production without first passing through factory dominance.

## The four production forms

### Tribal: household and kin production

**Sequence**. Kin production is the oldest form, older than farming. The farming household, which the wiki dates with the Farm to about 12,000 years ago, is its agrarian version. Ronfeldt dates the tribe to "the Neolithic era some 5000 years ago," which conflicts with the wiki's date; the Neolithic transition is usually dated much earlier, so the wiki's date is closer to the archaeological consensus [TBV].

**Solution**. Kin production solved provisioning and the transmission of skill by reciprocity and obligation, with no need for money or command. In Polanyi's terms (1944) it runs on reciprocity and householding.

**Externalization**. Under farming, the cost the TIME page recorded is "soil depletion, downstream water quality, and the commons the farm was carved from" (T sense). A second cost belongs here and is still unpriced: household labor, mostly women's. The ILO finds that women perform 76.2 percent of unpaid care work worldwide, 3.2 times as much as men (ILO 2018) [IC]. This cost is borne inside the household, so it is externalized from markets and national accounts more than from the household itself (T sense). The wiki's term for such work is [[glossary/t-z/unrecognized-contribution|unrecognized contribution]].

**Organization by the next form**. Temple and palace estates did organize agrarian surplus, through storage, records and redistribution. No evidence found here shows that the guild or the early estate arose to repair soil or the commons.

**Communications**. Speech and shared practice, matching Ronfeldt's "speech and storytelling for tribes."

**Operating now**. Unpaid care work runs at 16.4 billion hours a day, "equivalent to 2.0 billion people working eight hours per day with no remuneration." Valued at an hourly minimum wage, the work comes to 9 percent of global GDP, or US\$11 trillion at 2011 purchasing power parity (ILO 2018) [IC]. Food production holds the largest concentration of informal jobs, about a third of all informal employment (ILO, as reported in 2025) [TBV].

**Node**. The homestead and the family farm.

### Institutional: privileged production

**Sequence**. Temple and palace estates from about 3300 BCE, when the earliest Uruk tablets recorded economic information such as temple grain distributions (Metropolitan Museum of Art); craft guilds in Europe "from 1000 to 1880" (Ogilvie); the English and Dutch East India Companies from 1600 and 1602.

**Solution**. Hierarchy and privilege let production reach a scale and a distance kin could not. Ronfeldt credits the hierarchical form with "organizing large economic tasks." The guild's claimed solution, trust in quality and the training of skill, is contested. S. R. Epstein argued that guilds supplied skill. Ogilvie replied (2008) that "craft guilds adversely affected quality, skills, and innovation," that "guild rent-seeking imposed deadweight losses on the economy," and that "industry flourished where guilds decayed." A reviewer of her 2019 book in the *International Review of Social History* objects that she "considers all regulation by guilds as rent-seeking behaviour throughout." The dispute is live, and this brief does not settle it.

**Externalization**. Guild costs fell on consumers who paid monopoly prices and on women and outsiders excluded from trades (Ogilvie). These are redistributive costs in the economist's sense more than externalities. Chartered company costs fell on colonized labor and land (T sense); Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson (2005) is the lead on how Atlantic trade enriched merchant groups, and it was not read for this brief [TBV].

**Organization by the next form**. Ronfeldt's account is the closest support: hierarchy "cannot process complex exchanges," and traders "work to break free of autocratic controls." That describes a limit of capacity, and it names no cost pushed onto a substrate.

**Communications**. Writing and records, then print. This matches Ronfeldt's later pairing ("writing and printing for institutions").

**Operating now**. Licensed professions, protected geographical indications and craft associations persist, but this run gathered no global figures for them [TBV]. The chartered company's descendant, the state-owned enterprise, was not measured here.

**Node**. The guildhall and workshop; the temple estate before it; the company's warehouse and trading post.

### Market: factory, managerial corporation and global value chain

**Sequence**. The factory from 1771, when Richard Arkwright built Cromford Mill; the managerial corporation from the 1850s, when "the railroads were the first modern business enterprises" (Chandler); the global value chain from the late twentieth century (see the glocalization brief).

**Solution**. Chandler's proposition is that "modern multiunit business enterprise replaced small traditional enterprise when administrative coordination permitted greater productivity, lower costs, and higher profits than coordination by market mechanisms." The factory solved power and scale; the corporation solved throughput coordination; the value chain solved coordination across borders.

**Externalization**. This is the best-documented row. The Factory Act 1833 names the cost in its preamble: there were "great Numbers of Children and young Persons now employed in Mills and Factories, and their Hours of Labour are longer than is desirable, due Regard being had to their Health and Means of Education." It prohibited employment of children who had not completed their ninth year and capped under-18s at "Twelve Hours in any One Day, nor more than Sixty-nine Hours in any One Week" [IC]. The Leblanc alkali process vented about 1.5 tons of hydrogen chloride per ton of soda ash. The Science Museum records that the gas "polluted the air around factory towns and cities, killing almost all vegetation and livestock." When the Alkali Act forced condensation, producers dumped the acid "into rivers and seas where it devastated aquatic life." The cost moved from one sink to another ($ and T sense) [IC].

**Organization by the next form**. The costs were organized by the Institutional form. The Factory Act empowered the Crown "to appoint during His Majesty's Pleasure Four Persons to be Inspectors of Factories." The Alkali Act 1863 created a national inspectorate under Robert Angus Smith to enforce a 95 percent condensation limit, and that inspectorate became Her Majesty's Inspectorate of Pollution in 1987 [IC]. This is Pigou's remedy carried out by the state, and it reverses the direction E4 proposes.

**Communications**. Power and transport for the factory; telegraph for the corporation; the internet for the value chain. Ronfeldt pairs markets with print in one version and with "telegraphy and telephony" in another.

**Operating now**. Recorded in the glocalization brief; not repeated here.

**Node**. The mill and the factory, with the stock exchange as capital node and the stock index as [[glossary/f-l/legibility-signal|legibility signal]]. The data center belongs here as Market-owned infrastructure.

### Emergent: peer and cosmo-local production

**Sequence**. Free software from 1983 (Stallman's GNU announcement); fab labs from 2003 (Gershenfeld, MIT); Benkler's "commons-based peer production" from 2002; "design global, manufacture local" (DGML) from Kostakis and colleagues in 2015, the wiki's [[glossary/a-e/cosmo-local-production|cosmo-local production]].

**Solution**. Benkler argues that peer production "has an advantage over firms and markets" in matching people to creative tasks, because "the declining price of computation, communication, and storage have... placed the material means of information and cultural production in the hands of a significant fraction of the world's population." DGML describes "processes through which design is developed, shared and improved as a global digital commons, whereas the actual manufacturing takes place locally through shared infrastructures."

**Externalization**. The TIME page stated that for the BioHub "there is no substrate below it to receive the costs." Three costs contradict this for peer production.

- **Unpaid labor**. Tidelift's 2024 survey of more than 400 maintainers found that "60% of them remain unpaid hobbyists" and that 60 percent "have quit or considered quitting their maintenance work." Tidelift sells maintainer funding and is an interested party [MS]. Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou (Harvard Business School, 2024) find that 96 percent of open source value comes from 5 percent of developers, as reported by secondary sources [TBV]. The value flows to firms, which would need to spend 3.5 times more on software without it.
- **Energy and water through infrastructure the form doesn't govern**. The International Energy Agency estimates data center use at around 415 terawatt hours, about 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption in 2024, projected in its Base Case to reach around 945 TWh by 2030 (*Energy and AI*, April 2025) [IC]. The United States accounted for 45 percent of 2024 consumption, China 25 percent and Europe 15 percent (IEA figures as summarized by NS Energy) [IC].
- **Materials and energy of distributed fabrication**. Life cycle studies of cosmo-local products exist, including a comparison of small wind turbine life cycles reported as favoring open-source local manufacture. These are written by DGML's proponents and need independent replication [TBV].

**Organization by the next form**. There is no next form. The founders did not describe themselves as organizing the factory's debts. 
- Stallman, 1983: "I cannot in good conscience sign a nondisclosure agreement or a software license agreement." 
- The GNU Manifesto, 1985: "Software sellers want to divide the users and conquer them." 
- Benkler, 2006, names the catalyst as "the happenstance of the fabrication technology of computation." Where he contrasts the industrial economy's "coal, steel, and manual human labor," he is describing capital requirements, and the passage catalogs no externalities. 

The ecological framing of the digital commons arrives later, for example in Kostakis's degrowth work (2018). Commentary has since carried it to a general readership: Amelia Rose Barlow, writing in *Rolling Stone* on 22 October 2026 on the thirtieth anniversary of her father John Perry Barlow's "A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace," argues that "Bits are born in mines, factories, data centers, undersea cables, power grids, and human hands," that "The cloud is not weightless," and that "openness without responsibility can become extraction" [IC that she wrote it; commentary, and she co-stewards a campaign on the subject].

**Communications**. Networked information, matching Ronfeldt's network form.

**Operating now**. The Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou estimate (2024) puts the supply-side value of widely used open source at US\$4.15 billion and the demand-side value at US\$8.8 trillion. Chad Whitacre (2024) disputes the demand-side method and reports that the paper's own goods-market approach gives US\$177 million, "four orders of magnitude lower" [TBV for the value]. The Fab Foundation counts "more than 1,750 Fab Labs" in "more than 100 countries," and its FAB26 event page states "over 2000 digital fabrication facilities in 126 countries." Both are interested parties [MS], and other network sites claim 2,500 or more.

**Node**. The fab lab, the code repository and the BioHub. Place-based uses of AI, recorded in [[research/dark-fiber-and-the-ai-overbuild|Dark Fiber and the AI Overbuild]], belong in this row; The Sequence and the Debt reserves space for them.

## Reconciling the wiki's sequences

The sequences answer different questions. 
- The coordination-node sequence records where collective action becomes visible. 
- The production sequence records how making was organized. 
- The debt sequence recorded where capital and cost accumulated. 

They converged at the BioHub because the wiki wrote them to converge there, which is the teleology problem in miniature. The edits listed in Table 5.2 were made on 8 November 2026, on Michael's instruction, and the production nodes now have one home at [[frameworks/sequence-and-the-debt|The Sequence and the Debt]].

## Hypotheses E1 to E8

### E1. Sequence

Evidence for: Ronfeldt's ordering of tribes, institutions and markets by period of maturity; the dated rise of the factory, the managerial corporation (Chandler) and peer production (1983 to 2015).
Evidence against: markets and hierarchies coexisted from antiquity (Ronfeldt's agora; Coase's firm inside the market); guilds ran from 1000 to 1880 beside proto-industry; the East India Companies were hybrids.
Grade: [IC] for order by dominance; the first-appearance version fails.
Recommended status: academic-inference.
Practitioner: say the forms became dominant in this order; avoid saying each began after the last ended.

### E2. Solution

Evidence for: Chandler on administrative coordination; Benkler on information opportunity costs; Ronfeldt on hierarchy organizing "large economic tasks."
Evidence against: Ogilvie's finding that guilds "adversely affected quality, skills, and innovation."
Grade: [IC] for Market and Emergent; [TBV] for the guild row.
Recommended status: academic-inference.
Practitioner: safe to say each form solved a problem of scale or coordination; avoid claiming guilds solved quality.

### E3. Externalization

Evidence for: the Factory Act preamble; the Alkali Act and the Leblanc record; ILO unpaid care; Ogilvie on guild rents; Tidelift and the IEA for the Emergent form.
Evidence against: some Tribal and Institutional costs are redistributive (E sense) more than externalities, and costs borne inside households fit the definition only partly.
Grade: [IC] for Market; [MS] and [IC] mixed for Emergent; [TBV] for Tribal and Institutional colonial costs.
Recommended status: academic-inference. The claim that the BioHub has "no substrate below it" has been withdrawn from the TIME and Coordination Nodes pages.
Practitioner: every form, including the Emergent one, pushes costs somewhere; name them.

### E4. Organization by the next form

Evidence for: Ronfeldt's general claim that each form's limitation "takes us to the next form to arise"; temple estates did organize agrarian surplus.
Evidence against: the factory's costs were organized by the Institutional form (inspectors from 1833 and 1863), and the founders of peer production cited proprietary restrictions and cheap computation (Stallman 1983, 1985; Benkler 2006). The ecological framing of the commons postdates the founding.
Grade: rejected for production.
Recommended status: working-hypothesis at most, and only for coordination, where Ronfeldt makes the claim.
Practitioner: argue that the BioHub can choose to organize inherited costs, and that their return into prices gives that work its value; avoid arguing that it inherits them by design.

### E5. Communications

Evidence for: Tribal with speech, Institutional with writing, Emergent with networked information all match Ronfeldt.
Evidence against: the factory preceded the telegraph and rode power and transport; Ronfeldt moves print between institutions and markets across his texts.
Grade: [IC] for the partial match.
Recommended status: documented-framework for Ronfeldt's pairing; academic-inference for the production match.
Practitioner: tie production forms to communications for the first, second and fourth forms; for the Market form, name power and transport first.

### E6. Coexistence

Evidence for: ILO unpaid care (16.4 billion hours a day, 2018). ILO informal employment: more than 2 billion workers and 58.0 percent of the global workforce in 2023 (ILOSTAT, reporting *WESO Trends 2024*), and almost 2.03 billion workers, 57.8 percent, in 2024 (ILO, *WESO* May 2024 Update). Open source value estimates; fab lab counts.
Evidence against: the open source value estimate is contested (Whitacre), fab lab counts come from interested parties, and no global figures were gathered for guild-like production.
Grade: [IC] for Tribal and Market; [MS] and [TBV] for Emergent; [TBV] for Institutional.
Recommended status: documented-fact for household and informal production; academic-inference for the rest.
Practitioner: all four forms run now; cite ILO figures freely and label the peer production figures as estimates.

### E7. Reconciliation

Evidence for: the endpoints match.
Evidence against: the TIME and Coordination Nodes pages assigned different nodes and costs to the same ordinals, and the debt sequence omitted the Institutional form.
Grade: the rival holds: different sequences. [MS] (wiki pages).
Recommended status: structural-inference.
Practitioner: use the coordination nodes for TIME and The Sequence and the Debt for production nodes.

### E8. Glocalization's place

Evidence for: DGML describes globally shared design with local fabrication, which is the shape of the claim.
Evidence against: the glocalization brief found that relocated production remains governed from existing centers; Kostakis and colleagues describe DGML as a proto-model that cannot yet sustain itself independently of capitalism.
Grade: [TBV].
Recommended status: speculative-projection.
Practitioner: describe Emergent-governed place-based production as a goal and a small set of cases; avoid describing it as the present stage.

## Evidence the proposition must face

Teleology is the main weakness. The TIME page disclaims the ladder in its typology and, until this brief, restored it in the debt section. Popper's objection applies to any claim that history must arrive at the BioHub. Gerschenkron's shows that late adopters skip and recombine forms.

Ronfeldt's own framework is about coordination, and the wiki adapts it. His 2025 revision treats Tribes and Networks as "both network forms of organization" and argues that the tribal form is "a forever form." That supports coexistence and weakens a strict sequence.

The guild debate is unresolved, and the brief does not settle it. Where a source is an interested party (Tidelift, the Fab Foundation, the promoters of the Harvard estimate), it is labeled.

What the evidence does support is a different account of where a BioHub's value comes from. Each form's costs have been organized by whoever chose to take them up, and most often that has been the Institutional form putting a price on them: inspectors in the mills from 1833, compulsory condensation of factory acid from 1863, and the European Union's charge on carbon embedded in imports from 1 January 2026 [IC]. As costs the Market form left unpriced come back into prices, the work of retaining water, soil and biological complexity gains value, and so do the communities able to organize it [structural inference]. The Sequence and the Debt sets this out in full.

Searches for opposition. The run looked for evidence that peer production arose in response to factory costs in the GNU announcement, the GNU Manifesto and *The Wealth of Networks*, chapter 1, and found none. It looked for evidence that factory costs were organized by a later production form, and found the state.
