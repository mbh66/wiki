Retention methods measurably cut runoff and sediment, raise soil moisture and recharge groundwater near the structures, yet this review found no study that shows a gauged, controlled rise in catchment dry-season baseflow, and several strong studies show dense retention reducing water downstream.

## Overview

[[concepts/water-retention-landscapes|Water Retention Landscapes]] include pits, bunds, terraces, check dams, johads, sand dams, infiltration canals, leaky weirs and beaver dam analogues. At the scale where they are built, the evidence is large and consistent. A review of sub-Saharan plot studies found that cross-slope structures cut runoff by 13 to 71% and soil loss by 39 to 83% (Wolka, Mulder and Biazin 2018). Terraces and check dams were the main driver of the fall in Yellow River sediment from the 1970s to the 1990s (Wang et al. 2016). Under Arvari johads in Rajasthan, groundwater recharge reached 3 to 7 mm per day in the monsoon (Glendenning and Vervoort 2010).

The evidence thins with scale. A water payer would buy more dry-season river flow, and this run found no gauged, controlled demonstration of it. On total yield the best catchment studies point the other way. In the Arvari, past a threshold, more harvesting area added little recharge and "substantially reduces streamflow" (Glendenning 2009). In Murray-Darling study catchments, farm dams cut mean annual streamflow by a median of 13% (*Journal of Hydrology* 2023). On the Loess Plateau, Feng et al. (2016, *Nature Climate Change*) found that rising evapotranspiration under the Grain to Green program, running since 1999, caused "a significant (p < 0.001) decrease in the ratio of river runoff to annual precipitation". They also found that "NPP in this region is found to be already close to this limit" of 400 ± 5 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹. The strongest dry-season result, Peru's amunas, describes a delay with no added annual volume. Infiltrated water "is retained for an average of 45 d before resurfacing", and upscaling to Lima's source areas "can potentially delay 99 × 10^6 m3 yr−1 of streamflow and increase dry-season flows by 7.5% on average" (Ochoa-Tocachi et al. 2019, *Nature Sustainability*).

The flagship programs carry the weakest independent evidence. Slovakia's 2010 to 2012 Landscape Revitalization Program has no published hydrological evaluation. Its auditor found invoice fraud, and one of its dams failed fatally in 2021. India's Comptroller and Auditor General tabled a report on Maharashtra's Jalyukt Shivar in the state Legislative Assembly in 2020. It found that the scheme, at Rs 9,633.75 crore, "had little impact in achieving water neutrality and increasing groundwater level, and its execution was characterised by lack of transparency" (Outlook India). It also found that storage in 83 of 120 sampled villages did not meet water needs (Republic World). The Mulloon Institute's monitoring paper says catchment results are still to be published. A [[glossary/a-e/biohub|BioHub]] can fund retention on evidence now if it pays for soil moisture, erosion, local wells, sediment and yield, and treats catchment baseflow as a hypothesis to monitor.

## What the syntheses conclude

| Synthesis | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| Wolka, Mulder and Biazin 2018, sub-Saharan Africa | Runoff −13 to −71%, soil loss −39 to −83%; yield effects mixed |
| Joshi et al. 2005, 311 Indian watersheds | Mean benefit-cost ratio 2.14, IRR 22%; best with strong participation and 700 to 1,000 mm rain |
| Joshi et al. 2008, 636 watersheds | Benefit-cost ratio about 2; weak institutions limit gains |
| Glendenning et al. 2012, Indian rainwater harvesting review | Local recharge documented; net watershed effect poorly understood |
| Larsen, Larsen and Lane 2021, beaver review | Site-scale flood attenuation, altered low flows, raised water tables, more evaporation |
| Chen et al. 2025, Loess Plateau check dams | 58,776 dams by 2019; 5.5 billion m³ sediment retained; 34% annual Yellow River sediment cut 2000 to 2012 |
| Frontiers in Water 2021, sand dams | Local water tables rise; one model met 6% of demand and cut downstream flow 1% |
| Tigray ecosystem-based adaptation review | Exclosures and stone bunds most successful; water harvesting schemes limited |
| EU NWRM and Climate-ADAPT | Case factsheets (Lamone, Tamera) give intended functions and modeled storage |

None of the syntheses claims a demonstrated catchment-scale rise in dry-season flow. The Indian meta-analyses measure economic returns and do not isolate hydrology. WOCAT and post-2020 EU evaluations were not reached.

## The distinctions

- **Local versus catchment.** Glendenning measured recharge under seven johads and in 29 wells, then modeled the 476 km² catchment, where streamflow fell past a threshold.
- **Timing versus volume.** Upscaled amunas delay 99 million m³ a year, raising dry-season flow 7.5% and lowering wet-season flow. Makueni sand dams raise evapotranspiration from 84.5% to 89% of rainfall, a volume loss.
- **Scale.** Each table row records scale. No plot figure is multiplied up.
- **Retention versus revegetation.** On the Loess Plateau, engineering dominates sediment reduction before the 1990s and vegetation after (Wang et al. 2016). Most Tigray and Indian evaluations do not isolate structures.
- **Degraded versus functioning land.** The best results come from incised channels, gullied loess and crusted Sahelian soils. New storage on functioning land behaves like Australian farm dams.
- **Local climate.** Treated under P7. No independent measured study was found. See [[research/bioprecipitation|Bioprecipitation]] for the wiki's treatment of the rainfall hypothesis.

## Results by method

### Pits and microcatchments (zaï, demi-lunes)

Zaï raised Burkina Faso yields by 300 to 400 kg/ha in dry years and up to 1,500 kg/ha in wet years (Kaboré and Reij 2004). Half-moons with compost or manure gave 900 to 1,600 kg/ha of sorghum, 20 to 39 times unamended half-moons (Zougmoré et al. 2003). Water capture alone failed on nutrient-poor soils. No groundwater or streamflow measurement was found. Cost: labor; no per-hectare figure found.

### Bunds, trenches and terraces

Stone bunds cut soil loss 68% on 202 Tigray parcels (Gebremichael et al. 2005). Under 120 mm/h simulated rain on the Loess Plateau, maintained terraces cut runoff 100%; ridgeless terraces 28%. Effectiveness declines as trenches fill (Taye et al. 2015, not re-read). Bunds take cropland and can lower early yields.

### Check dams and small dams (johads, stop dams)

Check dams and dam lands cut watershed sediment yield by about 50 to 77% in China, Spain and the United States (*Scientific Data* 2024). Failures: the Arvari streamflow threshold; Jalyukt Shivar, where 38 of 58 audited villages saw no groundwater rise and none of 120 collected a maintenance cess (CAG 2020); sediment rebounds as Jiuyuangou reservoirs silt (Feng et al. 2026); the 2021 Rudno nad Hronom dam breach in Slovakia, which had no safety inspection or completion approval (STVR 2021). Cost: the CAG counted 6.41 lakh works started in 22,586 villages, of which 6.3 lakh (98%) were completed at Rs 9,633.75 crore (Republic World, citing the CAG). That is about Rs 1.53 lakh per completed work. Kravčík reports a €4 per m³ cap [MS].

### Sand dams

Households near Kitui sand dams used about 440 liters a day against 110 elsewhere. The dams stored about 5% of runoff (Lasage et al. 2008). In a 2016 survey, about half of 116 Kitui dams held no water (Springer 2020). In Makueni, 47.6% of reservoir losses evaporated and 3.8% seeped, and baseflow contribution fell (SWAT+, 2026). Lasage et al. (2015) cap Ethiopian catchments at 1,000 and 600 dams to keep flow reduction within 3%.

### Infiltration canals (amunas)

At Huamantanga, Peru, dye tracing showed a mean 45-day retention (Ochoa-Tocachi et al. 2019). Modeled local dry-season flow rose 3 to 554%. Basin upscaling is a scenario (E4) that diverts 35% of wet-season flow. Only three communities maintain amunas.

### Leaky dams and natural flood management

At Pickering, England, leaky dams, planting and an 85,000 m³ bund cut the Boxing Day 2015 peak 15 to 20%, half from land measures (Environment Agency, 2016). The event was 50 mm over 36 hours. Land measures alone were modeled at 4% for a 1-in-25 peak and 8% for a 1-in-100 peak (Defra 2015). The chair warned they would not prevent a 2007-scale flood. See also [[sources/environment-agency-2017-wwnp|Environment Agency (2017), Working with Natural Processes]].

### Natural Sequence Farming

Mulloon Creek has 14 leaky weirs on 2.5 km, gauges above and below, and floodplain piezometers. The institute reports maintained dry-season flow and a higher water table. Peel et al. (2022) call the pilot monitoring "more ad-hoc" and say catchment results await publication. Tony Coote's claims of 60% more carrying capacity, outflow above inflow and about 100 mm a year of dew are [MS]. No independent study of Tarwyn Park was found.

### Beaver dam analogues

Bridge Creek, Oregon, is a highly degraded incised stream, and BDAs were installed on 3.4 km of it (76 in 2009, 121 working by 2012). Bouwes et al. (2016, *Scientific Reports*) reported "significant increases in the density, survival and production of juvenile steelhead without impacting upstream and downstream migrations". Survival rose 52% against a control stream over seven years. The authors call the study "the first large-scale experiment to quantify the benefits of beavers and BDAs to a fish population". Baseflow was not reported. Natural beaver dams were "generally short lived" before posts were installed to anchor them. Ponds raise evaporation (Larsen et al. 2021).

### State programs in Tigray

Abreha we Atsbeha reports a groundwater rise of about 5 m (Gebregziabher et al. 2016), without a control catchment. Nyssen's 30-year photographs use an unchanged church woodland as a reference against rainfall change (E3).

## The predictions

**P1. Soil moisture and infiltration rise at plot scale.** For: runoff −13 to −71% (Wolka 2018); terraces −100% at 120 mm/h; 850 mm percolation in a Tigray gully diversion. Against: effects fade as structures fill. Grade [IC]. Status `documented-fact`. Wiki line: cross-slope structures and pits reduce runoff and erosion on plots, with effects that depend on maintenance.

**P2. Groundwater rises and springs revive locally.** For: 8 of 12 rows with well or spring records report a rise. Against: none used a control catchment; the CAG found no rise in 38 of 58 villages. Grade [IC] locally, [TBV] program-wide. Status `academic-inference`. Wiki line: structures recharge groundwater nearby where geology allows.

**P3. Catchment dry-season baseflow rises.** For: amunas model +7.5%; Mulloon reports [MS]. Against: zero gauged, controlled cases; Makueni and farm dam results show lower low flows. Grade [TBV]. Status `working-hypothesis`. Wiki line: no controlled gauged study demonstrates it.

**P4. Total downstream yield rises.** For: one model finds no yield loss from Loess Plateau revegetation, a vegetation result. Against: Arvari threshold; −13% farm dams; Loess Plateau limit; sand dam −1% and 3% caps. Grade [IC] against. Status `academic-inference`. Wiki line: dense retention reduces downstream yield.

**P5. Floods and sediment fall.** For: check dams −34% sediment; Pickering −15 to −20%; Devon beavers −30%. Against: Pickering land measures −4 to −8% modeled; Jiuyuangou storm rebounds; Robichaud (2008) log barriers work only in small storms. Grade [IC] for sediment and moderate floods, [TBV] for large floods. Status `academic-inference`.

**P6. Effects persist.** For: Tigray bunds aged 3 to 21 years still cut soil loss 68%. Against: half of Rudno dams damaged; fatal breach; half of Kitui dams dry; Jiuyuangou silting. Grade [IC] that persistence depends on maintenance. Status `academic-inference`.

**P7. Cooling and rainfall.** For: Kravčík thermal images and Coote dew claims [MS]. Against: no independent measured study found. Grade [TBV]. Status `working-hypothesis` for cooling, `speculative-projection` for rainfall.

**P8. Community-led outperforms state-led.** For: Joshi links participation to higher returns. Against: community-built Slovak and Kitui structures failed; state-engineered Loess Plateau has the strongest sediment record. Grade [TBV]. Status `working-hypothesis`. Wiki line: maintenance and siting predict outcomes better than who leads.

**P9. Practitioner claims match measurement.** For: amunas tracers; Pickering's "up to 20%". Against: Slovak, Jalyukt, TBS and Mulloon headline figures lack independent confirmation. Grade [IC] that a gap exists. Status `academic-inference`.

## Evidence the claims must face

1. **Rainfall.** Bouwes used a control stream; Nyssen a woodland reference. Mulloon and Slovak claims have no rainfall control.
2. **Upstream-downstream transfer.** Arvari, Murray-Darling interception policy, Ethiopian sand dam caps.
3. **Evaporation.** 47.6% of Makueni reservoir losses; 57% of Kitui depletion; beaver ponds.
4. **Abstraction rebound.** Irrigation expansion lowered Arvari resilience; the river parliament lacked aquifer data to enforce limits.
5. **Failure and silting.** Rudno, Kitui, Jiuyuangou, Tigray trenches.
6. **Survivorship.** No independent gauged study of Hiware Bazar, Ralegan Siddhi, Laporiya or Tarwyn Park was found.
7. **Self-reported totals.** Labeled [MS] in the table.

## What a credible retention monitoring design looks like

Pair the treated catchment with a control (Bridge Creek against Murderers Creek). Collect at least two years of baseline. Gauge above and below the works, run a well or piezometer network (29 wells in the Arvari), keep rain gauges in the catchment, use tracers for residence time (amunas), and log abstraction. Measure evaporation from new storage. Run for ten years or more, through at least one large event, with structure-condition surveys at years 1, 5 and 10. Publish data independently of the implementer, as the Environment Agency did at Pickering. For [[concepts/performance-based-water-bonds|performance-based water bonds]], this supports paying for O1, O2, O6 and O7 now and contracting O3 only with this monitoring.
