Households in mature economies are getting worse off on measures GDP leaves out, which is a claim about lost prosperity: the gauge adds up what is produced and spent, and the losses households carry in housing, wellbeing, natural capital and disaster damage sit outside it.

## Overview

On 18 March 1968, Robert F. Kennedy told about 20,000 people at the University of Kansas that the United States' Gross National Product, then "over \$800 billion dollars a year," counted "air pollution and cigarette advertising, and ambulances to clear our highways of carnage." It counted "special locks for our doors and the jails for the people who break them," and "the destruction of the redwood and the loss of our natural wonder in chaotic sprawl." Yet it did "not allow for the health of our children, the quality of their education or the joy of their play." It measured, he said, "everything in short, except that which makes life worthwhile." [IC]

Fifty-eight years later the instrument is called GDP and is built the same way. It sums output and spending within a period. A house destroyed by a flood or a fire is never subtracted from it, and the rebuilding is added to it. The household that lost the house, its savings and some years of income is recorded nowhere in the headline figure. The older meaning of [[economy|economy]] was the management of a household. The modern gauge tracks the flow through the household's accounts and leaves out what the household holds.

This brief tests the case in eight mature economies on four continents. Real GDP per person rose in all eight between 2008 and 2025. Over comparable years, life evaluations fell in five of them and house prices rose faster than incomes in six. Across the world, natural capital per person fell while output grew, and the cost of disasters fell mostly on households and governments outside insurance. The evidence is strongest in the four English-speaking economies, which worsened on both household measures in the table. It is weakest in Germany, Italy and Japan, where life evaluations rose.

The brief is the short companion to a longer evidence run, *The Gauge and the Household* (22 October 2026), which graded the full case and its counterevidence. It examines a measurement instrument and the steering that depends on it. It treats the Economy and the BioConomy as parallel systems and makes no case against markets.

## Where this brief sits in the S-curve argument

[[research/growth-economics|The S-Curve Thesis and the Retention Counter-Tradition]] argues that [[throughput-economics|throughput economics]], which measures value by what moves, fit the acceleration phase of the industrial [[s-curve|S-curve]] and loses its fit as the curve decelerates, while [[concepts/retention-logic|retention logic]], which measures value by what stays, becomes structurally necessary. GDP is the instrument of throughput economics: it counts flow and has no line for what stays. This brief supplies household evidence for that argument. Two of the S-curve brief's deceleration indicators carry straight into it: the wage-productivity gap, which that brief reads as growth's gains no longer reaching households, and debt saturation, which it reads as growth drawn from future claims (US\$318 trillion in 2024 there, US\$365.5 trillion by mid-2026 here). The national table fits the same frame. The gap between gauge and household shows in mature economies, and the longer evidence run found China, India and Poland, still on the steep part of their curves, gaining on household measures alongside GDP. A gauge built to count flow tracks the acceleration phase closely. Late in the curve a growing share of measured flow comes from drawdown, repair and borrowing, and the gauge books that share as growth. The S-curve mapping remains a structural inference: this brief tests one observable consequence of it, and its findings are consistent with the mapping without proving it.

## How the gauge books a loss as a gain

Simon Kuznets, who built the first US national income accounts, told the Senate in 1934 that the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measure of national income. The Stiglitz, Sen and Fitoussi commission (2009), the Dasgupta Review (2021) and the UN High-Level Expert Group on Beyond GDP (*Counting What Counts*, 7 May 2026) restated the point. The expert group proposed a 31-indicator dashboard and states that it "does not ask the world to abandon GDP." [IC] The fiscal rules governments are held to remain written in GDP: the EU's deficit and debt reference values are 3 and 60 percent of GDP, and SDG target 8.1 asks the least developed countries for at least 7 percent annual GDP growth. [IC] Four features of the gauge matter here.

1. **Destroyed assets sit outside GDP.** The US Bureau of Economic Analysis states that GDP "is not directly affected by the loss of property (structures and equipment) produced in previous periods," and that losses of fixed assets from disasters do not enter consumption of fixed capital either. Repair and replacement count as new production. [IC]
2. **Drawdown is booked as income.** The World Bank's *Changing Wealth of Nations 2024* finds real wealth per person up 21 percent from 1995 to 2020 while renewable natural capital per person fell more than 20 percent. [IC] The Dasgupta Review reports that from 1992 to 2014 produced capital per head roughly doubled and natural capital per head fell by nearly 40 percent. [IC] The atmospheric and ecological base is drawn down at the same time: the Potsdam Institute's *Planetary Health Check 2025* assesses seven of nine [[planetary-boundaries|planetary boundaries]] as breached, one more than in 2023 (see [[sources/richardson-planetary-boundaries|Richardson et al. 2023]]). [IC]
3. **Defensive spending is counted as output.** Kennedy's ambulances and locks are the founding examples. Christian Leipert's work on defensive expenditures, and Daly and Cobb's *Index of Sustainable Economic Welfare*, subtract them; the official accounts do not. No current estimate of their share of GDP growth was found. [TBV]
4. **Borrowing is counted as production.** The Institute of International Finance (IIF) puts global debt at a record US\$365.5 trillion in the first half of 2026, roughly three times world output, against about 2.4 times in 2005. Advanced economies paid over US\$3.3 trillion in interest on internationally traded government bonds over the year to September 2026. [IC]

## Disasters: the clearest case

Disaster recovery shows the gap between the gauge and the household most plainly, because the national accounts record the rebuilding and leave out the destruction.

- **Scale.** Munich Re put 2025 natural-disaster losses at about US\$224 billion, of which about US\$108 billion was insured. Losses averaged US\$266 billion a year over the past decade, with around 60 percent uninsured. The Los Angeles wildfires of January 2025 cost about US\$53 billion and killed 30 people. [IC] Uninsured losses fall on households, firms and governments.
- **Output does not bounce back.** Hsiang and Jina's study of 6,700 cyclones (NBER Working Paper 20352, 2014; not peer-reviewed in this form) finds that national incomes fall below their pre-disaster trend and do not recover within twenty years. A 90th-percentile cyclone lowers income per person by 7.4 percent two decades later, about 3.7 years of average development. The authors reject the hypothesis that disasters stimulate growth. [IC]
- **Wellbeing losses exceed asset losses.** The World Bank's *Unbreakable* study of 117 countries (Hallegatte et al., 2017) estimates that extreme disasters cost the equivalent of US\$520 billion a year in lost consumption, more than the asset losses usually reported, and push about 26 million people into poverty each year. [IC]
- **Recovery redistributes wealth.** Howell and Elliott (2019) followed about 3,500 US families from 1999 to 2013. In counties with heavy disaster damage, homeowners gained about US\$72,000 in wealth relative to low-damage counties and renters lost about US\$61,000. Counties that received more federal disaster aid saw wealth gaps widen. [IC]

Taken together: the reconstruction appears in GDP, the destroyed stock does not, and the households least able to rebuild end the decade with less. [IC]

What the table shows:

- **GDP rose everywhere over the long window.** Since 2019 it was close to flat in Germany, Canada and the United Kingdom. [IC]
- **Life evaluations fell in five of eight** while GDP per person rose in each of the five. The largest falls were in Canada and Australia. Among people under 25, life evaluations since 2006 to 2010 fell by 1.14 points in Canada and 0.96 points in the United States, and by 0.86 points on average across the United States, Canada, Australia and New Zealand (*World Happiness Report 2026*). [IC]
- **Housing became less affordable in six of eight,** most sharply in Canada, the United States and Australia. [IC]
- **The English-speaking economies worsened on both measures.** France worsened on life evaluation and improved on housing. Germany, Italy and Japan improved on life evaluation. [IC]
- **Trust sits low across the OECD.** In the OECD's 2023 trust survey of 30 countries, 39.3 percent of people had high or moderately high trust in their national government; the United Kingdom stood at 26.7 percent and Canada at 48.5 percent. [IC]

## The essay's objection, and the answer

The prompt for this brief was Robert W. Malone and Jill Glasspool Malone's essay "Paris Is Burning, and Brussels Is Still Writing Regulations" ([Malone News, 21 October 2026](https://www.malone.news/p/paris-is-burning-and-brussels-is)). It warns that "declining prosperity should not be confused with environmental progress," and that a smaller economy and a lower standard of living are no achievement simply because emissions fall. The brief agrees, and its evidence is built to separate the two positions. Every reading used here is a household outcome: whether a family can afford a home, how people rate their lives, whether the soil and water they depend on are intact, and whether a household recovers after a flood. A fall in consumption caused by poverty registers as a loss on each of them. The claim is that households have lost prosperity on these measures while the gauge recorded growth, and that the gauge counts the drawdown of natural capital, the repair of damage, and borrowing against future income as wealth created. Both positions hold that wealth must be created before it can be redistributed. The essay's authors have not been invited to respond to this reading.

## Where the case is weaker

- **Health improved in the United States.** Life expectancy reached a record 79.0 years in 2024, and overdose deaths fell from 105,007 in 2023 to 79,384 in 2024 (CDC). [IC]
- **Three of eight economies improved on life evaluation.** Germany, Italy, and Japan rose, two of them with slow GDP growth. [IC]
- **Household income recovered.** The OECD's *How's Life? 2024* found that average real disposable household income had not fallen significantly below its pre-pandemic level in any OECD country by 2022. [IC]
- **Distribution explains part of the gap.** The Economic Policy Institute attributes the US gap between productivity (up 90.2 percent, 1979 to 2025) and typical pay (up 33.0 percent) to rising inequality. Part of the household shortfall is an accurate gauge with badly shared gains. [IC]
- **Other causes compete.** The *World Happiness Report 2026* associates part of the youth decline in English-speaking countries with heavy social media use and says its data cannot establish causation. [IC]
- **Europe's gauge stalled too.** Since 2019, GDP per person barely grew in Germany, the United Kingdom, and France, so there the gap runs between a flat gauge and worse household readings. [IC]
- **Natural capital per person partly reflects population growth.** A 2026 preprint using the World Bank data argues that the aggregate stock of renewable natural capital stayed roughly constant. It is not peer-reviewed. [TBV]
